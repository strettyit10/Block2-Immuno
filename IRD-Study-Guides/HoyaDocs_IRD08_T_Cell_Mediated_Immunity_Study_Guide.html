Learning objectives

  2. LO 2Name the three professional antigen-presenting cells, and contrast what conventional dendritic cells and plasmacytoid dendritic cells each do.
  3. LO 3Explain the signals a naive T cell must receive to switch on and become an effector cell, and the brakes that keep that response in check.
  4. LO 4Explain how activated T cells split into effector types (CD4 Th1, Th2, Th17, Tfh and induced Treg; CD8 cytotoxic T cells) and what job each one does in cell-mediated immunity.
  5. LO 5Walk through T-cell development and selection in the thymus, and compare tolerance built centrally in the thymus with tolerance enforced in the periphery.

By the end: you can trace a naive T cell from blood into a lymph node T-cell zone, name its two survival signals, explain why γc or JAK3 loss gives T⁻ NK⁻ B⁺ SCID, and describe how an activated T cell is held in the node and later released.
Q1. What do T cells add to an immune response, and why must innate and adaptive immunity cooperate?
  • Innate cells (especially dendritic cells) sense microbes (PAMPs/DAMPs) → supply the "danger signal" that launches adaptive immunity.
  • T cells contribute four properties:
    • Specificity → each TCR recognizes one peptide:MHC
    • Effector function → cytokines (helpers) or cytotoxic killing (CTLs)
    • Trafficking → travel to infected/inflamed sites
    • Memory → faster, larger response on re-exposure
  • Antigen is carried from tissue to the lymph node by DCs → naive CD4 and CD8 T cells are primed there, not in the tissue.
CheckWhich of the four T-cell properties depends on homing receptors?
Trafficking. Homing receptors (L-selectin, CCR7 on naive cells; VLA-4, PSGL-1 on effectors) decide where the cell goes.

Q4. If a naive T cell never meets its antigen, how does it stay alive?
  • Two maintenance signals, received while passing through SLOs:
    1. Self peptide:MHC → weak, "tonic" TCR signaling
    2. IL-7 → via the IL-7 receptor (IL-7Rα + γc)
  • IL-7 is made by stromal cells: FRCs/fibroblasts and epithelial cells (not by DCs or T cells).
  • IL-7 is the main cytokine for naive T-cell survival and for T-cell development in the thymus.
  • Then the cell returns to the circulation (efferent lymph → thoracic duct → blood) and moves on to the next SLO. Without these signals, naive T cells die.
CheckWhich cell type secretes the IL-7 that keeps naive T cells alive in a lymph node?
Stromal cells (fibroblast reticular cells, fibroblasts, epithelial cells), not dendritic cells.
Q5. Why does losing one shared receptor chain (γc) or one kinase (JAK3) cause SCID?
  • The common γ chain (γc, CD132) is a shared signaling subunit of the receptors for IL-2, IL-4, IL-7, IL-9, IL-15 and IL-21. Each has its own private α chain but relies on γc for signaling.
  • Every γc receptor signals through JAK1 (private chain) + JAK3 (γc) → STATs.

The opening case

  • 13-month-old boy: recurrent respiratory infections from 7 months, refractory progressive pneumonia, nodular skin lesions; a maternal uncle died in infancy (→ X-linked).
  • Flow cytometry: few CD3⁺ T cells, normal CD19⁺ B cells, absent CD132; mother normal.
  • Reduced signaling from IL-7 best explains the low T cells (IL-15 explains absent NK cells; IL-21 affects B/plasma cells; IL-1 and IL-6 do not use γc).
CheckAn infant has a loss-of-function mutation in IL-7Rα only. Predict T, B and NK numbers.
T⁻ B⁺ NK⁺. IL-7 signaling is lost, but IL-15 (IL-15Rα + IL-2Rβ + γc) still works, so NK cells develop. A private-chain defect hits one cytokine; a γc/JAK3 defect hits all six.
Step 1 HY: SCID
  • X-linked SCID (IL2RG, γc) = most common SCID. JAK3 SCID = same phenotype but autosomal recessive (girls too).
  • Clues: failure to thrive, chronic diarrhea, thrush, recurrent viral/fungal/opportunistic infections, absent thymic shadow, ↓ TRECs on newborn screen.
  • Rx: HSC transplant; no live vaccines. JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, baricitinib) exploit this pathway in RA.
Step 1 trap

"B cells present" ≠ "antibody normal." In γc SCID, B cells are there but cannot class switch or form germinal centers without T-cell help (and IL-21). Also: the question asks about low T cells → IL-7, not IL-15 (that's the NK answer).

Memory tool

γc cytokines = "2, 4, 7, 9, 15, 21" → "Two For Seven Nine, Fifteen Twenty-one." 7 → T cells (lucky Seven makes T), 15 → NK.

Q6. What happens when a naive T cell does find its antigen, and how does it leave?
  • It recognizes its antigen on the surface of a mature DC → loses the ability to exit → stays several days to proliferate and differentiate into effector cells.
  • CD69 (up within hours) binds and internalizes S1PR1 (sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor) → the cell can't follow the S1P gradient out → retained.
  • Once differentiated, effector T cells regain exit receptors → leave via efferent lymphatics → blood in greatly increased numbers → home to inflamed tissue (LO 3).
  • Activation happens in SLOs, not in peripheral tissues.
CheckWhich molecule keeps a freshly activated T cell in the node, and which receptor must come back for it to leave?
CD69 holds it; re-expression of S1PR1 lets it exit.
Step 1 HY

Fingolimod (MS) = S1P-receptor modulator → lymphocytes trapped in lymph nodes → ↓ circulating lymphocytes, fewer reach the CNS. Side effects: first-dose bradycardia, macular edema, infections.

Buzzwords
"absent CD132" → X-linked SCID"absent thymic shadow, ↓ TRECs" → SCID"maternal uncle died in infancy" → X-linked"S1P receptor" → lymphocyte egress; fingolimod

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A 6-month-old girl has thrush, Pneumocystis pneumonia and no palpable lymph nodes. CD3⁺ and CD56⁺ cells are nearly absent; CD19⁺ cells are normal; CD132 staining is normal. Most likely defect?
JAK3 deficiency (autosomal recessive). Same T⁻ NK⁻ B⁺ phenotype as γc SCID, but a girl with normal CD132 points to the kinase downstream of γc.
A2A drug blocks IL-7 receptor signaling in an adult. Predict the effect on naive T cells over months.
Gradual loss of naive T cells. They depend on IL-7 (plus self-pMHC) for maintenance survival while recirculating.
A3A patient on fingolimod has a low blood lymphocyte count but no increase in infections from lymphocyte destruction. Where are the cells?
Sequestered in lymph nodes. Blocking S1PR1 signaling prevents egress (mimicking CD69-mediated retention); cells are not killed.
A4Why does activation of naive T cells require them to travel to a lymph node instead of meeting the virus in the lung?
Because mature, antigen-loaded DCs with co-stimulation meet naive T cells in the T-cell zone. Naive T cells (CCR7⁺, L-selectin⁺) home to SLOs, not tissues; the node concentrates antigen and rare specific T cells together.

By the end: you can name the three professional APCs, say why only DCs prime naive T cells, match each antigen-uptake route to MHC I or II, and contrast cDC1, cDC2 and pDC.
Q1. What do T cells actually "see," and which MHC goes with which T cell?
  • Conventional αβ T cells recognize antigen only as peptide:MHC complexes on cell surfaces.
    • CD4 T cells → peptide:MHC class II (extracellular/endocytosed antigen)
    • CD8 T cells → peptide:MHC class I (cytosolic/intracellular antigen)
  • MHC I is on all nucleated cells; MHC II is on professional APCs.
CheckA red blood cell infected with a parasite cannot be killed by CTLs. Why?
RBCs are not nucleated → no MHC I → nothing for CD8 TCRs to recognize.
Memory tool

Rule of 8: CD4 × MHC II = 8; CD8 × MHC I = 8.

Q2. Who are the three professional APCs, and why is only one of them able to prime naive T cells?
  • Dendritic cells, macrophages and B cells all express MHC class II and can process and present antigen to CD4 T cells. DCs bridge innate and adaptive immunity.

Dendritic cell+++ macropinocytosis + phagocytosis (tissue DCs); viral infectionConstitutive on mature DCs (++++)Activate naive T cells
Macrophage+++ macropinocytosis + phagocytosisInducible (− to +++)Receive help from effector/memory T cells → macrophage activation (IFN-γ)

  • DCs win because mature DCs have high MHC + high B7 together, and they migrate (CCR7) to the T-cell zone carrying antigen from tissue.
  • Macrophages and B cells present on MHC II mainly for their own benefit — to recruit help from T cells that are already activated.
CheckA B cell's BCR binds viral capsid, it internalizes it and shows capsid peptides on MHC II. Which T cell does it talk to, and what does it gain?
An already-activated Tfh cell specific for the same antigen (linked recognition) → CD40L + IL-21 → class switching, affinity maturation, plasma cells.
Step 1 trap

"Which APC activates naive T cells?" → dendritic cell only. Macrophages and B cells do present on MHC II, but to effector/memory/Tfh cells to get help. Don't confuse follicular DCs (stromal, hold intact antigen for B cells, no MHC II presentation) with conventional DCs.

CheckA virus infects only respiratory epithelium, never DCs. How are naive virus-specific CD8 cells primed?

Step 1 trap

The default rule is "outside → MHC II, inside → MHC I." Cross-presentation (outside → MHC I) and autophagy (inside → MHC II) are the two named exceptions. If a question emphasizes "virus does not infect DCs" yet CD8 cells are primed, the answer is cross-presentation.

Step 1 HY
  • TAP deficiency (bare lymphocyte syndrome type I) → ↓ MHC I surface → ↓ CD8 cells, recurrent respiratory infections.
  • MHC II deficiency (type II, e.g., CIITA) → ↓ CD4 cells.

Memory tool

cDC1 → CD8 (1 for class I), cDC2 → CD4 (2 for class II). pDC = "Pumps Da Cytokine" (interferon) but barely presents.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A Th1 effector recognizes mycobacterial peptide on MHC II of an infected macrophage. What does the macrophage get out of this?
Help. The Th1 cell releases IFN-γ (and CD40L) → macrophage activation → kills intracellular mycobacteria. Macrophages do not prime naive T cells.
A2A mouse lacking cDC1 cells is infected with a virus that only infects hepatocytes. Which response is most impaired?
Priming of naive virus-specific CD8 CTLs, because cross-presentation on MHC I is lost.
A3A drug blocks endosomal TLR7 and TLR9 signaling. Which DC function falls most?
Type I IFN release by plasmacytoid DCs (the basis of hydroxychloroquine's partial benefit in lupus).
A4A lymph node DC presents a viral peptide on both MHC I and MHC II, yet the virus never infected it. How did it get the antigen?
Transfer from an incoming migratory DC to a lymph-node-resident DC, which presents on MHC I and II → primes both CD8 and CD4 cells.

Step 1 HY: hyper-IgM

Most often X-linked CD40L deficiency: ↑/normal IgM, ↓ IgG/IgA/IgE, no germinal centers; recurrent sinopulmonary infections, Pneumocystis, Cryptosporidium, CMV (weak macrophage activation too).

Step 1 trap

CD40 = APC (B cell, DC, macrophage); CD40L = activated T cell. Answer choices love to flip these. And B7 is on the APC, CD28 on the T cell.

Memory tool

"Who, Yes, What": Signal 1 = who (antigen), signal 2 = yes, really (danger confirmed), signal 3 = what to become (cytokines). B7 × 4 = 28 → B7 binds CD28.

Q4. How does the TCR signal reach the nucleus, and what happens next on the clock?
  • Naive T cell's key surface kit: TCR (αβ, unique), CD3 (signaling complex with ITAMs), CD4 or CD8 co-receptor (carries the kinase Lck), CD28.
  • The TCR itself has no signaling domain → Lck (bound to CD4/CD8) phosphorylates the CD3 ITAMs → cascade → NFAT, AP-1, NF-κB (+ Akt–mTOR with CD28).
  • CD3 = pan-T-cell marker (the "CD3⁺" count in flow cytometry).

~6–12 hPeak IL-2; expression of CD69 and IL-2Rα (CD25)
~2–5 daysClonal expansion: divide every 4–6 h → 1,000–10,000-fold; CD8 expand more than CD4
CheckWhich enzyme actually phosphorylates the CD3 ITAMs?
Lck, the kinase associated with the CD4/CD8 co-receptor (not CD4/CD8 themselves).

Q5. What does IL-2 do, and why do CD8 cells need CD4 help?
  • IL-2 receptor: naive T = IL-2Rβ + γc (intermediate affinity). After activation, IL-2Rα (CD25) is added → high-affinity trimer → only antigen-activated T cells respond strongly.
  • Autocrine: the activated T cell makes IL-2 and responds via its own high-affinity receptor → its own proliferation.
  • Paracrine: IL-2 from one T cell binds the high-affinity IL-2R of a different T cell (e.g., CD4 → nearby CD8).
  • IL-2 is essential for Treg maintenance but not required for clonal expansion of some non-Treg cells. Treg dysfunction is common in autoimmune disease → low-dose IL-2 is being investigated as treatment.

CheckCTLA-4-knockout mice die within weeks of massive lymphoproliferation. Why?

Step 1 HY: checkpoint drugs
  • Ipilimumab = anti-CTLA-4. Nivolumab, pembrolizumab = anti-PD-1. Atezolizumab, durvalumab = anti-PD-L1.
  • Toxicity = immune-related adverse events: colitis, hepatitis, thyroiditis, hypophysitis, pneumonitis, dermatitis.
  • Opposite direction: abatacept (CTLA-4-Ig) dampens RA.
Step 1 trap

CTLA-4 is an inhibitory receptor on the T cell that binds the same ligand as CD28 (B7). Abatacept is CTLA-4 fused to Ig → it blocks B7 (immunosuppressive), whereas ipilimumab blocks CTLA-4 (immunostimulatory).

Memory tool

CTLA-4 = "Catch The Lymph-node Activation" (early, priming). PD-1 = "Peripheral Dampener" (later, in tissues).

Q4. Which cells do CD8 cytotoxic T cells kill, and how?
  • CTLs kill cells infected with intracellular pathogens (viruses, some intracellular bacteria) and tumor cells, recognized by foreign peptide on MHC I (on every nucleated cell).
  • Perforin forms pores in the target membrane → granzymes (serine proteases) enter → activate caspases → apoptosis.
  • Polarized secretion at the synapse confines killing to the target and spares bystanders.
  • Apoptosis avoids spilling intact pathogens and inflammatory contents.
  • The CTL survives and moves on to serial killing.
CheckA CTL sticks to an uninfected hepatocyte via LFA-1:ICAM-1 but its TCR finds no match. What happens?
No granule release; it releases LFA-1 and moves on. Adhesion is nonspecific; killing is antigen-specific.
Step 1 HY: CTL vs NK
  • CTLs also kill via Fas ligand → Fas.
  • NK cells use the same perforin/granzyme machinery but kill cells lacking MHC I ("missing self"); inhibitory signals from self-MHC I usually dominate. Key when viruses downregulate MHC I to dodge CTLs.
  • NK activity ↑ by IL-2, IL-12, IFN-α/β; NK development needs IL-15.
  • CTLs also mediate acute cellular graft rejection.
Step 1 trap

Perforin makes the hole; granzymes trigger apoptosis. The target dies by apoptosis, not osmotic lysis/necrosis — "complement-like lysis" is the distractor.

Buzzwords
"MTOC reorients" → CTL polarized secretion"missing self" → NK cell"perforin + granzyme B" → CTL or NK
Q5. How does the response end, and what is left behind?
  • Contraction: once infection is controlled, CTLA-4, PD-1 and Tregs (IL-10, TGF-β) limit activation and most effector cells die.
  • Memory: long-lived memory T cells persist → faster, larger response on re-exposure. CD4 help (IL-2, licensing) during priming improves CD8 memory.
  • Example of Treg value: enterocytes are very vulnerable to Th1 cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF) → gut Tregs making IL-10 and TGF-β are preferentially stimulated and inhibit Th1 activation.
Step 1 HY

Central memory T cells keep CCR7/L-selectin and recirculate through nodes; effector memory T cells lack them and patrol tissues.

Memory tool

"Cortex Can, Medulla Must-not." The cortex asks "can you bind self-MHC at all?" (positive); the medulla insists "you must not bind self too hard" (negative). Thymocytes travel outside → in, so the positive test always comes first.

Q2. What exactly happens in positive and negative selection?

Positive selection (cortex)

  • DP thymocytes meet self-peptide:self-MHC on cortical epithelial cells.
  • Moderate affinity → survival signal. No binding → death by neglect.
  • Ensures MHC restriction.
  • CD4 vs CD8 choice: TCR engages MHC I → CD8; MHC II → CD4 (other co-receptor turned off).

Negative selection (medulla)

  • Thymocytes meet self-peptide:MHC on medullary epithelial cells and DCs.
  • Too-strong binding → apoptosis (clonal deletion); some → natural (thymic) Tregs or receptor editing.
  • AIRE (a transcription factor in mTECs) switches on tissue-restricted antigens (e.g., insulin) so cells reactive to peripheral self can be deleted.
  • Autophagy feeds self peptides onto MHC II here.
CheckThree DP thymocytes: (A) binds no self-MHC; (B) binds self-pMHC I moderately; (C) binds self-pMHC II very strongly in the medulla. Fates?
(A) Death by neglect. (B) Positively selected → CD8 single-positive. (C) Clonal deletion by apoptosis, or diverted to a natural Treg.
Step 1 HY: AIRE

AIRE deficiency → APECED / APS-1 (autosomal recessive): Chandidiasis (chronic mucocutaneous), hypoParathyroidism, Adrenal insufficiency → "CHAR" / candidiasis + endocrine failure. Candidiasis comes from autoantibodies against IL-17/IL-22.

Step 1 trap

AIRE does not kill thymocytes. It is a transcription factor that makes mTECs express peripheral self-antigens; the autoreactive thymocytes then die by apoptosis.
