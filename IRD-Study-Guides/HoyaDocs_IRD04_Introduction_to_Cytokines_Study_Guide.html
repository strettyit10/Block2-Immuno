LO 1What cytokines do in immunity and inflammationThe messengers that start, steer and stop a response

By the end: you can define a cytokine, name the five big families with an example of each, use the words pleiotropy / redundancy / synergy / antagonism correctly, explain how the inflammatory triad produces fever and acute-phase proteins, and describe how a response is turned off.
Q1. What is a cytokine, and who makes them?
  • Cytokine = a small, soluble protein a cell secretes to communicate.
    • It works only by binding a specific receptor on the target cell → triggers a response in that cell.
    • Typical responses: activation, proliferation, differentiation of immune cells; some also cause fever.
  • Who makes them? Both immune and non-immune cells.
    • A virally infected epithelial cell secretes type I IFN (IFN-α/β) to warn its neighbors.
    • Epithelial cells also release TNF and the "alarmins" IL-25, IL-33, TSLP.
    • Bone marrow stromal cells make IL-7 and the CSFs.
  • When? A few are secreted constantly at a low rate; most are made on demand in response to infection, and secretion is transient.
  • Direction: they can be pro-inflammatory (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) or anti-inflammatory (IL-10, TGF-β).
CheckTrue or false: only leukocytes can secrete cytokines.
False. Any cell can. Classic example: a virus-infected (non-immune) cell secretes IFN-α/β before any immune cell arrives.

Q3. How do cytokines interact with each other and with many cells at once?

Pleiotropy

One cytokine → many cell types, different effects. (TNF-α acts on endothelium, hypothalamus, liver, leukocytes.)

Redundancy

Different cytokines → overlapping effects. (IL-4 and IL-13 both push IgE and share IL-4Rα.)

Synergy

Combined effect > sum. (IL-1β + TNF-α together amplify inflammation and leukocyte recruitment.)

Antagonism

One cytokine blocks another: IFN-γ (Th1) suppresses IL-4–driven Th2 differentiation → keeps the response "polarized" to the right program (and IL-4/IL-10 dampen Th1).

Combination = specific response

The body mixes cytokines like a recipe: e.g., TGF-β alone → Treg, but TGF-β + IL-6 (+ IL-23) → Th17.

  • Why therapeutic cytokines cause side effects: they are pleiotropic. Given systemically, a cytokine hits every cell bearing its receptor, not just the intended target → fever, malaise, hypotension, capillary leak.
CheckA patient given recombinant IFN-α for a tumor develops fever, fatigue and low mood. Which cytokine property best explains this?
Pleiotropy: IFN-α acts on many tissues (including brain), so systemic dosing produces effects far from the tumor.
Step 1 HY — therapeutic cytokines
  • Aldesleukin (high-dose IL-2) → metastatic RCC, melanoma → classic capillary leak syndrome.
  • IFN-α → chronic hepatitis B/C, hairy cell leukemia, Kaposi sarcoma; IFN-β → multiple sclerosis; IFN-γ → chronic granulomatous disease.
  • IFN side effects: flu-like symptoms, depression, neutropenia.
Step 1 trap

Pleiotropy ≠ redundancy. Pleiotropy = one cytokine, many targets. Redundancy = many cytokines, same effect. A question about "side effects in many organs" wants pleiotropy.

Q4. What is the inflammatory triad, and how does it cause fever and acute-phase proteins?
  • Triad = IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, made mainly by macrophages and dendritic cells (IL-6 also by B and T cells).
  • Locally → act on endothelium (↑ adhesion molecules, ↑ permeability) → leukocyte migration; IL-1β + TNF-α recruit cells and synergize.
  • At high levels → endocrine (hormone-like): enter blood → distant organs:
    • Liver → acute-phase reactants: C-reactive protein (CRP) and mannose-binding lectin (MBL) → bind microbes → complement + opsonization.
    • Brain (hypothalamus) → ↑ temperature set point → fever.
    • Clinically: fever, anorexia, malaise, leukocytosis (↑ WBC).
  • Too much → cytokine storm / septic shock: TNF-α → systemic vasodilation + ↑ vascular permeability → hypotension.
CheckWhich single cytokine is the classic "endogenous pyrogen," and which is the main driver of hepatic CRP?
IL-1β causes fever (all three contribute); IL-6 is the main driver of hepatic acute-phase proteins.
Step 1 HY — acute phase + sepsis
  • LPS (lipid A) → TLR4 on macrophages → TNF-α + IL-1 → septic shock.
  • Acute-phase ↑: CRP, ferritin, fibrinogen, hepcidin, serum amyloid A. ↓: albumin, transferrin.
  • IL-6 → hepcidin → anemia of chronic disease. TNF-α → cachexia.
Memory tool

"Hot T-bone stEAK": IL-1 = hot (fever) · IL-2 = T-cell growth · IL-3 = bone marrow · IL-4 = IgE · IL-5 = IgA · IL-6 = aKute-phase proteins.

Buzzwords
"endogenous pyrogen" → IL-1β"CRP from the liver" → IL-6"hypotension in septic shock" → TNF-α"cachexia" → TNF-α

  • Built-in safety: cytokine secretion is transient; as pathogen load falls, IL-1, TNF, type I IFN and their receptors decline.

Step 1 HY

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A 70-year-old with E. coli urosepsis has BP 78/40, warm flushed skin and fever. Which cytokine most directly explains the low blood pressure?
TNF-α. It activates endothelium → systemic vasodilation + ↑ vascular permeability → distributive (septic) shock.
A2Predict: a mouse lacking IL-10 and TGF-β signaling is infected and clears the microbe. What happens to its inflammation afterward?
Inflammation fails to resolve (chronic inflammation/autoimmunity). The late "brake" cytokines that ↓ costimulation and induce Tregs are missing.
A3A patient's CRP climbs within a day of a pneumonia. Trace the path from lung to lab value.
Alveolar macrophages → IL-6 (with IL-1β, TNF-α) → blood (endocrine) → hepatocytes → CRP.
A4IFN-γ is added to a culture of naive CD4⁺ T cells also receiving IL-4. What happens to Th2 differentiation, and what is this interaction called?
Th2 differentiation is suppressed — antagonism, enforcing Th1 polarization.

LO 2Autocrine, paracrine and endocrine signalingHow far a cytokine travels to deliver its message

By the end: you can define the three signaling modes, give the IL-2 and inflammatory-triad examples, and explain why most cytokine signaling stays local.
Q1. What do autocrine, paracrine and endocrine mean?
ModeDefinitionClassic example
AutocrineThe same cell secretes and responds.IL-2 from an activated CD4⁺ T helper → its own proliferation (clonal expansion).
ParacrineActs on a nearby cell; short range, low concentration.IL-2 from CD4⁺ T helpers → neighboring CD8⁺ T cells proliferate (CD8⁺ cells make little IL-2 themselves).
EndocrineEnters the bloodstream → acts at a distant organ.IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α from macrophages/DCs → liver (CRP, MBL) + brain (fever).
  • Cytokines work mainly autocrine + paracrine; endocrine to a lesser extent — when levels get high (acute inflammation, sepsis).
  • Same molecule, two modes: IL-2 is both autocrine and paracrine.
CheckWhich mode is it when IL-2 secreted by a helper T cell makes that same cell divide?
Autocrine. If it made a neighboring CD8⁺ T cell divide, that would be paracrine.
Q2. Why is most cytokine signaling local and low-level?
  • Specificity. Low-level, short-range (paracrine) signaling means only antigen-activated neighbors — the cells that have upregulated the receptor — respond.
  • Safety. Systemic spread at high concentration → fever, malaise, leukocytosis, and in excess → cytokine storm / septic shock.
  • Timing. IL-2 is secreted in a brief burst (~8 h after activation) and drives clonal expansion via JAK–STAT.

CheckWhy do bystander T cells that have not seen antigen ignore IL-2 floating nearby?
They have not yet expressed the high-affinity IL-2 receptor (CD25 chain), which appears only after activation.
Step 1 HY
  • High-affinity IL-2R α chain = CD25; constitutively high on Tregs (CD4⁺CD25⁺FOXP3⁺).
  • Basiliximab = anti-CD25 (transplant induction). Cyclosporine/tacrolimus block calcineurin → ↓ IL-2 transcription.
Step 1 trap

"Hormone-like" cytokine effects (fever, CRP) are endocrine, even though cytokines are not classic hormones. A question asking how a lung infection raises CRP wants endocrine, not paracrine.

Memory tool

Auto = self (autograph). Para = beside (paralegal next to the lawyer). Endo = into the blood (endocrine glands).

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A child with influenza has a fever of 39.5 °C. Which signaling mode carried the message from the infected airway to the hypothalamus?
Endocrine — IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α entered the circulation and acted on a distant organ.
A2A CD8⁺ T cell sitting beside an activated CD4⁺ helper begins dividing, though it makes little IL-2. Name the mode.
Paracrine — helper-derived IL-2 acts on the neighbor.
A3A drug blocks IL-2 transcription in T cells. Which two signaling routes for IL-2 are lost, and what is the clinical use?
Both autocrine and paracrine IL-2 signaling. Calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine, tacrolimus) are used to prevent transplant rejection.

Memory tool

Q2. How does JAK–STAT signaling work?
  1. Cytokine binds → receptor chains come together.
  2. Receptor-associated JAKs (Janus kinases) phosphorylate each other and the receptor on tyrosines.
  3. STATs dock on the phospho-tyrosines → get tyrosine-phosphorylated.
  4. Phospho-STATs dimerize → enter the nucleus → turn on genes.
  • Key fact: signals from the common cytokine (hematopoietin) receptor family are transduced by tyrosine phosphorylation.
  • IL-2 → JAK1/JAK3 → STAT5 → T-cell proliferation genes. (Some summaries list STAT1/STAT3 for IL-2; the canonical answer is STAT5.)
  • Hematopoietic factors (IL-7, GM-CSF, G-CSF) also use this route.
CheckWhat biochemical event transduces the signal after a cytokine binds a common cytokine receptor?
Tyrosine phosphorylation by JAKs (of receptor and STATs).
Step 1 HY — JAK drugs + defects
  • JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib, ruxolitinib) → RA, psoriatic arthritis, UC, myelofibrosis.
  • JAK2 V617F → myeloproliferative neoplasms (polycythemia vera).
  • STAT3 loss → hyper-IgE (Job) syndrome (↓ Th17). JAK3 deficiency → autosomal recessive SCID (same T⁻B⁺NK⁻ picture as γc).
Step 1 trap

Cytokine receptors are not receptor tyrosine kinases. They have no intrinsic kinase; associated JAKs do the phosphorylating. (Insulin/growth-factor receptors are the true RTKs.)

Q3. What is the common γ chain, and why does losing it cause X-linked SCID?
  • γc (CD132) = shared signaling subunit of the IL-2, IL-4, IL-7, IL-9, IL-15 and IL-21 receptors; it partners with JAK3.
  • Gene on the X chromosome (IL2RG) → inherited defect = X-linked SCID (most common SCID).

B-cell development does not need γc, but B cells need T-cell help (absent) and IL-21/IL-4 signals (γc-dependent) to switch and become plasma cells.

Q4. How do TNF-family receptors signal?
  • TNF is active as a trimer → binds trimeric TNF receptors.
  • Two opposite outcomes, depending on the target cell:
    • NF-κB activation → inflammatory gene expression (adhesion molecules, more cytokines).
    • Death domains → caspases → apoptosis.
  • Other TNF/TNFR pairs to know:
    • Fas–FasL → apoptosis (cytotoxic lymphocytes kill Fas⁺ targets).
    • CD40–CD40L → costimulation, B-cell help (class switching, germinal centers, memory B cells).
    • BAFF → B-cell survival (via BAFF-R/TACI).
CheckTNF binding to an endothelial cell upregulates adhesion molecules. Which arm of TNF-R signaling is that?
The NF-κB (inflammatory gene) arm, not the caspase/apoptosis arm.
Step 1 HY

Fas or FasL defects → autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome (ALPS): lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, autoimmune cytopenias, ↑ double-negative (CD4⁻CD8⁻) T cells.

Step 1 trap

"TNF" sounds like killing, but in most clinical questions TNF-α acts through NF-κB-driven inflammation (shock, cachexia, granulomas). Apoptosis questions usually point to Fas–FasL or perforin/granzyme.

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1A 5-month-old boy has thrush, PCP and failure to thrive. Flow cytometry: almost no T or NK cells, normal B-cell count; lymphocytes lack CD132. Which receptors are defective?
γc-sharing receptors: IL-2R, IL-4R, IL-7R (plus IL-9, IL-15, IL-21R) → X-linked SCID. No IL-7 signal → no T cells; no IL-15 → no NK cells.
A2A girl has the same T⁻B⁺NK⁻ picture as X-linked SCID but an autosomal recessive pedigree. Which kinase is likely missing?

A3Predict: a neutrophil treated with a drug that locks its G proteins in the GDP-bound state is exposed to IL-8. What happens?

CheckUninfected epithelial cells next to a virus-infected one become resistant to the virus before T cells arrive. Why?
Type I IFN (IFN-α/β) from the infected cell → neighbors switch on antiviral programs.
Step 1 HY — anti-TNF

Step 1 trap

Type I IFN ↑ MHC I; IFN-γ ↑ MHC II. And etanercept is a fusion receptor ("-cept"), not a monoclonal antibody ("-mab").

Q10. Chemotactic: CXCL8 (IL-8)

CheckIL-17 is not a chemokine, yet it brings in neutrophils. How?
It makes tissue cells produce chemokines (e.g., CXCL8), which neutrophils follow via GPCRs.
Memory tool

Step 1 HY

ApplyLO 4 application questions

A1A traveler returns with a hookworm infection, 25% eosinophils and high IgE. Name the T-helper subset and the two cytokines behind each finding.
Th2: IL-5 → eosinophilia; IL-4 (+ IL-13) → IgE. Upstream, gut alarmins (IL-25 from tuft cells, IL-33, TSLP) started it.
A2A patient on chemotherapy has an ANC of 300/µL. Which recombinant cytokine most specifically restores neutrophils, and how does it differ from sargramostim?
G-CSF (filgrastim) — neutrophil-specific. Sargramostim (GM-CSF) is broader: granulocytes + macrophages.
A3A child mounts an IgG2 response to pneumococcal capsular polysaccharide without T-cell help. Which DC cytokine allowed the class switch?
BAFF — TNF-family cytokine from DCs; provides the T-independent (TI-2) switch signal.
A4A psoriasis patient's skin shows neutrophil microabscesses. Trace the cytokine chain from DC to neutrophil.
IL-23 (+ IL-6, TGF-β) → Th17 → IL-17 → keratinocyte chemokines (CXCL8) → neutrophils; IL-22 → keratinocyte proliferation.
A5In the intestinal lamina propria, most B cells switch to one isotype. Which isotype and which cytokine?
IgA, driven by TGF-β — secretory IgA neutralizes microbes at the mucosal surface without inflammation.

1A 6-month-old boy has oral thrush, Pneumocystis pneumonia and chronic diarrhea. His maternal uncle died in infancy of infections. Flow cytometry shows almost no CD3⁺ T cells or CD56⁺ NK cells, but normal numbers of CD19⁺ B cells. A defect in which molecule best explains these findings?

C. Common γ chain. X-linked pattern + T⁻B⁺NK⁻ = γc deficiency (shared by IL-2, IL-4, IL-7, IL-9, IL-15, IL-21 receptors). No IL-7 → no T cells; no IL-15 → no NK cells. A CD40L gives hyper-IgM with normal T-cell numbers. B ADA is autosomal recessive and usually T⁻B⁻NK⁻. D IL-7Rα deficiency is autosomal recessive with normal NK cells. E STAT3 loss = hyper-IgE syndrome.

2A 45-year-old woman with rheumatoid arthritis is about to start adalimumab. Her physician orders an interferon-γ release assay first. Which normal function of the targeted cytokine explains the need for this test?

D. Adalimumab blocks TNF-α, which is required to form and maintain granulomas; blocking it can reactivate latent TB. A is IL-4, B IL-5, C type I IFN, E G-CSF.

3A man with metastatic renal cell carcinoma receives high-dose recombinant IL-2. Two days later he develops hypotension, weight gain, peripheral edema and pulmonary congestion. Which property of cytokines best explains these effects?

A. Pleiotropy — systemic IL-2 acts on many cell types (endothelium, lymphocytes, NK cells) → capillary leak syndrome. B redundancy means different cytokines share an effect. C antagonism = one blocks another. D and E would limit, not spread, effects.

4A 30-year-old man returns from rural Southeast Asia with abdominal pain and iron-deficiency anemia. Stool shows hookworm eggs. CBC shows 22% eosinophils. Which cytokine most directly explains the eosinophilia?

B. IL-5 (Th2) recruits and activates eosinophils. A IL-4 drives Th2 and IgE switching — the classic distractor. C, D are Th1. E IL-17 recruits neutrophils.

5A 58-year-old woman receiving chemotherapy for breast cancer has an absolute neutrophil count of 400/µL. Her oncologist prescribes a recombinant cytokine to specifically increase neutrophil production. Which cytokine is it?

E. G-CSF (filgrastim) specifically drives neutrophils. B GM-CSF (sargramostim) works but is broader (granulocytes + macrophages), so it is not the most specific. A IL-7 → lymphocytes. C M-CSF → monocytes/macrophages. D IL-2 → T-cell growth.

6A 34-year-old man with plaque psoriasis starts ustekinumab, an antibody against the p40 subunit shared by two cytokines made by dendritic cells. Differentiation of which T-helper subsets will be most reduced?

C. p40 is shared by IL-12 (→ Th1) and IL-23 (→ Th17). Th2 needs IL-4; Tregs need TGF-β (+ IL-2); TFH depend on IL-21/IL-6 and contact signals.

7A 2-year-old boy has recurrent otitis, sinusitis and Pneumocystis pneumonia. Serum IgM is elevated; IgG, IgA and IgE are nearly undetectable. Lymph nodes lack germinal centers. Which molecule is most likely defective?

D. CD40L — X-linked hyper-IgM: no contact signal for class switching or germinal centers; PCP because CD40L also activates macrophages. A BAFF loss would mainly reduce B-cell survival. B IL-21R loss impairs B cells but not this classic triad. C would only affect IgA. E → IBD-like inflammation.

8In a skin abscess, neutrophils migrate up a gradient of CXCL8 secreted by local macrophages and fibroblasts. Binding of CXCL8 to its receptor on the neutrophil directly triggers which event?

B. Chemokine receptors are GPCRs → G-protein activation → cytoskeletal rearrangement → migration. A class I/II cytokine receptors. C TGF-β receptor. D TNF-R/Fas. E IL-1R/TLR family.

9A 3-year-old girl develops disseminated infection after BCG vaccination and later has Salmonella bacteremia. Her T-cell, B-cell and neutrophil counts are normal. A receptor defect for a cytokine made by macrophages and dendritic cells is found. Which downstream process is most impaired?

E. IL-12 receptor deficiency → no Th1 differentiation or NK activation → ↓ IFN-γ → macrophages cannot kill intracellular mycobacteria/Salmonella. A, C are Th2 functions. B = NADPH oxidase (CGD). D is hepatic acute-phase output, independent of IL-12.

10A 67-year-old man with lobar pneumonia has a temperature of 39.4 °C and a serum C-reactive protein of 180 mg/L. Which cytokine is the principal stimulus for hepatic CRP synthesis?

A. IL-6, acting in an endocrine fashion on hepatocytes (IL-1β and TNF-α contribute). B, D are regulatory. C is a T-cell growth factor. E is antiviral.

11In a mouse model, B cells in the intestinal lamina propria fail to switch to the dominant mucosal isotype, while IgE and IgG responses in the spleen are normal. Loss of which cytokine best explains this?

C. TGF-β is required for IgA class switching in gut mucosa. A IL-4 → IgE (normal here). B IFN-γ → IgG. D IL-12 → Th1. E IL-17 → neutrophils.

12A 19-year-old woman has had persistent Candida infections of the nails, mouth and skin since childhood, without invasive fungal disease. Neutrophil function tests are normal. A defect in which cytokine pathway is most likely?

D. IL-17 (Th17) recruits neutrophils and boosts epithelial defense at mucosa and skin → defects cause chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis. A → eosinophils. B → antiviral. C loss → excess inflammation (IBD). E → myeloid production (and alveolar macrophage function).

13Specialized intestinal epithelial cells detect succinate produced by a newly established helminth and release a cytokine that activates type 2 innate lymphoid cells, starting a Th2 response. Which cytokine is released?

B. IL-25 from tuft cells — an epithelial alarmin (with IL-33 and TSLP). A, E drive Th1. C is a Th17 product acting on epithelium. D recruits neutrophils.

14A 28-year-old woman with systemic lupus erythematosus has persistent disease activity. She is started on a monoclonal antibody against a TNF-family cytokine, made largely by dendritic cells, that promotes survival of B cells (including autoreactive ones). Which cytokine is targeted?

E. BAFF (BLyS) — targeted by belimumab. A CD40L is TNF family but expressed by T cells. B tocilizumab targets IL-6R. C anti-TNF drugs are not standard SLE therapy (can induce drug-induced lupus). D IL-21 is from TFH, not a TNF-family member.
