Cancer Drugs I: Cytotoxic Chemotherapy

Learning objectives

  1. LO 1Explain what chemotherapy can realistically achieve for a cancer patient (true cure versus years of controlled disease) and what keeps drug treatment from working better.
  2. LO 2Judge whether an anticancer drug actually works by reading clinical-trial evidence: the right comparison group, the right endpoint and long enough follow-up.
  3. LO 3Explain how each family of cytotoxic drugs kills a dividing cell: spindle poisons, alkylating and platinum agents, anti-metabolites, and topoisomerase-targeting DNA drugs.
  4. LO 4List the side effects that every cytotoxic drug shares, and the signature organ toxicity that belongs to each individual drug.
  5. LO 5Explain why cytotoxic drugs are given in combination, name the parts of CMF, AC, FOLFIRI and FOLFIRINOX, and describe the two opposite jobs of leucovorin.

Q1. How does one damaged cell become a tumor that drugs struggle to kill?

  • A tumor is usually detected only after ~30+ doublings (hundreds of millions to ~10⁹ cells) → by diagnosis it already holds many variant clones, some of which may already resist a given drug.
  • Every patient's tumor has a different combination of driver mutations → cancer is a disease of the individual; treatment has to fit that tumor's biology.
  • Consequence → for best outcomes, different combinations of drugs are needed (LO 5).
CheckWhy might a tumor relapse after an excellent initial response to a single drug?
The tumor was heterogeneous from the start: a small pre-existing resistant subclone survived and was selected (Darwinian selection), then regrew.

Q3. What is the central limitation of cytotoxic drugs: the therapeutic window?
  • Cytotoxic drugs target proliferating cells, irrespective of the signal that drives proliferation.
  • They cannot tell a tumor cell from a normal fast-dividing cell → bone marrow, gut lining, hair follicles, gonads, the fetus are hit at the same doses that kill tumor.
  • Result → narrow therapeutic window: the dose that kills enough tumor is close to the dose that causes serious toxicity.

Other limits to effective drug treatment

  • Tumor heterogeneity / resistance → pre-existing or acquired resistant clones (details of resistance mechanisms come in the next session).
  • Late toxicity in survivors (Q5).
  • This is why targeted drugs and immunotherapy were developed: to exploit vulnerabilities specific to cancer cells.
CheckWhy do cytotoxic drugs with completely different mechanisms (a microtubule drug and an anti-metabolite) produce the same set of core side effects?
Because the shared side effects come from the common target population, proliferating normal cells, not from the specific mechanism.
Q4. Why do most classic chemotherapy drugs go after DNA?
  • A proliferating tumor cell must copy its DNA every cycle → DNA synthesis and DNA integrity are obligatory bottlenecks.
  • Many tumors have defective DNA repair and checkpoint control (genomic instability is how they pick up mutations) → DNA damage they cannot fix → cell death.
  • Normal cells generally repair DNA (or detoxify the drug) better → e.g., normal cells detoxify activated cyclophosphamide more efficiently.
  • Flip side → a tumor that improves its DNA repair becomes resistant.
CheckTrue or false: tumor cells have no DNA repair at all, which is why DNA drugs work.
False (overstated). Many tumors have defective repair/checkpoints, not absent repair; normal cells simply repair and detoxify better. Enhanced repair is a resistance mechanism.
Q5. "Cure" vs long-term survival: what do survivors carry with them?
  • Cure = cancer eradicated, normal life expectancy. Long-term survival = years of life with the disease controlled, often with ongoing or late treatment effects.
  • Combination chemotherapy has made several childhood cancers largely curable:
    • ALL 5-year survival now ~90%; Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma also dramatically improved since the 1970s.
    • Exception → CNS tumors (high-grade glioma, diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma) remain very poor.
  • ~20 million people in the USA will have survived cancer treatment by 2026 → late effects matter.
  • Toxicities are acute (mucositis, nausea, cytopenias) or late (cardiac, neuropathy, cognitive, endocrine, second cancers).
  • Chemobrain = long-term cognitive deficits (memory, attention) from ↓ turnover of neural progenitor cells (they divide too).
  • Adult survivors of childhood cancer → premature-ageing pattern across organs:
CheckWhy does chemotherapy cause long-term memory problems even though neurons do not divide?
The brain still has small pools of dividing neural progenitor cells; chemo reduces their turnover → chemobrain.
Step 1 HY
  • Synthetic lethality: PARP inhibitors (olaparib) kill BRCA1/2-mutant breast, ovarian, pancreatic and prostate cancers, which already lack homologous-recombination repair. Same logic as "tumors can't fix DNA damage."
  • Adjuvant = after surgery to kill micrometastases; neoadjuvant = before surgery to shrink the tumor.
Step 1 trap

"Cytotoxic drugs are selective for cancer cells" is wrong. Selectivity is only relative: they hit all dividing cells, and the window between tumor kill and marrow/gut toxicity is narrow. Selectivity comes from tumors' faster cycling and poorer repair/detoxification, not from a tumor-only target.

Buzzwords
"disease of the individual" → unique driver combination per tumor"irrespective of the proliferation signal" → cytotoxic drugs"chemobrain" → ↓ neural progenitor turnover"premature ageing in survivors" → late effects of childhood therapy

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1An oncologist chooses an aggressive, highly toxic regimen for metastatic pancreatic cancer but a gentle approach for low-risk prostate cancer. Justify.
Acceptable toxicity scales with how lethal the cancer is. Pancreatic cancer kills ~76–79% of patients; prostate ~13%. Different biology → different acceptable acute and long-term risks.
A2A 34-year-old survivor of childhood leukemia has hearing loss, infertility and early dyslipidemia. What concept explains this?
Late toxicity / premature ageing after cure. Treatment damaged proliferating and sensitive tissues years earlier; survivors need lifelong follow-up.
A3A drug kills 90% of tumor cells at a dose that also causes 70% neutropenia. What does this tell you about its therapeutic index?
Narrow therapeutic window. Effective and toxic doses overlap because both tumor and marrow progenitors are dividing.
A4Predict: a tumor clone acquires much better DNA repair. How will it respond to cisplatin?
Poorly (resistance). Cisplatin works because DNA crosslinks go unrepaired; better repair removes the adducts.

LO 2Proving efficacy in clinical trialsControls, endpoints and follow-up

By the end: you can explain why only controlled trials prove benefit, distinguish relapse-free from overall survival, and describe the 20-year CMF adjuvant breast-cancer trial and its dose-response result.
Q1. How do we know that a cancer treatment actually works?
  • Only well-controlled clinical trials can show that a drug (or any intervention) improves patient outcome.
  • A new treatment must be better than, or at least as good as, existing treatment (the comparator is standard of care, or observation when none exists).
  • Depending on the cancer's natural course, it may take several years of follow-up to judge efficacy, adverse effects and overall benefit.
  • Two key endpoints:
    • Relapse-free survival → is the cancer kept away? (tumor response)
    • Overall survival → does the patient live longer? (the endpoint that proves life is prolonged)
  • Tumor shrinkage or delayed relapse alone does not prove longer life.
CheckA drug delays relapse but overall survival data are not yet available. Can you say it prolongs life?
No. Relapse-free and overall survival are different endpoints; only overall survival shows the patient lives longer.
Q2. What did the 1973 CMF adjuvant breast-cancer trial show?
386 womennode-positive breast cancer, radical mastectomy
→
179 observationno chemotherapy (control)
+
207 adjuvant CMF12 monthly cycles at low (71), medium (94) or high (42) dose
→
20-year follow-uprelapse-free + overall survival
  • CMF = Cyclophosphamide + Methotrexate + 5-Fluorouracil (1 DNA-adduct former + 2 anti-metabolites).
  • Relapse-free at 20 y: ~¼ no treatment · ~⅓ low dose · ~½ high dose.
  • Overall survival followed the same pattern → low dose = no survival benefit over observation; benefit appeared only at higher doses.
  • Lesson → benefit was dose-dependent; but "higher dose = better" is not a universal rule. It must be proven in trials for each drug and cancer.
CheckWhich CMF dose group had the same 20-year survival as untreated women?
Low dose (25% vs 25%).
Step 1 HY
  • Survival curves = Kaplan–Meier plots; the comparison is a log-rank test; effect size usually a hazard ratio.
  • Randomization controls confounding; blinding controls bias; intention-to-treat analysis keeps randomization intact.
  • Surrogate endpoints (tumor shrinkage, progression-free survival) may not translate into longer overall survival.
Step 1 trap

A response (tumor shrinks, relapse delayed) ≠ the patient lives longer. When an answer choice says a drug "improves survival" based only on response or relapse data, reject it.

Memory tool

CMF 20-year scorecard: "25-25-32-52." Control and low are twins; only high breaks away (about doubles survival).

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A company reports its new drug shrinks tumors in 60% of patients in a single-arm study. Does this prove it is better than standard therapy?
No. No control arm and no survival endpoint; it must be compared with existing treatment in a controlled trial.
A2Why did the CMF investigators follow patients for 20 years instead of stopping at 2?
Breast cancer relapses late. The natural course of the cancer sets how long follow-up must be to judge efficacy, toxicity and overall benefit.
A3Based on the CMF trial, a resident suggests always using the highest tolerable dose of every chemotherapy. Critique.
Overgeneralization. CMF showed a dose-response in that setting, but more drug also means more toxicity; the best dose has to be established in trials for each regimen and cancer.

Cell-cycle phase of action

CheckWhich two drugs in this lecture still act on cells sitting quietly in G0?
Cyclophosphamide and the platins (cisplatin, oxaliplatin): they are cell-cycle nonspecific DNA-adduct formers.
Step 1 HY — phase map
  • M: vinca alkaloids, taxanes (also eribulin, ixabepilone).
  • S: anti-metabolites (MTX, 5-FU, 6-MP, cytarabine, hydroxyurea, gemcitabine); topo I inhibitors are S-phase heavy.
  • G2: bleomycin and etoposide (topo II); anthracyclines are commonly grouped with G2/M.
  • Nonspecific: alkylating agents, platins, nitrosoureas.
Memory tool

"Build → Copy → Divide." Anti-metabolites starve the cell of building blocks (S), topo drugs break DNA while it is copied (S/G2), spindle poisons stop division (M). Alkylators/platins don't care about timing: "glue anywhere."

Q2. How do the mitotic spindle poisons (vincristine, paclitaxel) work?
  • Microtubules are built from α/β-tubulin and constantly polymerize and depolymerize; the mitotic spindle needs both steps.
  • Both drugs are plant alkaloids (plant defenses against being eaten):
    • Vincristine (vinca alkaloid, rosy periwinkle Vinca rosea; also vinblastine) → binds tubulin, inhibits microtubule polymerization → no spindle forms.
    • Paclitaxel (taxane, Pacific yew Taxus brevifolia) → stabilizes microtubules (blocks depolymerization) → spindle "frozen," chromosomes cannot separate → no anaphase.
  • Opposite actions, same result → arrest in M phase → mitosis block → cell death.
  • Extra toxicity → microtubules also run axonal transport in long, non-dividing neurons → dose-dependent peripheral neuropathy (LO 4).
CheckA drug binds β-tubulin and prevents it from assembling. Where do treated cells pile up, and which drug is it?
M phase; vincristine (vinca alkaloid). Paclitaxel also arrests in M, but by stabilizing microtubules.
Step 1 HY
  • Vincristine: ALL, Hodgkin (in MOPP/ABVD-family regimens); dose-limiting neuropathy, constipation/paralytic ileus, little marrow toxicity.
  • Vinblastine: "blasts the bone marrow" (myelosuppression).
  • Paclitaxel: breast, ovarian, lung; myelosuppression, neuropathy, hypersensitivity (premedicate).
Step 1 trap

Don't swap the verbs. Vinca = can't build (inhibits polymerization). Taxane = can't tear down (stabilizes, prevents depolymerization). Colchicine, griseofulvin and mebendazole also block polymerization but are not anticancer drugs.

Memory tool

"Taxes stabilize society" (taxanes stabilize microtubules). "Vin-CRISP-tine crisps the nerves"; "vin-BLAST-ine blasts the marrow."

Q3. How do alkylating agents and platinum drugs damage DNA?

Origin

  • Sulfur mustard (WWI poison gas) → hemorrhagic lung damage, and bone marrow suppression in survivors → idea: kill fast-dividing blood cancers.
  • Derived drugs → mechlorethamine (nitrogen mustard; the "M" in MOPP for Hodgkin lymphoma) and cyclophosphamide (the "C" in CMF for breast cancer).

Cyclophosphamide: an oral prodrug activated in the liver

Cyclophosphamidegiven orally (or IV), inactive
→
Hepatic CYPs→ 4-hydroxycyclophosphamide ⇌ aldophosphamide
→
Phosphoramide mustardDNA alkylation (anticancer)
+
Acrolein→ hemorrhagic cystitis
  • Detox branch → enzymatic oxidation and aldehyde dehydrogenase convert intermediates to inactive 4-ketocyclophosphamide and carboxyphosphamide.
  • Selectivity → activation happens in the liver, not in the tumor; normal cells detoxify the active alkylator better than many tumor cells (and repair DNA better).

The DNA lesion

  • The reactive species covalently attaches to N7 of guanine → monoalkylation, then interstrand and intrastrand crosslinks.
  • Crosslinked strands cannot separate → replication fails → strand breaks → cell death.

Platinum drugs: cisplatin, oxaliplatin

  • Discovered when platinum released from electrodes into culture water stopped bacterial growth.
  • Form the same kind of N7-guanine adducts / crosslinks as cyclophosphamide → same end result.
  • Key difference → platins need no metabolic activation.
  • Used in combinations for solid tumors (e.g., oxaliplatin in FOLFIRINOX for pancreatic/colon cancer).
CheckIs cyclophosphamide activated by the cancer cells themselves?
No. It is activated by hepatic CYPs. Its relative tumor selectivity comes from normal cells detoxifying the active alkylator (e.g., aldehyde dehydrogenase) and repairing DNA better.
Step 1 HY
  • Cyclophosphamide: also an immunosuppressant (lupus nephritis, vasculitis); toxicities: hemorrhagic cystitis (prevent with mesna + hydration), ↑ bladder cancer, SIADH, myelosuppression.
  • Platins: testicular, bladder, ovarian, lung, GI cancers; nephrotoxicity (prevent with saline hydration ± amifostine), ototoxicity, neuropathy.
  • Nitrosoureas (carmustine, lomustine) cross the BBB → brain tumors; busulfan → pulmonary fibrosis.
Step 1 trap

Platins are often called "alkylating-like," but they are not prodrugs; cyclophosphamide (and ifosfamide) are. If a stem asks which DNA crosslinker needs liver activation, the answer is cyclophosphamide.

Buzzwords
"N7 of guanine" → alkylator / platin adduct"WWI mustard gas, marrow suppression" → nitrogen mustards"acrolein" → hemorrhagic cystitis"no metabolic activation" → cisplatin, oxaliplatin
Q4. How do topoisomerase inhibitors (irinotecan, doxorubicin) cause DNA breaks?
  • Topoisomerases cut DNA to relieve supercoiling during replication, let a strand pass, then religate the cut.
  • Inhibitors block religation → the enzyme stays stuck on cut DNA → strand breaks during DNA replication → cell death.

Irinotecan

  • Topoisomerase I inhibitor (camptothecin)
  • Use → FOLFIRI (colon) and FOLFIRINOX (metastatic pancreatic / colon)
  • Toxicity → GI (diarrhea), marrow; no cardiotoxicity

Doxorubicin (Adriamycin)

  • Anthracycline; topoisomerase (II) inhibitor
  • Use → AC regimen (Adriamycin + cyclophosphamide) for breast cancer
  • Toxicity → cardiotoxicity (oxidative stress, iron chelation)
CheckWhich topoisomerase inhibitor would you avoid in a patient with heart failure?
Doxorubicin. Irinotecan is not cardiotoxic.
Step 1 HY
  • Topo I: irinotecan, topotecan ("-tecans").
  • Topo II: etoposide, teniposide (from the mayapple), and anthracyclines.
  • Anthracyclines also intercalate DNA and generate free radicals → dilated cardiomyopathy; protect with dexrazoxane (iron chelator).
  • Irinotecan: early cholinergic diarrhea (atropine) and late diarrhea (loperamide); UGT1A1*28 (Gilbert) ↑ toxicity.
Memory tool

"-TecAN = topo ONE" (irinotecan, topotecan: single-strand nicks). Topo II = "double trouble": etoposide and doxorubicin cause double-strand breaks, and doxorubicin adds a second problem, the heart.

Q5. How do the anti-metabolites methotrexate and 5-FU block DNA synthesis?

Both attack the folate one-carbon cycle that makes dTMP (thymidine for DNA) and the purine ring.

5-Fluorouracil (5-FU): thymidylate synthase

  • Normal: thymidylate synthase (TS) adds a CH₃ to dUMP → dTMP (deoxyuridylate → deoxythymidylate; both are DNA precursors). The CH₃ comes from N5,10-methylene-FH4.
  • 5-FU = uracil with fluorine in place of H at C5 → converted to FdUMP → the F sits exactly where the CH₃ must go → TS is trapped → ↓ dTMP → ↓ dTTP → ↓ DNA synthesis.
  • Capecitabine = oral prodrug of 5-FU; works the same way.

Methotrexate (MTX): dihydrofolate reductase

  • Normal: DHFR reduces dihydrofolate (FH2) → tetrahydrofolate (FH4). (It adds hydrogens; it does not remove a methyl group.)
  • MTX = folate analog → inhibits DHFR → FH4 runs out → no one-carbon donors:
    • ↓ N5,10-methylene-FH4 → TS has no CH₃ → ↓ dTMP
    • ↓ N10-formyl-FH4 → ↓ purine ring synthesis
    • → ↓ DNA (and RNA) synthesis
  • Polyglutamation → like natural folates, MTX gets a chain of glutamates inside both normal and tumor cells → trapped storage form with much greater potency, now also inhibiting TS and early purine-synthesis enzymes. FH2-polyglutamates piling up behind the DHFR block also inhibit TS.
  • MTX's effect can be bypassed by giving a reduced folate → leucovorin (LO 5).

Target amplification = resistance

  • DHFR gene amplification in a tumor → more enzyme than MTX can inhibit → poor response (resistance) to methotrexate.
  • Same logic → TS amplification → resistance to 5-FU.
  • Rule → mutation or overexpression of the drug's target = a major resistance mechanism.
CheckWhich enzyme does each drug inhibit, and which product is lost? (a) methotrexate (b) 5-FU
(a) DHFR → ↓ FH4 → ↓ dTMP and ↓ purines. (b) Thymidylate synthase (via FdUMP) → ↓ dTMP.
Step 1 HY
  • MTX non-cancer uses: low-dose for RA, psoriasis, IBD; ectopic pregnancy/abortion (strongly teratogenic). Toxicity: myelosuppression, mucositis, hepatotoxicity, pulmonary fibrosis, nephrotoxicity at high dose.
  • 5-FU: colon, pancreatic cancer; topical for actinic keratosis/basal cell carcinoma; myelosuppression, hand-foot syndrome. DPD deficiency → severe toxicity; overdose antidote uridine triacetate.
  • Gemcitabine = difluoro-deoxycytidine → chain termination (pancreatic, NSCLC).
Step 1 trap
  • Leucovorin rescues MTX but NOT 5-FU; with 5-FU it makes toxicity worse (it potentiates TS inhibition).
  • Amplified target ≠ good response for enzyme-inhibitor drugs. DHFR amplification → MTX resistance. Contrast: HER2 amplification predicts response to trastuzumab, because there the amplified gene is the tumor's driver and the antibody blocks it.

"FdUMP" → 5-FU traps TS"dUMP → dTMP" → thymidylate synthase"FH2 → FH4" → DHFR, methotrexate"polyglutamation" → MTX trapping + ↑ potency"oral 5-FU prodrug" → capecitabine"DHFR amplification" → MTX resistance

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1A colon cancer stops responding to 5-FU. Sequencing shows massive amplification of the thymidylate synthase gene. Explain.
Target overexpression. Too much TS for FdUMP to block → dTMP synthesis continues. Same logic as DHFR amplification → MTX resistance.
A2Two drugs both arrest cells in mitosis, but one makes microtubules disappear and the other makes them abnormally stable. Name them.
Vincristine (blocks polymerization → no microtubules) and paclitaxel (stabilizes → frozen spindle).
A3A patient with severe liver failure receives cyclophosphamide. Predict its antitumor effect.
Reduced. Cyclophosphamide is an inactive prodrug that needs hepatic CYP activation to form phosphoramide mustard.
A4A slowly growing tumor has most of its cells resting in G0. Which class from this lecture is most likely to still damage them?
Alkylating agents / platins (cyclophosphamide, cisplatin): cell-cycle nonspecific. S- and M-phase drugs need cells to be cycling.
A5A lab adds excess thymidine to cells treated with 5-FU. Predict the effect on DNA synthesis.
Partially restored. Thymidine is salvaged to dTMP by thymidine kinase, bypassing the blocked thymidylate synthase.

Memory tool

"Hair, Gut, Blood, Baby, Gonads": the five fast dividers chemo always hits.

Q2. What is each drug's signature (organ-specific) toxicity?

Doxorubicin → heart

Cardiotoxicity (cardiomyopathy) tied to lifetime cumulative dose.

Mechanism → oxidative stress + iron chelation in cardiomyocytes.

Irinotecan → gut

GI toxicity (diarrhea); NO cardiotoxicity, even though it is also a topoisomerase inhibitor.

Cisplatin → kidney (+ nerves)

Nephrotoxicity, more than oxaliplatin; plus peripheral neuropathy (all platins).

Oxaliplatin → nerves

Peripheral neuropathy (all platins); less kidney damage.

Mechanism differs from microtubule drugs.

Vincristine · paclitaxel → nerves

Dose-dependent peripheral neuropathy from disrupted axonal microtubule transport.

Cyclophosphamide → bladder

Hemorrhagic cystitis from the urinary metabolite acrolein.

Methotrexate → fetus

Teratogenic; marrow/mucosal toxicity reversed by leucovorin rescue.

5-FU

Shared profile: myelosuppression, mucositis, diarrhea; leucovorin increases its effect (and toxicity).

CheckWhich drug classes in this lecture cause peripheral neuropathy? Do they share a mechanism?
Vinca alkaloids, taxanes and all platins. Microtubule drugs act through axonal transport; platins cause neuropathy by a different mechanism.
Step 1 HY — rescue / prevention
  • Mesna (binds acrolein) → cyclophosphamide/ifosfamide cystitis.
  • Amifostine (free-radical scavenger) + saline hydration → cisplatin nephrotoxicity.
  • Dexrazoxane (iron chelator) → anthracycline cardiotoxicity; monitor LVEF (echo/MUGA).
  • Leucovorin → MTX rescue. Uridine triacetate → 5-FU overdose.
  • Cisplatin also → ototoxicity, hypomagnesemia.
Step 1 trap

Both doxorubicin and irinotecan inhibit topoisomerase, but only doxorubicin is cardiotoxic. Its heart damage comes from oxidative stress / iron, not from topoisomerase inhibition. Also: trastuzumab + doxorubicin together compound cardiotoxicity.

ApplyLO 4 application questions

A1Eight years after AC chemotherapy for breast cancer, a woman has exertional dyspnea and a dilated, poorly contracting left ventricle. Culprit and risk factor?
Doxorubicin (the "A"); risk rises with lifetime cumulative dose (oxidative stress, iron chelation). Cyclophosphamide's signature is cystitis, not cardiomyopathy.
A2Three weeks after starting cisplatin for testicular cancer, a man's creatinine doubles. Would switching to oxaliplatin help?
Cisplatin nephrotoxicity. Oxaliplatin is less nephrotoxic, but all platins still cause peripheral neuropathy.
A3A child with ALL on vincristine develops foot drop, absent ankle reflexes and severe constipation. Explain.
Vincristine neurotoxicity: blocked microtubule polymerization disrupts axonal transport → peripheral and autonomic neuropathy (dose-limiting).
A4A 29-year-old with newly diagnosed breast cancer is 8 weeks pregnant. Why delay cytotoxic chemotherapy, and what else must be discussed?
Teratogenicity: the embryo is the most rapidly proliferating tissue. Also discuss fertility: chemo causes premature menopause (~42% in the 30s) and ↓ spermatogenesis in men.
A5A patient on high-dose cyclophosphamide develops gross hematuria and dysuria. Cause and prevention?
Hemorrhagic cystitis from acrolein in urine. Prevent with aggressive hydration and mesna (binds acrolein).

By the end: you can decode each regimen letter by letter, say what cancer it treats, explain why its drugs are combined, and explain why leucovorin is a rescue for methotrexate but a booster for 5-FU.
Q1. Why combine cytotoxic drugs at all?
  • Tumors are heterogeneous (LO 1) → one drug leaves resistant subclones behind.
  • Combine drugs with different mechanisms (and partly different toxicities) →
    • kill more tumor cells and their variants
    • reduce the chance of resistance
    • spread toxicity across different organs instead of stacking one
  • Combinations must themselves be tested in trials for efficacy and adverse effects (LO 2).
CheckCMF combines which mechanism classes?
One DNA-adduct former (cyclophosphamide) + two DNA-synthesis inhibitors / anti-metabolites (methotrexate, 5-FU).

Step 1 trap

The "FOL" in FOLFIRI is folinic acid (leucovorin), not folic acid. Folic acid would need DHFR to become active; folinic acid does not.

Q3. Leucovorin: why is it a rescue for methotrexate but a booster for 5-FU?
  • Leucovorin = folinic acid = N5-formyl-FH4 → a reduced folate that enters the folate pool without needing DHFR. (Also called "citrovorum factor," after the bacterium Leuconostoc citrovorum that needs it to grow.)

With methotrexate → RESCUE (↓ toxicity)

  • MTX blocks DHFR → FH4 runs out.
  • Leucovorin supplies reduced folate beyond the block → restores dTMP and purine synthesis in marrow and gut.
  • Used for MTX overdose and timed "rescue" after high-dose MTX.
  • Given together at full dose it would cancel MTX's effect → hence timed after.

With 5-FU → ENHANCEMENT (↑ efficacy)

  • Leucovorin ↑ N5,10-methylene-FH4.
  • This folate stabilizes the FdUMP–TS complex (tighter binding) → more complete TS inhibition.
  • → Given together on purpose: FOLFIRI, FOLFIRINOX.

Step 1 HY — drug interactions
  • MTX toxicity ↑ with drugs that ↓ its renal clearance (NSAIDs, probenecid, penicillins, PPIs) and with TMP-SMX (additional antifolate).
  • Doxorubicin + trastuzumab → additive cardiotoxicity; avoid giving together.
  • Irinotecan is a CYP3A4 substrate: inducers ↓ / inhibitors ↑ exposure.

ApplyLO 5 application questions

A1A patient accidentally receives a 10-fold methotrexate overdose and develops mucositis and falling counts. Antidote, and why does it work while DHFR is still blocked?
Leucovorin. It is already a reduced (FH4-type) folate, so it bypasses DHFR and restores thymidylate and purine synthesis.
A2A woman with metastatic colon cancer on FOLFIRINOX develops numb, tingling hands and feet. Which component?
Oxaliplatin (platin neuropathy). Irinotecan → diarrhea; 5-FU → marrow/mucositis; leucovorin → not toxic itself.
A3A student proposes adding leucovorin to 5-FU "to protect the bone marrow." What will actually happen?
5-FU effect (and toxicity) increases. Leucovorin stabilizes the FdUMP–TS complex; it rescues only from DHFR inhibition (MTX).
A4Why is CMF a more "rational" combination than giving three anti-metabolites together?
CMF mixes different mechanisms (DNA adduct + two synthesis blocks) so a clone resistant to one is likely killed by another, and toxicities are partly spread out.

1A 7-year-old boy with acute lymphoblastic leukemia receiving induction chemotherapy develops bilateral foot drop, absent Achilles reflexes and severe constipation. Blood counts are only mildly reduced. The responsible drug most likely acts by which mechanism?

C. Vincristine. Neuropathy (motor, sensory, autonomic → constipation) with relatively spared marrow is classic. A (paclitaxel) also causes neuropathy but is not used in ALL and causes more myelosuppression. B (alkylators/platins), D (MTX) and E (irinotecan) do not produce this picture.

2A 52-year-old woman completed adjuvant chemotherapy for breast cancer 6 years ago. She now has progressive exertional dyspnea, an S3 gallop and an echocardiogram showing a dilated left ventricle with ejection fraction 30%. Which agent was most likely responsible?

A. Doxorubicin (the "A" in AC) causes cumulative-dose dilated cardiomyopathy via oxidative stress and iron chelation. B → hemorrhagic cystitis. C → marrow, mucositis, teratogenic. D is also a topoisomerase inhibitor but is not cardiotoxic. E → marrow, mucositis.

3A 45-year-old woman with a large-vessel vasculitis is treated with high-dose intravenous cyclophosphamide. Two days later she has gross hematuria and suprapubic pain. Which intervention would have most specifically prevented this complication?

D. Mesna binds the urinary metabolite acrolein, preventing hemorrhagic cystitis (with hydration). A rescues from MTX. B protects the heart from anthracyclines. C protects kidneys from cisplatin. E prevents uric-acid nephropathy in tumor lysis.

4A 28-year-old man with metastatic testicular cancer receives a platinum-based regimen. Three weeks later his serum creatinine has risen from 0.9 to 2.0 mg/dL and he reports new tinnitus. Which statement about this drug is correct?

B. Cisplatin forms N7-guanine adducts/crosslinks, needs no metabolic activation (A describes cyclophosphamide), and is more nephrotoxic (and ototoxic) than oxaliplatin. C = doxorubicin/etoposide. D = methotrexate. E is wrong: platins are cell-cycle nonspecific.

5A patient with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma begins a four-drug regimen. After several cycles she reports numbness and tingling in her fingers and toes, worse when she holds a cold drink. Which component of her regimen is most likely responsible?

E. Oxaliplatin. FOLFIRINOX = folinic acid + 5-FU + irinotecan + oxaliplatin; all platins cause peripheral neuropathy, and oxaliplatin's is classically cold-triggered. A is not toxic itself. B → marrow, mucositis, hand-foot. C → diarrhea. D is not part of FOLFIRINOX.

6A man with osteosarcoma receives high-dose methotrexate. Twenty-four hours later, a second drug is started to protect his bone marrow and gut mucosa. This second drug works by which mechanism?

C. Leucovorin (N5-formyl-FH4) is already reduced, so it enters the folate pool beyond DHFR and restores dTMP and purine synthesis. A: DHFR stays inhibited. B describes glucarpidase, not leucovorin. D is leucovorin's effect with 5-FU (enhancement). E: leucovorin is a folate, not thymidine.

7A tumor initially responds to methotrexate but later progresses. Biopsy shows a 40-fold increase in copy number of a gene encoding an enzyme that reduces dihydrofolate. Which conclusion is best supported?

B. DHFR amplification → more enzyme than MTX can inhibit → resistance. A confuses this with HER2 amplification (which predicts trastuzumab response). C is another resistance mechanism, but not what the data show. D: leucovorin would rescue, not kill. E is unrelated to the finding.

8Investigators expose cultured carcinoma cells to a drug and find that after 24 hours most surviving cells have condensed chromosomes, an intact but non-depolymerizing mitotic spindle, and no cells in anaphase. Which drug was most likely used?

D. Paclitaxel stabilizes microtubules → spindle forms but cannot depolymerize → no anaphase → M arrest. A also causes M arrest, but no spindle forms. B and E act in S (/G2). C is cell-cycle nonspecific.

9In a randomized adjuvant trial, women with node-positive breast cancer were assigned after mastectomy to observation or to one of three doses of a three-drug regimen and followed for 20 years. Survival was 25% (observation), 25% (low), 32% (medium) and 52% (high dose). Which conclusion is most appropriate?

A. Only higher doses beat observation; low dose matched control. B contradicts the data. C overgeneralizes (must be shown per drug/cancer). D: overall survival is what proves longer life, and long follow-up was needed. E: the regimen was CMF (alkylator + two anti-metabolites).

10A 60-year-old man with metastatic colon cancer is started on folinic acid, 5-fluorouracil and irinotecan. What is the purpose of the folinic acid in this regimen?

E. In FOLFIRI, leucovorin ↑ N5,10-methylene-FH4, which stabilizes the FdUMP–TS complex → greater 5-FU efficacy. A: it does not rescue topoisomerase damage. B: not its role. C: that is methotrexate. D: it is given for potentiation, not nutrition.

11A patient with advanced cirrhosis and markedly reduced hepatic CYP activity requires treatment for lymphoma. Which of the following drugs would be expected to have the most reduced antitumor activity?

C. Cyclophosphamide is an inactive oral prodrug converted by hepatic CYPs to 4-hydroxycyclophosphamide → phosphoramide mustard. A acts directly without activation. B, D and E are active as given (hepatic dysfunction may raise their toxicity by slowing clearance, but does not prevent activation).

12A 31-year-old woman is about to begin cytotoxic chemotherapy for breast cancer. Which counseling point applies to essentially all cytotoxic anticancer drugs, regardless of their mechanism?

B. Gonadal toxicity (premature menopause ~42% when treated in the 30s) and teratogenicity follow from killing proliferating cells, and are shared by all cytotoxic drugs. A (doxorubicin), C (cyclophosphamide), D (cisplatin) and E (oxaliplatin) are drug-specific signatures.

13Cancer cells treated with a drug accumulate in S phase. Biochemical analysis shows that intracellular dUMP is markedly increased and dTMP is depleted, while tetrahydrofolate levels are normal. Which drug was used?

D. 5-FU (as FdUMP) inhibits thymidylate synthase directly → dUMP piles up, dTMP falls, FH4 is unaffected. A would also lower dTMP but by depleting FH4. B and E inhibit topoisomerases; C forms DNA adducts; none raises dUMP specifically.
