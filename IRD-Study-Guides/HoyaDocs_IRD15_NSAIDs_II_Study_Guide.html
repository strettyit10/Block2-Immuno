LO 5Adverse effects & NSAID hypersensitivityWhat goes wrong at normal doses, at toxic doses, and why some asthmatics wheeze

By the end: you can sort NSAID harms by dose, explain the leukotriene "shunt" behind NSAID hypersensitivity, give its incidence and timing, and name the one safe analgesic alternative.
Q1. What are the big NSAID side effects, and which happen at normal (therapeutic) vs toxic doses?

Almost every NSAID harm comes from the same place: prostaglandins (PGs) do protective "housekeeping" jobs, and an NSAID removes them everywhere, not just at the inflamed joint.

CheckReye syndrome: is it a feature of aspirin overdose?
No. Reye syndrome is a risk of giving aspirin to a child with a viral illness (influenza, varicella), even at normal doses; the risk is higher with aspirin than with acetaminophen or ibuprofen. It is not an overdose phenomenon.
Q2. What is NSAID hypersensitivity, how common is it, and is it a true allergy?
  • Incidence: 10–20% of asthmatics vs 1–2% of the general population.
  • Timing: minutes to hours after ingestion.
  • Looks like a conventional drug allergy (wheeze, flushing, rhinorrhea, urticaria, angioedema) but no immunologic mechanism is involved → a pseudo-allergic (non-IgE) reaction.
    • No prior sensitization needed; can occur on first exposure.
  • Why asthmatics? They have higher baseline lipoxygenase (LOX) activity, so they are primed to make leukotrienes.
CheckWould a skin-prick test or serum IgE against ibuprofen help confirm the diagnosis?
No. The reaction is not IgE-mediated, so allergy tests are negative. Diagnosis is clinical (or by supervised challenge).
Q3. How does blocking COX make someone wheeze? (the shunt theory)

  • Alternative: acetaminophen, the only option for a patient with aspirin/NSAID hypersensitivity (weak peripheral COX inhibitor, not an NSAID, does not shunt AA).
CheckAn asthmatic wheezed after aspirin. Her friend suggests naproxen "because it's a totally different chemical." Right or wrong?
Wrong. Cross-reactivity follows the mechanism (COX block → leukotriene shunt), not structure. Avoid the whole class; use acetaminophen.
Step 1 HY
  • Aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (Samter triad) = asthma + nasal polyps + NSAID sensitivity.
  • Leukotriene modifiers help: montelukast/zafirlukast (CysLT1 receptor blockers) and zileuton (5-LOX inhibitor).
  • LTC₄/D₄/E₄ → bronchoconstriction, vasoconstriction, ↑ permeability; LTB₄ → neutrophil chemotaxis.
Step 1 trap

Don't pick "IgE-mediated type I hypersensitivity" or "mast-cell degranulation by IgE cross-linking." NSAID-induced bronchospasm is pseudo-allergic: a drug-induced shift in eicosanoid synthesis. And don't pick a "structurally different NSAID" as the safe swap.

Memory tool

"Dam one river, the other floods." COX is dammed by the NSAID → arachidonic acid floods the LOX channel → leukotrienes. Asthmatics already have a wide LOX channel.

ApplyLO 5 application questions

A1A 30-year-old asthmatic develops wheezing and facial flushing 1 hour after ibuprofen. She now has a sprained ankle. What do you give?
Acetaminophen. All NSAIDs (naproxen, ketorolac, celecoxib…) cross-react via the leukotriene shunt.
A2Predict: if you gave this patient zileuton before aspirin, would the reaction be larger or smaller?
Smaller. Zileuton blocks 5-LOX, so the shunted arachidonic acid can't become leukotrienes.
A3A man has taken low-dose aspirin daily for years with no problems. He develops gastric bleeding. Is this a "toxic-dose" effect?
No: a therapeutic-dose effect. GI injury and bleeding occur at normal doses (↓ mucosal PGs + irreversible platelet COX-1 block). Toxic-dose effects are salicylism and acid–base disturbance.

LO 6Contraindications & precautionsAsthma/NSAID sensitivity, pregnancy, and ketorolac rules

By the end: you can list who should avoid NSAIDs, give the three third-trimester risks, state ketorolac's indication / not-recommended uses / contraindications, and name the patients in whom acetaminophen is the safe alternative.
Q1. Why are NSAIDs avoided in pregnancy, especially the third trimester?

Most NSAIDs are contraindicated throughout pregnancy. In the third trimester each risk maps onto a PG job:

1 · Hemorrhage

↑ maternal and fetal bleeding at delivery (antiplatelet effect).

2 · Prolonged labor

PGs drive uterine contraction → NSAID ↓ contractions → labor is prolonged/delayed.

3 · Ductus closes early

PGE₂ keeps the fetal ductus arteriosus open → NSAID → premature closure → fetal pulmonary hypertension.

  • Teratogenicity: high-dose aspirin is teratogenic in rats, but there is no epidemiological evidence that NSAIDs are teratogenic in humans.
  • Safe short-term alternative in pregnancy → acetaminophen.
CheckThe same PG effect is used therapeutically in neonates. How?
Indomethacin closes a patent ductus arteriosus in a premature infant: the "harm" in a fetus is the treatment after birth.
Step 1 HY
  • Current guidance: avoid NSAIDs from ~20 weeks on, because fetal renal PG loss → ↓ fetal urine → oligohydramnios.
  • PGE₁ (alprostadil) keeps a ductus open (e.g., transposition while awaiting surgery); indomethacin/ibuprofen close it.
  • Exception: low-dose aspirin is given in pregnancy to prevent preeclampsia in high-risk women.
Step 1 trap

"NSAIDs are classic human teratogens causing limb defects" is false. Their pregnancy danger is late (ductus, labor, bleeding, oligohydramnios), not organogenesis.

Q2. What are the rules for ketorolac?
CategoryKetorolac (Toradol)
Indicated forShort-term management of pain (analgesic indication only)
Not recommended forObstetric pre-operative medication · obstetric analgesia · routine use with other NSAIDs
Contraindicated inHypersensitivity to ketorolac · complete or partial syndrome of nasal polyps, angioedema, or bronchospastic reactivity to aspirin/NSAIDs
  • Logic: it is a very potent COX inhibitor → strongest PG-loss harms (bleeding, labor, kidney, GI), so keep it short and alone.
CheckWhy is ketorolac "not recommended" with other NSAIDs?
Additive COX inhibition → stacked GI bleeding, renal and antiplatelet toxicity with no extra benefit.
Q3. Who should get acetaminophen instead of aspirin/NSAIDs?

Acetaminophen is a safe short-term analgesic/antipyretic alternative to aspirin in:

Children

No Reye syndrome risk (aspirin + viral illness).

Pregnant women

No ductus closure, labor or bleeding effects.

Asthmatics

No leukotriene shunt; only option with NSAID hypersensitivity.

Bleeding disorders

No antiplatelet effect; also preferred after surgery with bleeding risk.

Renal deficiency

Doesn't remove the renal PGs that support GFR.

  • Its own catch: hepatotoxicity at high doses (max 4000 mg/day; LO 11).
Memory tool

"Kids, Bumps, Wheezes, Bleeds, Kidneys" → acetaminophen. (children, pregnant bump, asthma, bleeding disorders, renal disease)

Buzzwords
"3rd-trimester NSAID" → premature ductus closure"child + flu/varicella + aspirin" → Reye syndrome"nasal polyps + aspirin bronchospasm" → no ketorolac"short-term only, IV/IM NSAID" → ketorolac

A1A woman at 34 weeks' gestation has a tension headache and asks for ibuprofen. What do you recommend and what fetal risk are you avoiding?
Acetaminophen. Main fetal risk: premature closure of the ductus arteriosus (also prolonged labor and delivery bleeding).

LO 7Renal effects of NSAIDsWhy the kidney needs prostaglandins most when it is under-perfused

By the end: you can say where COX-1/COX-2 sit in the nephron, fill in what each renal PG does, list the five NSAID renal syndromes with mechanism and risk group, and spot the high-risk patient.
Q1. Where are COX-1 and COX-2 found in the kidney?
  • Both isoforms: renal arteries / glomerular vasculature and medullary interstitial cells.
  • COX-1: along the collecting ducts.
  • COX-2: macula densa / thick ascending limb (links to renin release).
  • Takeaway → because both isoforms matter, COX-2-selective drugs are not kidney-safe.
Q2. What do renal prostaglandins normally do?
Renal siteEicosanoidNormal actionNSAID removes it →
VasculaturePGI₂, PGD₂Vasodilation↓ renal blood flow
GlomerulusPGI₂, PGE₂ (TxA₂ opposes)Maintain GFR (TxA₂ ↓ GFR)Acute ↓ GFR
Collecting tubulePGE₂, PGF₂α↑ excretion of Na⁺, Cl⁻ and waterNa⁺/water retention → edema, ↑ BP
Medullary interstitial cellsPGE₂Vasodilation + natriuresis–diuresisRetention; papillary ischemia

PGs also counteract vasoconstrictors (angiotensin II, norepinephrine). With normal perfusion they contribute little, so healthy people rarely have renal problems on NSAIDs. The problem comes when the kidney is under-perfused:

CheckWhy does a healthy 25-year-old taking ibuprofen for a week usually have normal kidney function?
With normal perfusion, Ang II/NE are low and renal PGs contribute little to GFR, so removing them changes little.
Step 1 HY
  • NSAIDs constrict the afferent arteriole (↓ RPF, ↓ GFR, FF ~unchanged); ACEi/ARBs dilate the efferent (↓ GFR, ↑ RPF).
  • NSAID + ACEi/ARB + diuretic = "triple whammy" AKI.
  • Boards list for NSAID kidney injury: prerenal AKI, acute interstitial nephritis, renal papillary necrosis.
Step 1 trap

"Switch to celecoxib to protect the kidney" is wrong: COX-2 is expressed at the macula densa, TAL, vessels and medullary interstitium, so COX-2 inhibitors cause the same Na⁺ retention, hyperkalemia and ↓ GFR.

CheckWalk through why an NSAID raises potassium.
↓ renal PGs → ↓ renin → ↓ aldosterone (less K⁺ secretion) and ↓ Na⁺/fluid delivery to the distal tubule → ↓ K⁺ secretion → hyperkalemia.
Q4. Which patients are most likely to have GFR drop on an NSAID?

Anyone with a disease accompanied by poor renal perfusion:

"Low effective volume"

  • Congestive heart failure
  • Liver failure with ascites
  • Nephrotic syndrome
  • Diuretic use / other volume contraction

"Sick or fragile kidneys"

  • SLE
  • Chronic glomerulonephritis
  • Underlying renal insufficiency
  • Premature infants · elderly
  • Monitor daily weights: weight gain / edema is often the first sign of NSAID-induced renal retention.
Memory tool

Papillary necrosis causes, "SAAD papa": Sickle cell, Acute pyelonephritis, Analgesics (NSAIDs, phenacetin), Diabetes. Presents with gross hematuria + flank pain ± sloughed papillae in urine.

Buzzwords
"HF patient + NSAID → edema, ↑ creatinine" → loss of afferent PGs"rash, fever, eosinophiluria after NSAID" → AIN"chronic analgesic combo, hematuria, sloughed papilla" → papillary necrosis"fenoprofen" → nephrotic + interstitial nephritis"hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism" → NSAID hyperkalemia

ApplyLO 7 application questions

A1A 70-year-old with heart failure on furosemide starts high-dose ibuprofen for gout. In a week he gains 3 kg, his ankles swell and creatinine rises. Explain.
Loss of renal PGs in a low-perfusion state → afferent constriction (↓ GFR) + Na⁺/water retention; the NSAID also blunts the loop diuretic. Stop the NSAID.
A2A diabetic on spironolactone gets indomethacin for an acute gout flare. Which lab do you worry about most?
Potassium. Diabetes (often hyporeninemic) + K⁺-sparing diuretic + indomethacin → hyperkalemia; avoid indomethacin in high-risk patients.
A3A 55-year-old who has taken an aspirin + acetaminophen combination product daily for 15 years passes tissue in her urine with flank pain and hematuria. Diagnosis?
Renal papillary necrosis from chronic analgesic combination (direct toxicity).
A4Ten days after starting an NSAID, a patient has fever, rash, rising creatinine and white cell casts. Mechanism?
Acute interstitial nephritis: immune-mediated (↑ lymphocyte recruitment/activation), not hemodynamic. Stop the drug.

LO 8Drug–drug & drug–disease interactionsPharmacokinetic vs pharmacodynamic

By the end: you can explain what albumin displacement really does to free and total drug levels, fill in the PK/PD interaction table, and predict the result of combining an NSAID with lithium, warfarin or an antihypertensive.
Q1. NSAIDs are highly albumin-bound. What happens when one displaces another drug?
  1. ↑ Free drug = ↑ availability for metabolism and excretion.
  2. Most drugs reach a new steady state quickly, shortening the time the free level is actually elevated (free returns near baseline; total concentration falls).
  3. For some drugs, the ↑ free drug is toxic: those with a narrow therapeutic range (phenytoin, high-dose methotrexate, digoxin, oral hypoglycemics, warfarin).
CheckDoes an NSAID raise the levels of "all other drugs"?
No. It causes a transient rise in free drug; most drugs re-equilibrate fast with a lower total level. It matters clinically for narrow-therapeutic-range drugs.
Step 1 trap

After displacement the lab's total phenytoin level can look low while the free (active) level is normal or high. Don't "correct" a low total level by raising the dose; check a free level.

Step 1 HY
  • Lithium toxicity precipitants: NSAIDs, thiazides, ACEi, dehydration (all ↓ Li⁺ clearance).
  • Methotrexate + NSAID → ↓ renal MTX clearance → marrow suppression, mucositis.
  • Ibuprofen taken before aspirin occupies platelet COX-1 and can block aspirin's irreversible acetylation → blunts cardioprotection.

By the end: you can sort the 10 drugs to know by class, explain aspirin's dose-dependent (first → zero-order) kinetics, describe sulindac and acetaminophen metabolism, and give each drug's signature feature.
Q1. Which class does each drug to know belong to?

Q2. What happens to aspirin in the body, and why does its half-life depend on dose?
  • Step 1 (fast): aspirin (t½ ~20 min) is deacetylated by plasma and gastric esterases → salicylate (active, longer-lived).
  • Low dose → first-order kinetics: salicylate t½ 3–4 h
    • ~80% liver conjugation (glycine, glucuronate) · ~10% gentisic acid (liver) · ~10% renal excretion.
    • A constant fraction is eliminated per unit time.
  • High dose → zero-order kinetics: hepatic conjugation saturates
    • Renal excretion of salicylate rises to ~90%; t½ lengthens to 12–24 h.
    • A constant amount is eliminated per unit time → small dose increases cause disproportionate rises in level.
  • Aspirin t½ can range from 3–4 h to >12 h across the normal therapeutic range.
  • Different preparations (buffered, effervescent, sodium salt) → different absorption rates but no significant increase in analgesia.
CheckIs zero-order kinetics caused by the switch to renal excretion?
No. Zero-order comes from saturated hepatic conjugation. The rise in renal excretion (to ~90%) is a consequence of the liver being maxed out, which is exactly why alkalinizing urine helps in overdose. Other NSAIDs are cleared by liver metabolism.
Step 1 HY
  • Drugs with zero-order elimination at high doses: Phenytoin, Ethanol, Aspirin ("PEA": a pea is round like the zero in zero-order).
  • Aspirin acetylates COX irreversibly (serine); salicylate itself is a weak reversible inhibitor.
  • Low-dose aspirin ↓ uric acid excretion (can precipitate gout); high doses are uricosuric.
Step 1 trap

First-order = constant fraction, t½ constant. Zero-order = constant amount, t½ not constant (increases with dose). A question saying "doubling the dose more than doubled the level" is pointing at saturable (zero-order) kinetics.

Q3. What is special about diflunisal and the propionic acids?

Diflunisal salicylate

Synthetic difluorinated (stabilized) aspirin analog. Higher analgesic ceiling and longer t½ (~12 h) than aspirin. Analgesic + anti-inflammatory; works in dysmenorrhea → suitable for chronic pain.

Ibuprofen propionic

Short t½ ~4 h; analgesic ceiling higher than aspirin and acetaminophen; lower GI-bleed risk; first choice for fever, pain, inflammation, dysmenorrhea. Stop ~1 day before surgery.

Naproxen propionic

Higher ceiling, long t½ (~12 h) → twice-daily dosing; dysmenorrhea; prolongs bleeding time longer (reversible antiplatelet effect lasts with its long t½).

Ketoprofen propionic

Higher ceiling than aspirin (even than ibuprofen); t½ ≈ aspirin (3–4 h); but the highest GI-bleed risk of the drugs to know.

  • Propionic acids: all have anti-inflammatory indications; those with analgesic indications = ibuprofen, naproxen, ketoprofen, flurbiprofen; long t½ (>12 h) = naproxen, oxaprozin.
CheckFollowing the 5-half-lives rule, how long before surgery should naproxen be stopped vs ibuprofen?
Naproxen ~2.5–3 days (5 × ~12 h); ibuprofen ~1 day. Aspirin is different: irreversible, so stop it ≥7 days before (platelet lifespan 8–10 days).
Q4. What distinguishes the acetic acids: indomethacin, sulindac and ketorolac?

Indomethacin

Potent COX inhibitor but analgesic ceiling no higher than aspirin; anti-inflammatory indication only; t½ 4–8 h; closes PDA in neonates; avoid in hyperkalemia risk.

Sulindac (prodrug)

Sulfoxide (prodrug) ⇌ sulfide (active), reversible reduction in the liver; irreversible oxidation → sulfone (inactive). Ceiling = aspirin, slower onset, longer duration; anti-inflammatory only. Designed as GI-sparing, not proven.

Ketorolac

The exception: analgesic indication only (short term). Oral, IV and IM; greater water solubility and COX potency; t½ 4–6 h. IM ≈ morphine; oral ≈ ibuprofen.

  • Two drugs to know with active metabolites: aspirin → salicylate and sulindac → sulfide. (Acetaminophen is converted too, but to inactive conjugates and toxic NAPQI.)
  • Other GI-sparing ideas that never proved beneficial: prodrugs (sulindac, nabumetone) and COX-2 selective drugs.
CheckIndomethacin is a more potent COX inhibitor than aspirin. Is it a better analgesic?
No. COX potency does not predict analgesic ceiling; indomethacin's ceiling is no higher than aspirin's.
Step 1 HY
  • Indomethacin: acute gout flare, PDA closure, ankylosing spondylitis; classic cause of headache.
  • Ketorolac: the parenteral NSAID for post-op pain; limit to ≤5 days (GI bleed, AKI).
  • Celecoxib: sulfonamide (avoid in sulfa allergy); spares platelets/GI somewhat; ↑ MI/stroke.
Memory tool

Sulindac's S-ladder: SulfOxide = "Original" (prodrug) ⇌ SulfIde = "In action" (active) → SulfONe = "ONe-way out" (inactive, irreversible).

Q5. How do acetaminophen and celecoxib fit in?

Acetaminophen

  • Acts mainly centrally (slows nociceptor depolarization); analgesic + antipyretic.
  • Poor anti-inflammatory because: weak COX inhibitor · weak base · <20% protein bound · no accumulation at inflamed sites.
  • Analgesic ceiling ≈ aspirin (≈ aspirin 600 mg); t½ ~4 h.
  • Metabolism: mostly glucuronide (major) + sulfate (minor); a small fraction via CYP2E1/CYP1A2 → NAPQI, detoxified by glutathione (glutathione S-transferases).
  • Toxicity: dose-dependent hepatotoxicity (LO 11).

Celecoxib

  • ~30× more potent on COX-2 than COX-1 (rofecoxib/valdecoxib ~300×, both withdrawn).
  • Used for arthritis and menstrual pain; t½ ~12 h.
  • Analgesic efficacy similar to other NSAIDs; no advantage in chronic inflammatory disease; fewer clinically relevant adverse effects not proven.
  • Black box: stroke, MI, ↑ BP (↓ endothelial PGI₂, TxA₂ unopposed).

Solutions to NSAID limitations (GI, CV, renal): (1) choose the NSAID by patient risk (e.g., ibuprofen rather than ketoprofen for lower GI risk) · (2) acetaminophen when COX inhibition must be avoided · (3) combine with an opioid (dose-sparing).

Buzzwords
"prodrug, sulfide metabolite" → sulindac"IM NSAID ≈ morphine" → ketorolac"difluorinated salicylate, BID" → diflunisal"neonate PDA closure" → indomethacin"black box CV, COX-2" → celecoxib"weak base, <20% protein-bound" → acetaminophen

LO 10Efficacy compared with aspirinAnalgesic ceiling, half-life and anti-inflammatory power

By the end: you can name which drugs to know beat aspirin's analgesic ceiling, which only match it, which last longer, and explain why COX potency doesn't predict any of this.
Q1. What is an "analgesic ceiling," and what are the rules?
  • Analgesic ceiling = the maximum pain relief a drug can give; above it, more dose adds side effects, not analgesia.
  • Rules:
    • All NSAIDs have a ceiling, but not all ceilings are the same.
    • COX potency does NOT predict the analgesic ceiling (or anti-inflammatory efficacy).
    • Anti-inflammatory efficacy of all NSAIDs = aspirin; none beats aspirin here (not even indomethacin or sulindac).
    • Adding an opioid is additive/synergistic and "raises" the ceiling (dose-sparing).

Step 1 trap

"More potent COX inhibitor = better pain reliever" is the trap (indomethacin, sulindac). Potency only changes the dose needed, not the maximum effect (efficacy).

Step 1 HY

Potency = position on the dose–response curve (EC₅₀); efficacy = height of the plateau (Emax). The "analgesic ceiling" is Emax. Boards phrase it as "same efficacy, different potency."

Q2. How does the acid–base picture evolve?
  • Early: salicylate directly stimulates the medullary respiratory center → hyperventilation → respiratory alkalosis.
  • Then: uncoupled oxidative phosphorylation + ↑ fat/carb metabolism → ketones, lactate → anion-gap metabolic acidosis.
  • Acidemia makes more salicylate un-ionized → it crosses into the brain → worse CNS toxicity (vicious cycle).
CheckWhy is the patient febrile?
Uncoupled oxidative phosphorylation: energy from the proton gradient is released as heat instead of ATP ("paradoxical fever" from an antipyretic).
Step 1 HY
  • Classic ABG: mixed respiratory alkalosis + anion-gap metabolic acidosis (low PaCO₂, low HCO₃⁻, near-normal pH).
  • Anion-gap mnemonic MUDPILES / GOLDMARK: the "A/S" = aspirin/salicylates.
  • Other clues: tinnitus, hyperthermia, noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, low brain (CSF) glucose despite normal serum glucose.
Step 1 trap

Don't call early salicylate toxicity a "metabolic alkalosis." It's respiratory (↓ PaCO₂ from hyperventilation). And don't confuse it with methanol/ethylene glycol (AG acidosis but no tinnitus, visual loss or oxalate crystals instead).

Q3. How is salicylate poisoning treated, and why does bicarbonate work?

Alkalinize the urine with IV sodium bicarbonate → ↑ renal excretion of free salicylate.

H⁺ + S⁻ ⇌ HS

  • Salicylic acid is a weak acid. Only the un-ionized HS crosses tubular membranes back into blood.
  • ↑ urine pH (↓ H⁺) drives the equilibrium toward S⁻ (ionized) → it is trapped in tubular fluid → excreted (ion trapping).
  • Clearance rises steeply above urine pH ~7.5 (see plot).
  • Works for aspirin because at toxic doses ~90% leaves via the kidney; most other NSAIDs undergo liver metabolism, so alkalinization is ineffective for them.

Q4. How does acetaminophen damage the liver?
  • NAPQI (N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine) reacts with sulfhydryl groups → liver necrosis.
  • At therapeutic doses it is detoxified by hepatic glutathione S-transferases.
  • Higher doses → dose-dependent hepatotoxicity.

  • Generally safe at recommended doses for short use in healthy people; maximum daily dose = 4000 mg.
  • ~38% of US acute liver failure is due to acetaminophen overdosing.
  • Untreated mortality is high (40–80%); ~400 deaths and ~42,000 hospitalizations per year.

Q6. How do you decide to give N-acetylcysteine? (Rumack–Matthew concept)
  • Antidote: N-acetylcysteine (NAC) → supplies cysteine to regenerate glutathione (and boosts sulfation) so NAPQI is neutralized.
  • Most effective if started within ~8–10 h of ingestion; still give it late if there is hepatotoxicity.
  • Don't wait for symptoms or rising transaminases: by then necrosis is underway.
