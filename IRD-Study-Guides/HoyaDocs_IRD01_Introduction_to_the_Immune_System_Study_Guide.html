LO 1Innate vs adaptive immunity + barriersTwo speeds of defense, and the walls in front of both

By the end: you can fill in the innate vs adaptive table from memory, sort any immune cell or molecule into the right arm, explain why the adaptive response is slow but remembered, and match each chemical barrier to its site.
Q1. What are the three parts of the immune system, and how do they relate?
  1. Innate immune system → the "first responders": already on site, react immediately, same response to every invader.
  2. Specific (adaptive) immune system → trained specialists (T cells and B cells) that must be activated, then launch a response tailored to one invader.
  3. Complement system → a cascade of plasma proteins (C1–C9) that works alongside both.
  • They are not separate silos:
    • Innate dendritic cells switch on the adaptive system (LO 4).
    • Adaptive antibodies switch on complement (classical pathway, LO 3).
CheckName one way innate immunity "talks to" adaptive immunity, and one way adaptive talks back.
Innate → adaptive: dendritic cells carry antigen to CD4+ T cells. Adaptive → innate/complement: antibodies trigger the classical complement pathway and opsonize for phagocytes; Th cells recruit macrophages.

  • Main advantage of adaptive = immunologic memory. Main disadvantage = slow (days to weeks on first exposure).
    • Why slow? Specific lymphocytes are rare and sit in lymphoid tissue → must be found by antigen-carrying dendritic cells → activated → multiply before they can act.
  • Clonal expansion = an activated antigen-specific lymphocyte proliferates into many identical cells.
  • Contraction = once the pathogen is cleared, most of those cells die by apoptosis; a few survive as memory cells that can expand again on re-exposure.
    • You may see this die-off loosely called "clonal deletion"; the standard meaning of clonal deletion is removing self-reactive lymphocytes during development.
  • Humoral vs cellular (in-house definitions):
    • Humoral immunity = the soluble arm: antibodies and complement.
    • Cellular immunity = all the cells.
  • Pathogen size → which innate cell:
    • Small pathogens (bacteria) → macrophages, neutrophils (phagocytose them; neutrophils mainly attack extracellular bacteria).
    • Huge pathogens (worms, too big to eat) → eosinophils, basophils (release granule contents onto the surface).
CheckSort these: NK cell, plasma cell, eosinophil, complement, CD8+ T cell, dendritic cell, lysozyme.
Innate: NK cell, eosinophil, complement, dendritic cell, lysozyme. Adaptive: plasma cell, CD8+ T cell.
CheckAn innate cell vs a T cell: which one carries many receptor types, and which one has clonality?
The innate cell carries many receptor types (a broad set shared by all innate cells) and lacks clonality. The T cell carries one receptor specificity and has clonality.
Step 1 HY
  • Innate receptors are germline-encoded (e.g., TLRs); adaptive receptors are made by V(D)J recombination → huge diversity.
  • Boards phrase humoral vs cellular as B cells/antibody vs T-cell–mediated immunity.
  • Worms → eosinophils (with IgE/mast cells); bacteria → neutrophils.
Step 1 trap
  • NK cells are lymphocytes but innate (no antigen-specific receptor, no memory).
  • Dendritic cells are innate, even though their job is to activate adaptive cells.
  • "Clonal deletion" (development, self-reactive cells) ≠ contraction (post-infection die-off).
Memory tool

Innate = fire alarm sprinklers: always installed, go off fast, spray everything the same way. Adaptive = SWAT team: has to be called and briefed, but brings the exact tool, and remembers the building next time.

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A child gets a booster vaccine. Her antibody response is faster and much larger than after the first dose. What explains this?
Immunologic memory. Memory B and T cells left after the first clonal expansion respond rapidly; the innate system would respond identically both times.
A2A man on a proton-pump inhibitor for 3 years develops bacterial gastroenteritis. Which defense was weakened?
The chemical barrier of gastric HCl. Less acid → more swallowed bacteria survive into the gut, even though his immune cells are normal.
A3A traveler has an intestinal worm infection. Which innate cells are best suited, and why not neutrophils?
Eosinophils (and basophils). The worm is too big to phagocytose; eosinophils release granule contents onto its surface. Neutrophils/macrophages handle small pathogens they can engulf.
A4Predict: a week after a first infection, most pathogen-specific T cells disappear. Is the person losing protection?
No — this is contraction. Effector cells die off by apoptosis once the pathogen is gone; a small pool of memory cells remains for a faster response next time.

LO 2The innate response to infectionRecognize → eat → alarm → inflame → whole-body response

By the end: you can explain how a macrophage recognizes and destroys a microbe, list what inflammation does locally, match IL-1 / IL-6 / IL-8 / IL-12 / TNF-α to their effects, and explain where CRP comes from and why it is measured.
Q1. How does a macrophage know a microbe is there?
  • Macrophages carry pattern-recognition receptors, e.g., Toll-like receptors (TLRs).
  • These bind PAMPs (pathogen-associated molecular patterns) = conserved molecules found on microbes but not on our cells.
    • PAMPs = MAMPs (microbe-associated molecular patterns): two names, same thing → found on pathogens.
    • DAMPs (damage-associated molecular patterns) → released by damaged host cells/tissue.
  • Innate responses unfold in two waves:
    • Innate immediate immunity → macrophages (phagocytosis + cytokines), dendritic cells, inflammation.
    • Innate induced immunity (called in hours later) → neutrophils, other granulocytes, NK cells.
CheckA sterile crush injury (no microbes) still triggers inflammation. What did the macrophages detect?
DAMPs released by the damaged host cells — not PAMPs, since no pathogen is present.
Step 1 HY
  • Classic PAMPs: LPS (gram-negative) → TLR4/CD14; flagellin → TLR5; viral nucleic acids → endosomal TLRs.
  • TLR signaling → NF-κB → pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α).
Step 1 trap

PAMP and MAMP are synonyms, not two classes. The real contrast is PAMP (microbe) vs DAMP (damaged self).

Q3. What does cytokine-driven inflammation do at the site?
  1. Recruits more immune cells: macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils.
  2. Triggers local processes:
    • Vasodilation → redness, heat
    • ↑ Vascular permeability → swelling; plasma proteins and cells reach the tissue
    • Mast-cell activation + degranulation → release of more cytokines
    • Activation of the clotting system and the kinin system
  3. Stimulates macrophages to release more cytokines (interleukins) → the acute phase response (Q4).
CheckWhich two vascular changes explain a red, warm, swollen infected finger?
Vasodilation (red, warm) and increased vascular permeability (swollen).
Q4. What is the acute phase response, and what does each interleukin do?
  • Acute phase response = macrophage interleukins (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α) travel through the bloodstream → systemic inflammatory response (fever, liver acute phase proteins).
    • Local inflammation = at the site. Acute phase = whole-body effects of the same cytokines.

Opsonins and CRP

  • Opsonin = a molecule that coats a pathogen so macrophages and neutrophils can recognize and phagocytose it more easily.
    • Examples: acute phase proteins (CRP), complement fragments (C3b), antibodies (IgG).
  • C-reactive protein (CRP):
    • Made by the liver → in response to IL-6 → acts as an opsonin.
    • Measured in serum as a marker of inflammation; level tracks the severity of infection/inflammation.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A patient with pneumonia has fever, lethargy and no appetite. Which macrophage cytokine, acting on the brain, best explains this?
IL-1 (TNF-α similar). Acts on the hypothalamus → fever plus "sickness behavior."
A2Serum CRP rises sharply during a bacterial infection. Which cytokine stimulated which organ?
IL-6 → liver (hepatocytes). CRP is an acute phase protein and opsonin.

LO 3The complement systemThree jobs, three triggers

By the end: you can define complement, list its three functions, and say which pathway each trigger uses and whether it needs antibody.
Q1. What is complement and what does it do?
  • A set of plasma proteins (C1–C9) that, once triggered, activate each other in a cascade → helps both innate and specific immunity destroy pathogens.
  • Part of humoral immunity (soluble), alongside antibodies.
  • Three functions:
    1. Opsonization → coat pathogens so phagocytes recognize them
    2. Inflammation → recruit and activate immune cells
    3. Direct killing → attack and lyse pathogens
CheckName the three complement functions with one word each.
Opsonize, inflame, lyse.
Q2. How is complement switched on?
PathwayTriggerNeeds antibody?Arm
LectinDirectly by the pathogen (lectin binds microbial sugars, e.g., mannose)NoInnate, immediate
AlternativeDirectly by the pathogen surfaceNoInnate, immediate
ClassicalAntibody–antigen complexes made by the specific immune systemYesAdaptive → complement link
  • Early in a first-ever infection → only lectin and alternative work (no specific antibody yet).
  • Once plasma cells make antibody → classical pathway adds on.
    • Exception worth knowing: natural IgM, made before any exposure, binds many microbes → allows quick C1q binding and early classical activation.
CheckWhich pathway is the bridge from adaptive immunity to complement?
The classical pathway (antibody–antigen complexes bind C1).
Step 1 HY
  • C3b = opsonin; C3a, C5a = anaphylatoxins (C5a also neutrophil chemotaxis); C5b–C9 = membrane attack complex.
  • Classical: C1 binds IgM or IgG. Lectin: mannose-binding lectin. CRP can also activate the classical pathway.
  • Terminal (C5–C9) deficiency → recurrent Neisseria infections. Factor H is a key regulator that inactivates C3b.
Step 1 trap

"Classical" sounds like "oldest/first," but evolutionarily and in timing it is the one that needs antibody, so it comes later in a first infection. Lectin and alternative fire first.

Memory tool
  • "GM makes classic cars" → IgG and IgM trigger the classical pathway.
  • Functions: "O-I-L" → Opsonize, Inflame, Lyse.

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1In the first few hours of a never-before-seen infection, which complement pathways can be activated, and why not the third?
Lectin and alternative. The classical pathway needs antibody–antigen complexes, which the specific immune system has not yet made.
A2A bacterium is coated in C3b but not lysed. Which complement function is still working, and what does it accomplish?
Opsonization → macrophages/neutrophils recognize C3b and phagocytose the bacterium more efficiently.
A3Predict: a patient lacks mannose-binding lectin but has normal antibodies. Which pathway is lost, and which two remain?
Lectin lost. Alternative (direct) and classical (antibody) remain; early innate complement activation is reduced.

LO 4Dendritic cells, T cells, B cells and antibodiesHow the specific response is started and what each player does

By the end: you can explain where lymphocytes wait and why each is "specific," trace a dendritic cell to a CD4+ T cell, list the three jobs of helper T cells, the two killing mechanisms of CTLs, and the functions of antibodies.
Q1. Where do T and B cells wait, and what makes each one "specific"?
  • T and B cells are lymphocytes → can travel in lymph and blood but spend most time in lymph nodes and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT).
    • Analogy: lymphoid tissue = army barracks full of soldiers, each trained for one specific enemy, waiting for word.
  • Antigen = a molecule (often from a pathogen) that a receptor recognizes.
  • Each T cell has TCRs, and each B cell has surface antibody (BCR), specific for one single antigen → the body holds millions of different T and B cells.
  • Only the few cells whose receptor fits a given antigen are activated → clonal expansion.
  • T cells see only peptide on MHC; B cells bind native antigen directly (no MHC needed).

Q4. How do cytotoxic T cells kill?
  • The CTL binds antigen displayed on the infected cell's MHC (HLA) class I, then kills by one of two routes:

Granule exocytosis

  • Sprays enzymes onto the target → cell lysis/death.
  • Granules hold perforin (pores) + granzymes (activate caspases).

Fas activation

  • CTL's FasL engages Fas on the target → apoptosis.
  • Works even without granules.
CheckCTLs lacking cytotoxic granules still make their virus-infected targets undergo apoptosis. Which pathway is left?
The Fas pathway (FasL on the CTL → Fas on the target).
Step 1 HY
  • NK cells use the same perforin/granzyme machinery (innate; activated by IL-12).
  • Fas–FasL also deletes self-reactive lymphocytes; Fas mutation → autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome (ALPS).
Buzzwords
"Perforin and granzyme" → CTL or NK killing"Fas ligand" → extrinsic apoptosis"CD3+" → T cell"Innate–adaptive link" → dendritic cell → CD4+
Q5. How do antibodies protect?
  • Antibodies = Y-shaped proteins made by plasma cells; they float in the blood and bind matching antigen.
    • Variable region (tips of the Y, Fab) → binds one specific antigen.
    • Constant ("fixed") region (stem, Fc) → recognized by immune cells (and C1).
  • Antibody functions to know cold: neutralization (toxins and attachment), opsonization, agglutination, complement activation.
CheckAntibodies link several bacteria into large slow-moving clumps. Which function is this, and what structural feature allows it?
Agglutination; each antibody has two antigen-binding arms, so it can bridge two microbes.
Step 1 HY
  • Neutralizing antibody is how toxoid vaccines (tetanus, diphtheria) protect.
  • IgG = best opsonin (Fc read by phagocyte Fc receptors); IgM (pentamer) = best agglutinator and complement activator.
Memory tool

"NOAC" → Neutralize, Opsonize, Agglutinate, Complement.

ApplyLO 4 application questions

A1A patient with untreated HIV has a very low CD4+ count. Which arms of immunity are impaired?
Everything needing T help: CD8+ cytotoxic responses, B-cell differentiation into plasma cells, and helper-driven macrophage recruitment. CD4+ Th cells are the coordinators.
A2Predict: dendritic cells cannot migrate out of infected tissue. What happens to the first-ever adaptive response?
It fails to start (or is badly delayed). Antigen never reaches naive CD4+ T cells waiting in the lymph node; innate defenses still work.
A3An antibody binds diphtheria toxin so it can no longer attach to host cells. Which function?
Neutralization.
A4Does a B cell need its antigen presented on MHC to recognize it?
No. The BCR binds native antigen directly; the B cell then presents peptide on MHC II to get help from a matching Th cell.

1A 67-year-old man has taken omeprazole daily for 4 years for reflux. He develops fever and watery diarrhea, and stool testing confirms Salmonella enteritis. His white cell count and immunoglobulin levels are normal. Loss of which defense most likely predisposed him?

A. Gastric HCl. PPIs raise gastric pH, so swallowed bacteria survive into the intestine — a chemical-barrier failure. Lysozyme (B) is in tears/sweat and targets peptidoglycan; cilia (C) protect the airways; complement (D) and T cells (E) are intact given normal labs.

2An enzyme present in tears and sweat kills Staphylococcus epidermidis far more effectively than Escherichia coli. Which bacterial structure does this enzyme attack?

B. Peptidoglycan. Lysozyme cleaves peptidoglycan; gram-positives have a thick, exposed layer, while gram-negatives shield theirs under an outer membrane. LPS (A) and porins (D) are gram-negative outer-membrane features; flagellin (C) is a TLR5 ligand; capsules (E) resist phagocytosis.

3A 34-year-old woman with lobar pneumonia has a temperature of 39.4 °C, profound fatigue and no appetite. Alveolar macrophages have released a cytokine that acts on the hypothalamus to produce these symptoms. Which cytokine is most responsible?

C. IL-1 (with TNF-α) is the endogenous pyrogen → fever, lethargy, ↓ appetite. IL-2 (A) drives T-cell growth; IL-8 (B) recruits neutrophils; IL-12 (D) activates NK cells and Th1; IL-4 (E) drives IgE class switching.

4A man with a dental abscess has a serum C-reactive protein that is 20 times normal. Which cytokine acting on which cell type most directly caused this rise?

D. IL-6 → hepatocytes. CRP is an acute phase protein made by the liver in response to IL-6; it acts as an opsonin and tracks inflammation severity. The other pairs are real cytokine actions (A, B, C) but do not make CRP; plasma cells (E) make antibodies, not CRP.

5In a mouse model, tissue macrophages are engineered so they cannot secrete one chemokine. After bacterial skin inoculation, the lesion contains abundant macrophages but very few neutrophils. Which mediator is missing?

E. IL-8 (CXCL8) recruits and activates neutrophils. IL-1 (A) causes fever; IL-6 (B) drives acute phase proteins; IL-12 (C) activates NK cells; CRP (D) is an opsonin, not a chemokine.

6Two hours after a child is first exposed to a bacterium she has never encountered, complement fragments are already coating the organism. She has no antibodies against it. Which pathways could account for this?

A. Lectin (mannose-binding lectin on microbial sugars) and alternative (direct surface activation) are triggered by the pathogen itself. The classical pathway (B, C) needs antibody–antigen complexes; the alternative pathway does not use IgG (D); complement is innate and needs no sensitization (E).

7A researcher blocks one complement fragment in serum. Bacteria in the treated serum are still lysed normally, but neutrophils engulf them far less efficiently. Which fragment was blocked?

B. C3b is the main complement opsonin. C3a/C5a (A, C) are anaphylatoxins (inflammation); C5b–C9 (D) forms the MAC — lysis is intact, so it is not blocked; C1q (E) starts the classical pathway, and blocking it would also reduce lysis.

8A 29-year-old man with untreated HIV infection has a CD4+ count of 90/mm³. After a pneumococcal vaccine, he makes almost no new class-switched antibody, although his B-cell count is normal. Which step is most directly impaired?

C. B cells need cytokine help from CD4+ Th cells to proliferate and become antibody-secreting plasma cells. TLR recognition (A), lectin complement (B) and neutrophils (E) are innate and CD4-independent; MHC I display (D) is used by CD8+ cells and is not CD4-dependent.

9Cytotoxic T cells from a knockout mouse lack perforin. When mixed with virus-infected target cells, the targets still show caspase activation, DNA fragmentation and apoptotic bodies. Which interaction explains the remaining killing?

D. Fas–FasL triggers apoptosis independently of granules. Fc binding (A) is how phagocytes/NK cells read antibody; the MAC (B) is complement; CTLs are CD8+ and use MHC I, not CD4/MHC II (C); IL-8 (E) is a neutrophil chemokine.

10A nurse receives a hepatitis B booster. Within days her anti-HBs titer rises far higher and faster than after her first dose years earlier. Which cells are mainly responsible for the difference?

E. Memory B cells persist after the first response and rapidly expand on re-exposure → faster, larger antibody response. Macrophages, neutrophils, NK cells and dendritic cells (A–D) are innate and have no antigen-specific memory.

11An unvaccinated child with diphtheria is given antitoxin, a preparation of antibodies that bind the circulating toxin and stop it from entering cells. This therapy works mainly through which antibody function?

A. Neutralization — antibody coats the toxin and blocks its binding to host cells. Agglutination (B) clumps whole microbes; opsonization (C) and complement lysis (D) act on organisms, not a soluble toxin; CTLs (E) are not activated by antibody.

12During a first influenza infection, a cell in the lung epithelium takes up viral protein, migrates through lymphatic vessels to a mediastinal lymph node and displays viral peptide on MHC class II to naive CD4+ T cells. Which cell is this?

B. Dendritic cell — the antigen carrier that primes naive T cells and links innate to adaptive immunity. Neutrophils (A) phagocytose and die locally; plasma cells (C) secrete antibody; NK cells (D) kill; mast cells (E) degranulate in tissue.

13Flow cytometry of a lymph node identifies a cell population that is CD3+ and CD8+. When activated, what is the main function of these cells?

C. CD3 = T cell; CD8 = cytotoxic T cell, which recognizes antigen on MHC I and kills via granules or Fas. Antibody (A) comes from plasma cells; MHC II presentation (B) is by APCs; phagocytosis (D) by macrophages/neutrophils; IL-6 (E) mainly from macrophages.

14A boy has a cut on his forearm that becomes red, warm and swollen within an hour, before any neutrophils have arrived. Which process best explains the swelling?

D. Local inflammation → vasodilation (red, warm) + ↑ vascular permeability (swelling). Clonal expansion (A) and CTL killing (E) take days; acute phase proteins (B) are systemic; the MAC (C) lyses microbes but does not cause edema.
