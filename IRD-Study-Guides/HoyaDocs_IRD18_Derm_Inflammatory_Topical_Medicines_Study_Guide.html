LO 1PsoriasisWhat it looks like, what drives it, and how we climb the treatment ladder

By the end: you can name the six clinical types, draw the TNF-α / IL-23 / IL-17 loop, describe the biopsy, list triggers and comorbidities, and match each therapy (topical, light, oral, biologic) to its key caveat.
Q1. What is psoriasis, and who gets it?
  • Chronic inflammatory disease of the skin and/or joints.
    • Keratinocytes turn over ~10× faster than normal → cells reach the surface before they mature → thick plaques with silvery scale.
    • Intense flares → pain and itch; inflammation can involve the joints (psoriatic arthritis).
  • Epidemiology
    • ~2.5% of the U.S. population; ~150,000 new cases/year.
    • 1:1 M:F.
    • Reported as less common in African American and Native American patients, but underdiagnosed in darker skin (redness is harder to see → plaques look violaceous, gray or hyperpigmented).
  • A systemic disease: the same inflammation is linked to
    • diabetes, obesity, metabolic syndrome · cardiovascular disease · non-alcoholic fatty liver disease · psoriatic arthritis · depression/psychological disease.
    • → care involves cardiology, endocrinology, hepatology, rheumatology, primary care and psychiatry, not only dermatology.
CheckWhy does a patient with psoriasis need their lipids, glucose and blood pressure checked?
Psoriasis is systemic inflammation: it travels with metabolic syndrome, diabetes and cardiovascular disease, so screen for those risk factors.
Q2. What immune loop keeps a plaque going?
  • You do not need the whole pathway, but know the "known players":
    • Lymphocytes making TNF-α, IL-17 and IL-12/IL-23
    • Dendritic cells and mast cells
  • Genotype + environmental triggers (stress, microbes, drugs, trauma, smoking) activate keratinocytes → the loop below starts and feeds itself.
CheckWhich cell recruited by IL-17 shows up inside the epidermis on biopsy?
Neutrophils, forming collections in the stratum corneum (Munro microabscesses).
Step 1 HY
  • Psoriasis is Th17-driven: DC IL-23 maintains Th17 → IL-17 → keratinocyte proliferation + neutrophil recruitment.
  • Psoriasis is an independent cardiovascular risk factor; also linked to uveitis.
Memory tool

"23 makes 17 make plaques." IL-23 (from DCs) keeps Th17 alive → IL-17 drives the plaque. TNF-α fans the whole fire.

Q3. What are the clinical types, and how do I tell them apart?

Plaque (vulgaris) most common

Red plaques with thick white/silver scale; extensor elbows, knees, scalp, lower back.

Guttate (eruptive)

Many small 0.5–1.5 cm "drop-like" lesions on trunk/upper limbs; children and young adults; acute onset; 50–85% had a preceding strep infection.

Erythrodermic

Full-body redness ± typical plaques. One of many causes of erythroderma (also drug reactions, AD, cutaneous T-cell lymphoma) → fluid/protein loss, temperature instability.

Pustular

Sterile pustules on an erythematous base; diffuse disease → systemic symptoms. Provoked by withdrawal of systemic steroids, pregnancy, certain medications, infections.

Nail

Fingernails ~50%, toenails ~35%. Pits (defective nail-plate keratinization), oil spots (yellow-brown macules = nail-bed psoriasis), onychodystrophy. → ↑ psoriatic arthritis.

Psoriatic arthritis

The sixth major type. Screen anyone with nail disease for joint pain and stiffness.

CheckA man's plaque psoriasis turns into fever and sheets of sterile pustules a few days after a prednisone burst ends. What happened?
Pustular psoriasis provoked by systemic steroid withdrawal. This rebound is why systemic steroids are generally avoided in psoriasis.
Step 1 HY
  • Auspitz sign: scraping scale → pinpoint bleeding (dilated capillaries in elongated dermal papillae).
  • Koebner phenomenon: new plaques at sites of trauma.
  • Generalized pustular psoriasis of von Zumbusch: fever, leukocytosis, hypocalcemia; can be fatal.
  • PsA: asymmetric DIP disease, dactylitis ("sausage digits"), pencil-in-cup X-ray; seronegative spondyloarthropathy (HLA-B27).
Step 1 trap

Location splits the two big "itchy plaque" diseases: psoriasis = extensor (elbows, knees); atopic dermatitis in children/adults = flexural (antecubital, popliteal). Infant AD is the exception: cheeks and extensors.

Buzzwords
"silvery scale on extensor elbows/knees" → plaque psoriasis"drop-like papules after sore throat" → guttate"nail pitting, oil spots" → nail psoriasis → PsA risk"sterile pustules after steroid taper" → pustular
Q4. What does a psoriatic plaque look like under the microscope?
  • Acanthosis = epidermal thickening (↑ stratum spinosum).
  • Regularly elongated rete ridges / dermal papillae (both lengthen, like interlocking fingers).
  • Neutrophils in the epidermis and stratum corneum (Munro microabscesses).
  • Parakeratosis = nuclei retained in the stratum corneum (cells rushed up before maturing) → thick silvery scale.
  • Thinned / lost granular layer (normal skin has a stratum granulosum and anucleate corneum).

CheckName four histologic features you should list for a psoriasis biopsy.
Acanthosis, elongated dermal papillae/rete ridges, neutrophils in the epidermis, and nuclei in the stratum corneum (parakeratosis).
Step 1 HY

Boards phrase it as: ↑ stratum spinosum, ↓ stratum granulosum, parakeratosis, Munro microabscesses, elongated rete ridges.

Memory tool

Psoriasis = too much epidermis (acanthosis, parakeratosis). Eczema = water in the epidermis (spongiosis). Pemphigus = keratinocytes falling apart (acantholysis).

CheckWhy can't cyclosporine be the long-term plan for psoriasis?
With time it causes hypertension and renal disease. It is a fast short-term rescue; maintenance moves to methotrexate or a biologic.
Step 1 HY
  • Before any anti-TNF: screen for latent TB (TNF holds granulomas together) and hepatitis B. Anti-TNF can also cause drug-induced lupus.
  • Methotrexate (DHFR inhibitor) → myelosuppression, hepatotoxicity, pulmonary fibrosis, teratogenic. Cyclosporine → nephrotoxicity, HTN, hyperkalemia, gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism.

Step 1 trap

Acitretin is a teratogen that lingers for years: patients must not donate blood during or for years after therapy (it could reach a pregnant recipient). It is not an immunosuppressant, so it is not the drug to stop for infection risk.

Memory tool

-cept = receptor fusion protein (etanercept "intercepts" TNF). -kizumab = interleukin antibody. IL-17: "secure the ixes" (secukinumab, ixekizumab). IL-23: "gus & risk till 23".

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A 15-year-old develops dozens of 1 cm scaly pink papules over the trunk two weeks after a sore throat. Diagnosis and likely trigger?
Guttate psoriasis after group A streptococcal pharyngitis. Small drop-like lesions, acute onset, young patient.
A2A patient with psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis starts ustekinumab. Which cytokines are blocked?
IL-12 and IL-23 (shared p40 subunit). Blocking IL-23 → fewer Th17 cells → ↓ IL-17.
A3A man on an oral psoriasis drug is told never to donate blood during therapy and for years afterward. Which drug?
Acitretin: an oral retinoid that is teratogenic and persists in the body.
A4A patient has pitted fingernails with yellow-brown "oil spots." What should you ask about next?
Joint pain and stiffness: nail psoriasis carries a higher incidence of psoriatic arthritis.

LO 2Atopic dermatitis"The itch that rashes": age patterns, cause, a first plan for a child, and steroid safety

By the end: you can describe AD at each age, name the four contributing factors, write a starter plan for a child's flare, and list the side effects of topical steroids and the body sites where potent ones are off-limits.
Q1. What is atopic dermatitis, and why is it "the itch that rashes"?
  • Chronic, pruritic, inflammatory skin disease with a wide range of severity; periods of remission and flare.
  • Most common skin disorder in developed countries: up to 20% of children, 1–3% of adults.
  • Usually starts before age 5 and often clears by adolescence.
  • Itch (pruritus) comes first → scratching makes the rash → the itch–scratch cycle worsens the disease.

CheckWhat is the primary symptom of AD?
Pruritus (itch). The rash follows the scratching.
Q2. Why does it happen?
  • Multifactorial and not fully understood. Four contributing factors:
    • Genetics
    • Skin barrier dysfunction (the central problem → water loss = xerosis, allergens/microbes get in)
    • Inappropriate immune response
    • Environment
  • Part of the atopic triad: atopic dermatitis + asthma + allergic rhinitis (genetically linked allergic conditions). Children with AD often develop the others (the "atopic march").
CheckA child has AD. Which two other conditions should you ask about?
Asthma and allergic rhinitis (the atopic triad).
Step 1 HY
  • Loss-of-function filaggrin (FLG) mutations → barrier defect; associated with ichthyosis vulgaris and food allergy.
  • Inflammation is type 2 / Th2 (IL-4, IL-13) with ↑ serum IgE; atopy = tendency to type I (IgE) hypersensitivity.
Step 1 trap

Calling AD simply "a type I hypersensitivity reaction" misses the point: the core defect is the barrier. IgE and Th2 are part of the story, but barrier repair (emollients) is step one of treatment.

Q3. How does AD look, and how does the distribution change with age?
  • Lesions start as erythematous papules → coalesce into erythematous plaques that may weep, crust or scale.
  • Xerosis (dry skin) at every stage.
AgeWhere / what
InfantsEczematous plaques on cheeks, forehead, scalp and extensor surfaces
Children & adolescentsLichenified (thickened) plaques in flexures: neck, antecubital fossae, wrists, popliteal fossae (behind the knees), ankles
AdultsFlexural lichenification + hands, wrists, ankles, feet, face (especially forehead and around the eyes)
All agesXerosis
  • Eczema ≠ AD. Eczema is a nonspecific term for inflammatory rashes with pruritus, erythema and scale clinically and spongiosis (intercellular edema in the epidermis) on pathology. AD is one specific type of eczematous dermatitis.
CheckWhere would you expect AD on a 6-month-old vs an 8-year-old?
Infant: cheeks, forehead, scalp, extensors. School-age child: flexures (antecubital, popliteal, wrists, neck, ankles) with lichenification.
Memory tool

"Crawlers hit extensors; kids bend at flexors." Babies crawl on knees/elbows and rub cheeks; older kids get it in the creases. All AD is eczema, but not all eczema is AD (squares and rectangles).

Step 1 HY

Allergic contact dermatitis (nickel, poison ivy, neomycin) is another eczema: a type IV hypersensitivity, shaped like the contact.

Buzzwords
"the itch that rashes" → atopic dermatitis"lichenification" → chronic scratching/rubbing"spongiosis" → eczema"asthma + hay fever + eczema" → atopic triad
Q4. How do I treat a child with atopic dermatitis?
  1. Emollients + gentle skin care for everyone (barrier repair: the core defect).
  2. Topical corticosteroids for acute inflammation (in a child: low–medium potency, short course).
  3. Maintenance with steroid-sparing topicals: calcineurin inhibitors, PDE-4 inhibitor, aryl hydrocarbon receptor agonist.
  4. Secondary skin infection is common → treat with topical or systemic antibiotics.
  5. nbUVB.
  6. Moderate–severe: biologics (dupilumab, tralokinumab) or JAK inhibitors (upadacitinib, abrocitinib oral; ruxolitinib topical).
CheckWhat two things make up the initial plan for a child's AD flare?
Emollients + gentle skin care and a topical steroid for the acute inflammation.
Step 1 HY
  • Dupilumab blocks IL-4 receptor α → inhibits IL-4 and IL-13 signaling; tralokinumab neutralizes IL-13.
  • Usual colonizer/infector is S. aureus (honey crusts = impetiginization).
  • Clustered punched-out erosions in AD skin → eczema herpeticum (HSV) → acyclovir.
Step 1 trap

Psoriasis targets Th17 (IL-17/IL-23); AD targets Th2 (IL-4/IL-13). Matching dupilumab to psoriasis or secukinumab to AD is a classic wrong answer.

Q5. What harms do topical steroids cause, and where should potent ones be avoided?
  • Generally safe, but watch for:
    • Long-term use → atrophy, neovascularization (telangiectasia), steroid acne.
    • Tachyphylaxis → loss of efficacy from "resistance" to the steroid (controversial).
    • Systemic absorption → adrenal (HPA) suppression when applied to a large surface area, especially in small children (large surface-area-to-weight ratio).
  • Avoid potent steroids where atrophy risk is high: face, skin folds, groin (and occluded skin, e.g., under a diaper).
CheckList the four main topical steroid side effects.
Atrophy, neovascularization, steroid acne, adrenal suppression (plus tachyphylaxis).
Step 1 HY

Chronic topical steroids → dermal atrophy (collagen loss), striae, telangiectasias, easy bruising, perioral dermatitis. HPA suppression → adrenal insufficiency on withdrawal.

Memory tool

"FFG: Face, Folds, Groin" = thin skin + natural occlusion = no potent steroid. "Thin skin, thin steroid."

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A 4-year-old has an itchy flare in the antecubital and popliteal fossae. Initial plan?
Daily emollients + gentle skin care, plus a low–medium potency topical steroid for a short course; then steroid-sparing maintenance (e.g., calcineurin inhibitor) and watch for infection.
A2Parents have applied clobetasol over most of their 9-month-old's body, including under the diaper, for 2 months. What systemic risk?
Adrenal (HPA) suppression: super-potent steroid + large surface area in an infant + occlusion by the diaper → high systemic absorption. Also atrophy in the groin.
A3A child with AD develops weeping, honey-crusted erosions within his patches. Complication and management?
Secondary bacterial infection (usually S. aureus) → add topical or systemic antibiotics to AD care.
A4An adult asks to use clobetasol on eyelid eczema. Your advice?
No: the face is a high-atrophy site. Use a low-potency steroid (hydrocortisone) briefly or a calcineurin inhibitor.

LO 3AcneFour pathogenic steps, three morphologies, one treatment ladder

By the end: you can list the four factors in order, classify an acne photo, say which factor each drug hits, and pick the next rung when a regimen fails.
Q1. Where does acne occur, and what is the first lesion?
  • Areas rich in sebaceous glands: face, neck, upper trunk, arms.
  • Starts with a clogged pilosebaceous unit = comedone.
    • Open comedone = blackhead (pore open; plug contents oxidize and darken, not dirt).
    • Closed comedone = whitehead (covered by skin; small white/flesh papule).
Q2. What four factors produce acne, and which drug hits which?
  1. ↑ Sebum production, influenced by androgens.
  2. Keratin + sebum plug the pore and accumulate → hyperkeratosis (the comedone).
  3. Cutibacterium acnes proliferates in the sebaceous follicle → stimulates pro-inflammatory cytokines.
  4. Inflammatory response.
CheckA bodybuilder on anabolic steroids gets severe chest and back acne. Which factor rose first?
Sebum production: exogenous androgens enlarge and stimulate sebaceous glands.
Memory tool

S-K-C-I: Sebum → Keratin plug → C. acnes → Inflammation. Every acne drug hits at least one letter.

Step 1 HY

C. acnes was formerly Propionibacterium acnes. Acne flares at puberty because adrenarche (adrenal androgens) enlarges sebaceous glands. Anabolic steroid misuse also → testicular atrophy, erythrocytosis, gynecomastia.

Q3. How is acne classified?

Comedonal

Open and/or closed comedones, few inflamed lesions.

Inflammatory

Papules and pustules.

Nodulocystic

Nodules and cysts → highest scarring risk.

  • Equally important to describe severity, scarring and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation: these drive how aggressive therapy should be.
CheckWhich acne category carries the highest risk of permanent scars?
Nodulocystic (and severe inflammatory) acne.

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1A 15-year-old has many blackheads and whiteheads on the nose and forehead with few papules. Best initial therapy?
Topical retinoid (tretinoin) or benzoyl peroxide: mild comedonal acne.
A2A teen dabs tretinoin on single pimples each morning and quits after 3 weeks. What do you fix?
Apply a thin layer to all acne-prone skin (it prevents new comedones), at bedtime, with a mild cleanser, start 2–3×/week and build up, and wait 2–3 months.

A5A college student on long-term acne pills develops dizziness and blue-gray skin pigmentation. Drug?
Minocycline. (Doxycycline's classic AEs are GI upset and photosensitivity.)

LO 4Drug rashesImmediate vs delayed, exanthem vs SJS/TEN, and how to build a drug timeline

By the end: you can classify a drug reaction by timing, separate a harmless morbilliform eruption from SJS/TEN at the bedside, stage SJS/TEN by body surface area, and use a Day-0 timeline to name the culprit.
Q1. What general rules apply to every suspected drug rash?
  • Always consider medications as the cause of a skin reaction.
  • Most drug reactions are inflammatory, generalized and symmetric.
  • Diagnosis rests on morphology and timing; a skin biopsy can help.
  • Document the drug and a description of the reaction in the chart.
FeatureImmediateDelayed
Timing<1 hour after the last dose>1 hour; typically days to weeks after starting (exanthem ~7–10 d; SJS/TEN within 8 wk)
ExamplesUrticaria, angioedema, anaphylaxisExanthematous eruption; systemic reactions (SJS, TEN)

CheckHives 20 minutes after an IV dose: immediate or delayed?
Immediate (<1 h): urticaria from IgE-triggered mast-cell degranulation.
Q2. How do I build a drug timeline?
  • Timing is the single most important clue.
  • Set rash onset = Day 0, then plot every drug's start and stop backwards and forwards.
  • For an exanthematous eruption the culprit was usually started 7–10 days before the rash (much sooner on re-exposure).
CheckIn the timeline above, why are the opioids an unlikely cause?
Started only 3 days before the rash: too soon for a first exposure (expected ~7–10 d).
Step 1 trap

The drug a patient has taken for years is rarely the culprit for a new exanthem. Look for what was started 1–2 weeks before Day 0. Exception: re-exposure to a drug that caused a prior reaction → the rash comes much faster.

Memory tool

"Rash = Day 0, look back a week and a half." Draw it: one horizontal bar per drug, a red vertical line at the rash.

Q3. What does an exanthematous (morbilliform) drug eruption look like?
  • Most common cutaneous drug eruption (~90%); limited to the skin.
  • Lesions start on the trunk and spread centrifugally, symmetrically to the extremities; pruritus may be present.
  • Appears >2 days after starting the drug, mainly days 7–10; may persist a few days after stopping.
  • Common culprits: antibiotics, anticonvulsants, ibuprofen, allopurinol, antiretrovirals.
  • Course: resolves in days to a week after stopping, without sequelae (scaling/desquamation can occur). May continue the drug if the rash is not severe and no substitute exists.
  • Treatment: topical steroids, oral antihistamines, reassurance.
CheckName three red flags that would move you from "simple exanthem" to "possible SJS/TEN."
Any of: skin pain, dusky/purpuric lesions or blisters, mucosal erosions, positive Nikolsky, fever/prodrome.
Step 1 HY

Classic vignette: amoxicillin/ampicillin given for "strep throat" in a patient who actually has EBV mononucleosis → widespread morbilliform rash.

Q4. What are SJS and TEN, and how do I recognize them?
  • Acute, life-threatening mucocutaneous reactions: extensive necrosis and detachment of the epidermis and mucosal surfaces. Same process, differing by body surface area (BSA) detached.
  • Dermatologic emergencies. Mortality 5–12% (SJS), >20% (TEN). Worse with older age, comorbidities, larger BSA.

Q5. What complications follow, and how is SJS/TEN managed?

Complications

  • Corneal damage → consult ophthalmology
  • Oral: sicca, pain
  • GU: adhesions, urethral/introital erosions
  • Pulmonary: bronchitis, bronchiectasis
  • Fluid/electrolyte and nutrition problems
  • Secondary infection (bacteremia, sepsis)

Management

  • Early recognition + withdraw the offending drug(s); if in doubt, stop all non-life-sustaining drugs
  • Supportive care in a burn unit
  • Consult dermatology at the first concern
  • Multidisciplinary: ophthalmology, gynecology, urology
  • Systemic medications may be used to stabilize the skin faster
CheckWhy is a burn unit the right place for TEN?
Loss of the epidermis behaves like a large burn: fluid and protein loss, temperature instability and infection/sepsis.
Step 1 HY

Systemic agents used in practice vary by center: cyclosporine, TNF-α inhibitors (etanercept), IVIG, corticosteroids. The answer that is always right: stop the drug + supportive (burn) care.

LO 5Immunobullous disordersPemphigus vulgaris vs bullous pemphigoid, and how to work up a blistering rash

By the end: you can predict blister type, Nikolsky, mucosal involvement, antigen, level of split and immunofluorescence pattern for each disease, and order the right tests for a new blistering rash.
Q1. What holds the epidermis together, and where can it split?
  • Desmosomes = spot welds between keratinocytes (cadherins: desmoglein 1 and 3).
  • Hemidesmosomes = anchors from basal cells to the basement membrane (BPAG1 = BP230, BPAG2 = BP180).
  • The level of the split decides whether a blister is flaccid or tense.
CheckWhich split gives a blister whose roof is the entire epidermis?
Subepidermal (bullous pemphigoid): full-thickness roof → tense, hard to rupture.
Memory tool

FLACCID = fragile = INTRAepidermal (pemphigus). TENSE = tough = SUBepidermal (pemphigoid). PemphigUS = between US (cells); pemphigOID = bOTTOM (below the epidermis). BP antibodies are "bullow" the epidermis.

Q2. What are the features of pemphigus vulgaris?
  • Clinical: average onset 50–60 y; multiple flaccid bullae and erosions (intact bullae often not seen); Nikolsky positive; mucosa often involved: oral mucosa, lips, palate (also conjunctiva, esophagus, anogenital). Oral erosions often come first.
  • Pathogenesis: autoimmune IgG against the keratinocyte cell surface → antigens desmoglein 1 & 3 (desmosome, cell-to-cell adhesion) → acantholysis.
  • Histology: intraepidermal split just above the basal layer; basal cells stay stuck to the basement membrane ("row of tombstones").
  • Direct IF: IgG between keratinocytes (intercellular, net-like/"chicken-wire").
  • Treatment: untreated → can be fatal (10–20% mortality). High-dose systemic glucocorticoids to start → transition to an alternate immunosuppressive ("steroid-sparing agent": lower steroid dose and side effects, ↑ chance of remission). Rituximab (anti-CD20 → removes pathogenic B cells; durable remissions) is now first-line for moderate and severe disease.
CheckWhy are intact blisters often missing in pemphigus?
The split is within the epidermis, so the roof is thin and flaccid: it ruptures easily, leaving erosions.
Step 1 HY
  • PV and BP are type II hypersensitivity (antibody to a fixed tissue antigen).
  • Dsg 3 dominates in mucosa → mucosal-predominant disease; adding anti-Dsg 1 → skin disease. Pemphigus foliaceus = anti-Dsg 1 only → superficial crusts, no mucosa.
Buzzwords
"row of tombstones" → pemphigus vulgaris"fishnet / chicken-wire IgG" → pemphigus"oral erosions first, then skin" → pemphigus vulgaris"acantholysis" → pemphigus
Q3. What are the features of bullous pemphigoid?
  • Clinical: onset >60 y; multiple tense, pruritic bullae; early lesions may be urticarial (pink, hive-like, but persistent); Nikolsky negative; mucosa in only 10–35% (mainly buccal).
  • Pathogenesis: autoimmune IgG against the hemidesmosome → BPAG1 & BPAG2 (BP230 & BP180), the complex anchoring basal cells to the basement membrane. Complement → eosinophils/neutrophils → proteases → split.
  • Histology: separation below the epidermis with eosinophils.
  • Direct IF: band-like (linear) IgG and C3 at the dermal–epidermal junction.
  • Treatment: systemic or topical glucocorticoids (depending on extent) → steroid-sparing: doxycycline/niacinamide, rituximab, dupilumab.
  • Prognosis: ~50% remission in 2–6 years; poorer if >65 y.
CheckAn 80-year-old has itchy hive-like plaques for weeks, then tense blisters. Which disease starts this way?
Bullous pemphigoid: urticarial (pre-bullous) stage.
Step 1 HY

BPAG2 = type XVII collagen. Boards contrast: PV = younger, mucosa, flaccid, Nikolsky +; BP = elderly, itchy, tense, Nikolsky −, eosinophils.

Step 1 trap

The BP split is below the basal layer (at the basement membrane zone), not "above the basal layer." "Above the basal layer" = suprabasal = pemphigus.

Q1. How do topical steroids work, and how is strength graded?
  • Bind steroid (glucocorticoid) receptors in skin and immune cells (intracellular → altered gene expression) → anti-inflammatory: ↓ itch, swelling, redness.
  • Graded in 7 classes: class 1 = super potent → class 7 = least potent.
  • Look at the class, not the percentage. When several are listed, they are listed in order of strength.
