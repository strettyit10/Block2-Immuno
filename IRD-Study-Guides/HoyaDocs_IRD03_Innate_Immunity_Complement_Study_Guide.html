Innate Immunity & the Complement System

Learning objectives

  1. LO 1Name the myeloid and lymphoid cells of innate immunity, explain what switches each one on, and describe how each kills microbes and tumor cells.
  2. LO 2Walk through the complement cascade from start to finish, and contrast how the classical, lectin and alternative pathways get it going.
  3. LO 3Explain what the innate system does in the first seconds of an infection (immediate phase) versus over the following hours (induced phase).
  4. LO 4Show how innate proteins and innate cells help each other, and how innate immunity hands off to, and is re-focused by, the adaptive response.
  5. LO 5Spot the diseases caused by broken innate defenses (complement deficiencies, C1 inhibitor deficiency, CGD and related reactions) from the clinical picture.

LO 1The innate cell rosterWho the innate cells are, what turns them on, and how they kill

By the end: you can sort every innate cell into phagocyte / degranulator / innate lymphocyte, match TLRs to their ligands and locations, trace the respiratory burst, and predict when an NK cell will or will not kill.
Q1. Which cells belong to the innate system, and how are they grouped?
  • Myeloid innate cells split into two effector styles (with some overlap):
    • Phagocytes → engulf and kill: macrophages, neutrophils (and dendritic cells, which eat to present).
    • Degranulating cells → dump soluble killing factors next to the pathogen: eosinophils, mast cells, basophils.
  • Lymphoid innate cells come from the common lymphoid progenitor (like B and T cells) but have no unique, clonal antigen receptor:
    • ILC1, ILC2, ILC3 ↔ parallel the three classes of CD4⁺ T helper cells.
    • NK cells ↔ parallel CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells (now counted as a type of ILC).

Host DNA/RNA from dying cells is floating around outside cells. Keeping TLR3/7/8/9 inside endosomes means they mostly see nucleic acids from ingested microbes, limiting responses to self.
Step 1 HY

  • Boards call MAMPs PAMPs. DAMPs: mitochondrial DNA, histones, heat-shock proteins.

Step 1 trap

TLRs signal through TIR-domain adaptors (MyD88/TRIF) → NF-κB, not through ITAMs (those belong to the BCR, TCR and Fc receptors). And "innate receptors" does not mean every innate cell has identical receptors; it means germline-encoded, non-clonal receptors.

Memory tool

"Outside stuff → outside receptor; inside stuff → inside receptor."

  • Endosomal = 3, 7, 8, 9 → "nucleic acids hide in 3-7-8-9" (ds-RNA, ss-RNA ×2, CpG DNA).
  • TLR4 = LPS ("four-endotoxin"); TLR5 = flagellin ("a flagellum is a 5th limb"); TLR2 pairs up (with 1 or 6) for lipopeptides.
Q3. What makes the macrophage the "sentinel," and how does any phagocyte kill what it eats?
  • Origin: many tissue macrophages seed tissues before birth (fetal liver / yolk sac); after birth, bone-marrow monocytes enter tissues and become macrophages.
    • Tissue names: Kupffer cells (liver), microglia (brain), alveolar macrophages (lung), osteoclasts (bone).
  • Three jobs: (1) bind invaders via MAMP receptors → (2) phagocytose and kill → (3) secrete cytokines + chemokines that recruit help (the induced phase, LO 3).
  • Scavenger receptors → clean up damaged tissue, apoptotic cells and atherosclerotic lipid. T cells (IFN-γ) can boost macrophage killing.

Q5. How do dendritic cells, mast cells, basophils and eosinophils work?

Dendritic cells

  • cDC → phagocytose, break down microbes, present to T cells → bridge to adaptive immunity.
  • pDC → specialized for type I interferon (IFN-α/β) via endosomal TLR7/9.

Mast cells

  • Tissue-resident, staged at mucosal surfaces.
  • Activated mostly by antigen cross-linking IgE on FcεRI; also complement fragments (C3a, C5a), neuropeptides, microbial products, cytokines, injury, temperature, pressure.

Basophils

  • Circulate in blood (vs mast cells in tissue).
  • Also FcεRI⁺; overlapping mediators; both take part in mild and severe allergy, including anaphylaxis.

Eosinophils

  • Target helminths too big to phagocytose.
  • Triggered by IgE, IgA or IgG (or complement) on the parasite → degranulate ROS + granule proteins (major basic protein) onto its membrane.
  • Drive collagen deposition → granuloma that walls off the parasite.

Preformed (granules)Histamine, heparin, tryptase, serotonin, TNFSeconds (early)

  • Mast cells are double-edged: helpful → kill bacteria/infected cells/parasites, recruit neutrophils, DCs, T and NK cells; harmful → strong local vasodilation/leak can let viruses or parasites into the circulation.
  • Over-activation of mast cells, basophils and eosinophils → asthma, allergy, type I hypersensitivity.
CheckWhich innate cell is best suited to attack a schistosome, and why not a neutrophil?
Eosinophil. The worm is far too large to phagocytose, so the cell must degranulate onto its surface (antibody-guided) and wall it off with a granuloma.
Step 1 HY
  • Eosinophilia causes: NAACP → Neoplasia, Asthma, Allergic processes, Chronic adrenal insufficiency, Parasites (invasive).
  • Basophilia is a clue to CML and other myeloproliferative neoplasms.
  • Mast-cell tryptase is the lab marker of anaphylaxis.
Step 1 trap

The complement pieces that degranulate mast cells are the anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a, not the opsonin C3b. "Basophil vs mast cell" questions usually hinge on location: blood vs tissue.

Q6. How does an NK cell decide to kill, and how does it do it?
  • NK cells read the balance of activating vs inhibitory signals.
    • Inhibitory receptors (KIRs, CD94:NKG2A) bind normal MHC class I → shut the NK cell off → healthy cell spared.
    • "Missing self": no MHC I (many viruses + tumors downregulate it to hide from CD8⁺ T cells) → no brake → kill.
    • "Stressed self": lots of stress ligands / death receptors → activation overwhelms the brake → kill.
    • ADCC: CD16 (FcγRIII) binds the Fc of IgG coating a target → cross-linking → kill.

Kill route 1: degranulation

Perforin makes pores → granzymes enter → caspases → apoptosis.

Kill route 2: death receptor

NK TRAIL → DR4/DR5 on stressed cell → FADD → caspase-8 → apoptosis.

  • Apoptosis, not necrosis → contents stay packaged → minimal local inflammation.
  • Activated by IL-12 (macrophages/DCs), type I IFN, IL-15 (also IL-2); NK cells secrete IFN-γ → activates macrophages.
  • Tumors: many lose MHC I → NK lysis. IL-15 has been used therapeutically to boost NK anti-tumor activity. Tumor-microenvironment TGF-β suppresses NK cells → immune escape.
CheckWhich molecule on a healthy cell turns NK cells off: MHC I, MHC II, CD1 or CD40?
MHC class I. Nearly all nucleated cells express it; MHC II is restricted to APCs, so it could not protect most body cells.
Step 1 HY
  • NK markers: CD16 and CD56 (no CD3, no TCR).
  • NK kill without prior sensitization and without MHC restriction.
  • Therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (e.g., anti-CD20) partly work by ADCC via NK CD16.
Step 1 trap

A virus that downregulates MHC I escapes CD8⁺ T cells but becomes a target for NK cells. Don't pick "CD8 T cells" or "helper T cells" for a cell that has lost MHC I.

Memory tool

MHC I = "ID badge." NK = security guard: badge shown → you walk by; no badge, looking stressed, or tagged with an IgG "wanted" sticker → removed (quietly, by apoptosis).

Q7. What are innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), and what activates them?

  • They make their effector cytokines in response to cytokines from other innate cells (macrophages, DCs, epithelium) → amplify those signals.

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A melanoma has lost surface MHC class I. Which arm of immunity is now best placed to recognize it, and what therapy idea follows?
NK cells (missing self). CD8⁺ T cells can no longer see tumor peptides. Boosting NK activity (e.g., IL-15) is a logical strategy; tumor TGF-β works against it.
A2A mouse lacks TLR4. Predict its response to an injection of purified LPS.
Little or no response: no NF-κB → no TNF-α/IL-1/IL-6 → no fever or septic shock from LPS. It still responds to flagellin (TLR5) and CpG DNA (TLR9).
A3After a bee sting, a beekeeper gets a wheal within seconds and more swelling hours later. Which mast-cell mediators explain each phase?
Early: preformed histamine (and heparin, tryptase). Late: newly made leukotrienes and prostaglandins plus cytokines.
A4A patient's neutrophils are given a drug that blocks Rac2 activation. Which killing mechanism fails, and which still work?
The respiratory burst (NADPH oxidase assembly) fails. Oxygen-independent killing still works: acidification, lysozyme/hydrolases, defensins, lactoferrin, NETs.

LO 2The complement cascadeThree ways in, one central C3 step, three ways to hurt a microbe

By the end: you can draw all three activation pathways to the C3 and C5 convertases, name each convertase, assign every fragment to its effector function, and explain how host cells keep complement off themselves.
Q1. What is complement, and what is the one naming rule you need?
  • An ancient innate system of soluble zymogens (inactive precursors) in blood and body fluids, made mostly by the liver.
  • Activated by sequential proteolytic cleavage; named for "complementing" antibody, but it works alone or together with other innate and adaptive parts.
  • Three stages: (1) activation (3 pathways) → (2) C3 convertase (amplification) → (3) effector functions: inflammation, opsonization, membrane attack.
  • Naming rule: "b binds, a goes away." The larger b fragment binds the activating surface and drives the next step; the small a fragment diffuses off (some act like cytokines).
    • e.g., C3 → C3a (leaves → inflammation) + C3b (sticks to microbe → opsonin).
    • Exception: C2. The C2a (serine-protease piece) stays bound in C4b2a; C2b leaves.
CheckC5 is cleaved into C5a and C5b. Which one forms the MAC, and which one recruits neutrophils?
C5b stays on the membrane and seeds the MAC (with C6–C9). C5a diffuses away → anaphylatoxin and potent neutrophil chemoattractant.
Step 1 HY

Some references rename the C2 fragments so the rule holds and write the classical convertase as C4b2b (C5 convertase C4b2b3b). It is the same complex as C4b2a; recognize either spelling.

Memory tool

"b binds, a away" → think "a = alarm" (C3a/C5a sound the inflammatory alarm), "b = bound / build" (C3b, C5b build the next step). C2 is the rebel.

Q2. How does the classical pathway start, and why is IgM so good at it?
  • C1 complex = C1q + C1r + C1s. C1q = sensor (collagen stalk + globular heads); C1r and C1s = serine proteases.
  • C1q can bind:
    • the Fc of IgM or (some) IgG bound to a microbe, including natural IgM made before any infection;
    • the acute-phase protein C-reactive protein (CRP);
    • MAMPs such as lipoteichoic acid directly.

Q3. How is the lectin pathway different from the classical pathway, and how is it the same?

Different front end

  • Mannose-binding lectin (MBL) → binds clusters of mannose and fucose with high avidity.
  • Ficolins → bind glycans with acetylated sugars.
  • Carried proteases: MASP-1, -2, -3. MASP-1 cleaves MASP-2 → MASP-2 cleaves C4 and C2.
  • No antibody needed; these sugar patterns are on microbes, not host cells.

Same back end

  • MBL/ficolin = same architecture as C1q (collagen stalk + recognition heads).
  • C4b + C2a → C4b2a: identical C3 convertase to classical.
  • C5 convertase also identical: C4b2a3b.
  • MBL deficiency is common (~10% of people), highly variable and usually clinically silent when the rest of the immune system is intact (other pathways still make C3b). It matters with co-existing immunodeficiency or in infancy.
CheckWhy do classical and lectin pathways produce the same convertase?
Both end by using a serine protease (C1s or MASP-2) to cleave C4 and C2 → C4b2a. Only the sensor and protease differ.

Q5. Once C3b coats a microbe, what happens next?
  • Deposition of large amounts of C3b on the target is the critical, central step, no matter which pathway started it. Then one of two things:
    • Opsonization → phagocytes with C3b (complement) receptors engulf and kill → clears far more microbes than the MAC → the most important effector function.
    • Membrane attack → C3 convertase + one more C3b = C5 convertase → C5b → recruits C6, C7, C8, C9 → MAC pore → osmotic lysis. Advantage: kills without help from any other immune component. But many pathogens have evolved MAC resistance.
  • Along the way the small fragments C3a, C4a, C5a act like cytokines (anaphylatoxins): ↑ vascular permeability (influx of antibody, complement, leukocytes), mast-cell degranulation (histamine, TNF-α), phagocyte recruitment → local inflammation. Systemic activation → shock resembling IgE anaphylaxis.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A patient lacks factor B. Which pathway fails, and is C3b still made?
Alternative pathway (and its amplification loop) fails. C3b is still made by C4b2a from the classical and lectin pathways, just with less amplification.
A2A child has C2 deficiency. Which pathway still makes C3b, and why is disease milder than C3 deficiency?
The alternative pathway (doesn't use C2). Classical and lectin both need C2 for C4b2a and fail. Since some C3b is still made, opsonization is partly preserved.
A3Massive systemic complement activation causes widespread vasodilation, edema and hypotension. Which products are responsible?
The anaphylatoxins C3a, C4a, C5a → mast-cell degranulation + ↑ permeability throughout the body → shock resembling IgE anaphylaxis.
A4Predict the effect of a drug that blocks C5 cleavage on (a) opsonization and (b) defense against meningococcus.
(a) Opsonization intact (C3b still deposited). (b) No MAC → ↑ risk of Neisseria infection → vaccinate first. (This is eculizumab.)

LO 3Immediate vs induced innate responseThe first seconds at the barrier, then the hours-long, body-wide follow-up

By the end: you can place every innate defense on the immediate or induced side of the timeline, explain each barrier and antimicrobial protein, name the macrophage cytokines and their effects, and explain why the same cytokine protects locally but kills systemically.
Q1. What separates the immediate phase from the induced phase, and why isn't "induced" the same as "adaptive"?
  • Innate response = parts that respond rapidly to infection or tissue damage, triggered by MAMPs and DAMPs. It is roughly constant through life: responds the same way each time, with little ability to adapt (tissues may show some "training"). Many microbes are cleared before you ever notice.
  • Immediate phase → seconds, at the site of encounter: barriers, antimicrobial proteins, complement, resident sentinels.
  • Induced phase → hours, can involve the whole body: triggered by sentinel cytokines and chemokines → recruited leukocytes, acute-phase proteins, fever, interferons.
  • Adaptive response = activation of B and T lymphocytes (antigen-specific, clonal, days). The induced innate phase is still innate.
CheckNeutrophils pour into a wound 6 hours after injury. Immediate, induced, or adaptive?
Induced innate. Recruitment needs sentinel cytokines/chemokines (hours) but no B or T cells.
Step 1 trap

"Induced" ≠ "adaptive." Fever, CRP rise, neutrophilia and interferon production are all innate. Adaptive means antigen-specific lymphocytes.

Buzzwords
"MAMP / PAMP" → conserved microbial pattern"DAMP" → damaged-tissue signal"germline-encoded, non-clonal" → innate receptor
Q2. What anatomic barriers protect each body surface?
  • Epithelium = watertight barrier most microbes cannot cross. Break it (wound, burn injury) → infection much more likely.
  • Skin: stratum corneum cells joined by tight junctions; lamellar bodies in the stratum granulosum release the watertight lipid layer plus β-defensins and cathelicidins.
  • Mucous membranes: mucus traps microbes; cilia sweep them out (mucociliary escalator). Altered mucus or cilia (e.g., cystic fibrosis) → ↑ infection.
  • Microbiota everywhere: occupy space/nutrients/receptors → block colonization, and stimulate natural antibody (low-level, low-affinity IgM present even without an active adaptive response).

The microbiological barrier. Antibiotics killed normal gut flora (they don't discriminate "good" from "bad"), leaving room for Clostridioides difficile.

Q3. How do lysozyme and the antimicrobial peptides kill microbes without hurting us?

Lysozyme (enzyme)

  • Degrades peptidoglycan of the bacterial wall.
  • Found in tears and saliva, secreted by phagocytes and Paneth cells of gut crypts.
  • More active against gram-positive bacteria (thick, exposed peptidoglycan); in gram-negatives the outer LPS membrane shields it.
  • Paired with secretory phospholipase A₂, which attacks the membrane once it's exposed; both also clean up debris.

Antimicrobial peptides

  • Defensins, cathelicidins, lecticidins, histatins.
  • Amphipathic (hydrophilic + hydrophobic parts); positive charge + membrane electric field pull them into the microbial bilayer → self-associate into pores → lysis.
  • Hit bacteria, fungi, some enveloped viruses.
  • Made as inactive precursors (zymogens), activated by cleavage outside the cell.
  • Sterols (cholesterol) in host membranes protect our cells.

CheckWhich would work best against oral Candida: lysozyme, histatins or α-defensins?
Histatins: salivary peptides active against pathogenic fungi in the mouth. Lysozyme targets bacterial peptidoglycan; α-defensins are mostly gut (Paneth cells).
Memory tool

Lysozyme thinks like penicillin: both attack peptidoglycan, so both work best on gram-positives. Histatins = "his-mouth" (saliva, fungi).

Step 1 trap

Lysozyme is an enzyme (cuts peptidoglycan), not a pore-former. Defensins don't spare host cells because of "specific receptors"; they're spared because host membranes contain cholesterol and are less negatively charged.

Q4. What do sentinel macrophages secrete to launch the induced phase?
  • The pathogen's MAMPs (via TLRs etc.) decide which cytokines a macrophage makes. The "big three" pro-inflammatory cytokines = IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, with lots of overlap:
    • Local (paracrine): endothelial activation, vasodilation, ↑ permeability, swelling/redness, leukocyte recruitment.
    • Systemic (endocrine): fever, acute-phase proteins, leukocytosis, metabolite mobilization, shock.

Q6. What are acute-phase proteins and why does the liver make them?
  • IL-6 (with IL-1, TNF-α) → hepatocytes release induced-phase proteins that amplify innate defense:
    • C-reactive protein (CRP) → binds bacterial phosphocholine → opsonin + activates C1q.
    • MBL, surfactant proteins SP-A / SP-D → opsonins / lectin-pathway trigger.
    • Extra complement components → complement enhancement.
    • Fibrinogen → clotting near the infection. Serum amyloid protein.
CheckHow does CRP connect a liver protein to the complement cascade without any antibody?
CRP coats bacteria (phosphocholine) and is bound by C1q → classical pathway starts antibody-free.
Step 1 HY

↑ Acute-phase reactants: CRP, ferritin, fibrinogen, hepcidin (→ anemia of chronic disease), serum amyloid A (→ AA amyloidosis), haptoglobin, procalcitonin. ↓ (negative): albumin, transferrin, transthyretin.

Q2. What do type I interferons do, and who makes them?
  • IFN-α and IFN-β are made by many cell types after viral infection; plasmacytoid DCs are specialized to make huge amounts.
  • They interfere with viral replication. Effects:
    1. Antiviral state in nearby uninfected cells (Mx proteins, 2′-5′ oligoadenylate, PKR; IFIT proteins block viral RNA translation).
    2. ↑ MHC class I and antigen presentation → better CD8⁺ T-cell killing.
    3. Activate DCs and macrophages.
    4. Activate NK cells to kill virus-infected cells.
    5. Induce chemokines to recruit lymphocytes.
  • Signal through STAT1/STAT2 + IRF9 (ISGF3).
CheckHow do type I interferons from a virus-infected cell help the adaptive response?
They ↑ MHC I on surrounding cells so infected cells show more viral peptide to CD8⁺ T cells; they also activate DCs and recruit lymphocytes.
Step 1 HY
  • IFN-α: chronic hepatitis B/C (historically), some cancers. IFN-β: multiple sclerosis. IFN-γ: CGD prophylaxis.
  • Adverse effects: flu-like symptoms, depression, neutropenia.
Memory tool

Interferon = "smoke alarm" for neighbors. The burning (infected) cell can't save itself, but it warns the neighbors to lock down (antiviral state) and raises the MHC I "flag" for CD8 T cells.

Q3. How does innate immunity start the adaptive response?
  • cDCs carry and present antigen to T cells (the main bridge).
  • IL-12 → CD4 T cells → TH1; IL-6 → ↑ antibody production.
  • Type I IFN → ↑ MHC I → better CD8 recognition.
  • TLR signaling is the basis of vaccine adjuvants: adjuvants supply MAMP-like signals so innate cells activate and help the adaptive response to the vaccine antigen.
  • C3b on antigen also helps B-cell activation (part of why C3-deficient patients get reinfected with the same organism).
Q4. How does the adaptive response later re-aim innate killers?

IgM / IgG → complement

Antibody bound to microbe → C1q → classical pathway. Natural IgM does this even before infection.

IgG → NK (ADCC)

CD16 binds IgG Fc on a target → NK kills it. ADCC re-focuses NK cells later in the response.

IgE (± IgA, IgG) → granulocytes

IgE arms mast cells (FcεRI); antibody-coated parasites trigger eosinophil degranulation.

  • T cells → macrophages: IFN-γ boosts macrophage killing.
  • Take-home: the innate system alone is not enough to beat all pathogens or clear all tumor cells; an intact adaptive system is essential for health.
CheckIgG coats a virus-infected cell expressing a viral glycoprotein. Name two innate mechanisms the antibody recruits.
(1) NK ADCC via CD16. (2) Classical complement (C1q on clustered IgG) → C3b opsonization ± MAC.
Step 1 HY
  • Monoclonal antibodies against tumors (e.g., rituximab) kill via complement-dependent and NK-mediated (ADCC) cytotoxicity.
  • Natural IgM is made by B-1 cells; it fixes complement extremely well.
Step 1 trap

NK cells are innate, but ADCC needs IgG made by the adaptive system. If a question says "IgG-coated cell killed by a CD16⁺ CD3⁻ cell," the answer is NK cell via ADCC, not a cytotoxic T cell.

Buzzwords
"adjuvant" → TLR stimulation"natural antibody" → low-affinity IgM → C1q"ADCC" → IgG + CD16 (NK)"↑ MHC I" → type I IFN

Memory tool

"Top = toddlers, tail = teens." Defects at the top/middle of the cascade (C1–C4, C3) start in early childhood; defects at the tail (C5–C9) show up in the teens with meningococcus.

Q2. How do you recognize C1 inhibitor deficiency (hereditary angioedema)?
  • Mechanism (as taught): no C1-INH → C1 activates spontaneously (rare, unchecked) → excess C4 cleavage (C4a accumulation) → swelling.
  • Clinical picture:
    • Recurrent, non-itchy, non-urticarial swelling of face, lips, tongue; mildly painful; lasts days.
    • Early episodes are often asymmetric; bilateral swelling develops over time.
    • Often after mild trauma or dental procedures.
    • GI wall edema → crampy acute abdominal pain (mimics surgical abdomen).
    • Airway involvement → the dangerous, potentially fatal form.
    • Hereditary or acquired; onset childhood–adolescence.
CheckHow can you tell hereditary angioedema from an allergic reaction at the bedside?
No urticaria (hives) and no itch, slow onset over hours, often asymmetric, triggered by trauma/dental work, with abdominal attacks. Allergy = itchy hives, rapid onset, responds to antihistamines/epinephrine.
Step 1 HY
  • Boards' mechanism: C1-INH also inhibits kallikrein → deficiency → unregulated kallikrein → ↑ bradykinin → angioedema.
  • Lab: ↓ C4 (consumed); C1-INH level or function ↓.
  • ACE inhibitors are contraindicated (ACE breaks down bradykinin).
  • Treat: C1-INH concentrate, ecallantide (kallikrein inhibitor), icatibant (bradykinin B2 blocker).
Buzzwords
"swelling after dental work, no hives" → C1-INH deficiency"recurrent abdominal pain + facial swelling" → HAE"low C4" → C1-INH deficiency (between attacks too)
Q3. How do you diagnose chronic granulomatous disease (CGD)?

Case: teenager near respiratory failure

15-year-old garden-center worker → rapid severe dyspnea, "cotton ball" densities on CXR, aspirate grows Aspergillus fumigatus; two more lung infections in hospital.

Labs: ↑ WBC (normal proportions), ↑ IgG; neutrophils fail NBT, severely ↓ H₂O₂.

Answer: defective respiratory burst (not chemotaxis, cytokine production, extravasation or phagocytosis).

Defect + consequences

Deficient NADPH oxidase → no respiratory burst → phagocytes engulf but can't kill → frequent bacterial + fungal infections; leukocytes form granulomas (dense nodules) around persistent microbes.

Inheritance: X-linked (most common) or autosomal recessive (rarer).

In CGD, phagocytosis and chemotaxis are normal; killing fails. Don't confuse with LAD (no pus, delayed cord separation, ↑↑ neutrophils in blood, defective migration) or MPO deficiency (Candida, normal NBT/DHR).

Q4. What is the Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction, and what other "innate overreactions" should you recognize?

Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction cytokine surge

Who: patients with spirochete infections (syphilis, Lyme disease) starting antibiotics.

When: within 24 h; resolves within a day without treatment.

What: fever, chills, rigors, N/V, headache, tachycardia, hypotension, hyperventilation, flushing, myalgia, worsening skin lesions. Feels terrible, usually not dangerous.

Why: killed spirochetes release a burst of MAMPs → transient spike of IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α.

Septic shock systemic TNF-α

Bacteremia → liver/spleen macrophages release TNF-α into blood → systemic leak, ↓ BP, DIC, multi-organ failure.

Complement anaphylaxis C3a · C4a · C5a

Massive systemic complement activation → mast-cell degranulation everywhere → shock resembling IgE anaphylaxis.

Barrier failures first line lost

Burn injury/wounds → skin infection; CF → thick mucus, recurrent lung infection; antibiotics → loss of flora → C. difficile.

CheckA man with secondary syphilis gets IM penicillin and 6 hours later has fever, rigors and a worse rash. Should penicillin be labeled an allergy?
No. This is the Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction (MAMP release → cytokine surge), not drug hypersensitivity. Continue treatment; it resolves within a day.
Step 1 HY

JHR also occurs with leptospirosis and relapsing fever (Borrelia recurrentis). Classic setting: first penicillin dose for syphilis.

Step 1 trap

JHR vs drug allergy: JHR has no urticaria/wheezing pattern, starts within hours of the first dose, and is self-limited within a day. Penicillin allergy would recur with every dose.

11Twelve hours after the onset of pneumococcal pneumonia, a patient's serum C-reactive protein rises sharply. CRP then binds phosphocholine on the bacteria. Which further event does CRP most directly promote?

C. CRP (made by the liver in response to IL-6) is an opsonin bound by C1q → classical pathway without antibody. A is the alternative pathway and B the lectin pathway. D is CD59's job. E requires IgG, not CRP.

12A healthy 35-year-old blood donor is incidentally found to have very low serum mannose-binding lectin. She has never had a serious infection. Which statement about her condition is most accurate?

E. MBL deficiency affects about 10% of people and rarely causes disease when other pathways are intact. A: classical and alternative pathways still make C3 convertases. B describes terminal-complement deficiency. C: the classical pathway uses C1q, not MBL. D applies to C1-INH deficiency.

13A Peace Corps volunteer returning from sub-Saharan Africa has hematuria and an eosinophil count of 2,800/µL. Biopsy shows Schistosoma eggs surrounded by eosinophils and fibrous tissue. Which mechanism best describes how eosinophils attack this organism?

D. Helminths are too large to engulf; IgE/IgA/IgG (or complement) on the parasite trigger eosinophil degranulation, and collagen deposition forms a granuloma around it. A describes neutrophils with small microbes. B is NK killing. C is antiviral. E: the MAC is formed by complement on the target, not by eosinophils.

14A 4-year-old boy with recurrent Serratia and Staphylococcus abscesses has neutrophils with essentially no fluorescence shift on dihydrorhodamine flow cytometry. His mother's neutrophils show two distinct populations: one with a normal shift and one with no shift. Which explains the mother's result?

A. X-linked CGD carriers have mosaic neutrophils (lyonization): those expressing the normal X make ROS, the others don't. B: AR patients show a uniform small, broad shift, and the mother is well. C: MPO deficiency gives a normal DHR result (superoxide/H₂O₂ still made). D would give one uniformly reduced population. E is not the best explanation for a classic pattern.
