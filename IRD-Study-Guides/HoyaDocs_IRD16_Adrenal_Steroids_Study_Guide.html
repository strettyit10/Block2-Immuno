Adrenal Steroids (Glucocorticoids)

Learning objectives

  1. LO 1Walk through the hypothalamus → pituitary → adrenal chain that makes cortisol, and explain what keeps blood cortisol in check (negative feedback, plasma binding proteins, breakdown).
  2. LO 2Sketch how cortisol rises and falls over 24 hours, and use that daily pattern to decide when to dose steroids and when to draw blood.
  3. LO 3Name the non-adrenal diseases that glucocorticoids are used to treat, grouped by the type of effect being exploited.
  4. LO 4Explain, step by step, how glucocorticoids switch genes on and off to shut down inflammation and immune activity.
  5. LO 5Compare common steroids by how strongly they fight inflammation versus how much salt they hold on to, and connect those differences to their chemical tweaks and to the enzymes that turn them on and off.
  6. LO 6Rank the common corticosteroids as short-, intermediate- or long-acting.
  7. LO 7Predict the adverse effects of corticosteroid therapy from cortisol's normal actions, and the conditions in which steroids should be avoided.
  8. LO 8Outline how to limit harm during long-term steroid use, including why and how to taper.
  9. LO 9Choose drug therapy for too little adrenal cortex output (primary vs secondary insufficiency) and approach too much (Cushing syndrome).

MAPBig picture

  • Hypothalamus → CRH → anterior pituitary → ACTH → zona fasciculata → cortisol ⊣ both levels
    • Peak ~8 AM, trough ~midnight → dose in the morning (+ late afternoon for replacement)
  • Plasma: ~90% bound (CBG high-affinity/low-capacity; albumin low/high) → ~10% free does the work
  • Free GC → cytoplasmic GR (drops hsp90) → dimer → nucleus → GRE
    • ↑ lipocortin-1 (⊣ PLA₂), IκBα (⊣ NF-κB) · ↓ COX-2, cPLA₂, cytokines, chemokines, adhesion molecules
  • Drug choice = GC (anti-inflammatory) potency vs MC (salt) potency + duration
    • Dexamethasone 30 / 0, long · Fludrocortisone 10 / 250 · Cortisone, prednisone = 11-keto prodrugs (no topical use)
  • Excess → Cushingoid metabolic effects + HTN, osteoporosis, infection, ulcers, CNS → contraindications mirror them
  • Chronic use → HPA suppression → lowest dose, alternate day, local, stress-dose, taper (recovery up to ~9–12 mo)
  • Addison → hydrocortisone + fludrocortisone · secondary → hydrocortisone alone

LO 1The HPA axis and plasma cortisolHow cortisol is made, carried, controlled and cleared

By the end: you can draw the HPA axis with its feedback loops, explain what exogenous steroids do to it, describe how CBG and albumin buffer cortisol (and what changes CBG), and explain how the kidney keeps cortisol off the mineralocorticoid receptor.
Q1. Where is cortisol made, and what does it normally do?
  • The adrenal cortex has three zones, outside → in:
    • Zona glomerulosa → mineralocorticoids (aldosterone) — "salt"
    • Zona fasciculata → glucocorticoids (cortisol) — "sugar"
    • Zona reticularis → androgens — "sex"
    • (The medulla makes catecholamines — epinephrine, norepinephrine.)
  • Cortisol = the naturally occurring glucocorticoid (GC). Main jobs:
    • Stress response — major component
    • Gluconeogenesis → keeps blood glucose up during fasting or stress
    • Permissive for many functions, e.g. adrenergic and noradrenergic signaling (catecholamines act weakly without it)
    • Anti-inflammatory / immunosuppressive (LO 4)
  • Cortisol also has mineralocorticoid-receptor (MR) activity → Na⁺ retention, K⁺ loss in the kidney → ↑ blood pressure and volume.
    • This is normally minimized by inactivation of GCs in the kidney (11β-HSD2 — Q4).
CheckWhich zone would you expect to keep working if ACTH disappeared, and why?
Zona glomerulosa. Aldosterone is driven mainly by the renin–angiotensin system (angiotensin II, K⁺), not ACTH. This is why aldosterone is preserved in secondary adrenal insufficiency (LO 9).
Q2. How does the HPA axis control cortisol, and what do exogenous steroids do to it?

  • Negative feedback: cortisol acts on both the hypothalamus (↓ CRH) and the anterior pituitary (↓ ACTH).
  • Inputs that ↑ CRH: stress and the circadian clock (LO 2).
  • Exogenous glucocorticoids use the same feedback loop → suppress CRH + ACTH → no trophic signal → the patient's own zona fasciculata atrophies.
CheckA patient has taken prednisone 20 mg daily for a year. Predict her plasma ACTH and the size of her adrenal cortex.
ACTH ↓, adrenal cortex atrophied. Prednisone (→ prednisolone) binds GR in the hypothalamus and pituitary → ↓ CRH and ACTH → no trophic drive to the zona fasciculata.
Step 1 HY
  • ACTH is cleaved from POMC along with MSH → states of ↑ ACTH (primary adrenal insufficiency) cause skin and mucosal hyperpigmentation.
  • Cortisol actions, boards style: "A BIG FIB" — ↑ Appetite, ↑ BP, ↑ Insulin resistance, ↑ Gluconeogenesis/lipolysis/proteolysis, ↓ Fibroblasts, ↓ Inflammation/Immunity, ↓ Bone formation.
  • Permissive effect: cortisol ↑ α₁-receptor expression on arterioles → ↑ sensitivity to NE/epinephrine.
Memory tool

GFR = "salt, sugar, sex" — Glomerulosa (aldosterone), Fasciculata (cortisol), Reticularis (androgens). "The deeper you go, the sweeter it gets."

Q3. How is cortisol carried in blood, and what changes the carrier?
  • ~90% bound, ~10% free. Only the free fraction enters cells (binds GR) or gets cleared.

Corticosteroid-binding globulin (CBG, transcortin)HighLow (~40 µg/100 mL)Binds >70% of cortisol → circulating reservoir

  • High affinity + low capacity → CBG grabs cortisol tightly but saturates at high levels (e.g., high-dose therapy) → free fraction climbs sharply.
  • CBG is dynamically regulated:
ConditionEffect on CBGResult

  • Chemical t½ (plasma) is short; biological t½ is longer, because GCs act by changing gene expression, which outlasts the drug in plasma.
CheckA woman on an estrogen-containing contraceptive has a high total serum cortisol but no Cushingoid features. Why?
Estrogen ↑ hepatic CBG → more cortisol bound → total cortisol rises while free (active) cortisol stays about normal.
Q4. If cortisol binds the MR, why don't we all retain salt?
  • Cortisol binds the MR about as well as aldosterone and circulates at far higher concentrations.
  • In MR-expressing kidney cells, 11β-HSD2 converts cortisol → cortisone (inactive) → aldosterone, not cortisol, occupies the MR.
  • Two ways to break this protection (details in LO 5):
    • Saturate it → high-dose cortisol → Na⁺/fluid retention, K⁺ loss
    • Inhibit it → licorice (glycyrrhetic acid)
CheckWhat lab pattern would you expect if renal 11β-HSD2 were blocked?
Hypertension, hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis with low renin and low aldosterone (the MR is being driven by cortisol, so the RAS is switched off).
Step 1 trap

An elevated total cortisol in pregnancy or on oral contraceptives is a CBG effect, not Cushing syndrome. When CBG is abnormal, measure free cortisol (24-h urine free or late-night salivary cortisol).

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A patient with septic shock has a low total cortisol. Before diagnosing adrenal insufficiency, what plasma factor should you consider?
Low CBG. Sepsis (and burn injury) lowers CBG → total cortisol falls even if free cortisol is adequate.
A2A pituitary tumor destroys all corticotrophs. Which adrenal hormone is lost, and which is largely spared?
Cortisol lost; aldosterone spared. No ACTH → fasciculata fails; glomerulosa runs on angiotensin II.
A3Predict the effect on CRH and ACTH when a patient starts high-dose dexamethasone.
Both fall. Dexamethasone binds GR in the hypothalamus and pituitary and engages negative feedback, the basis of the dexamethasone suppression test.
A4Why does a patient's glucocorticoid effect last long after the drug has left the plasma?
Biological t½ > chemical t½. The drug works by altering gene expression; the new (or missing) proteins persist after plasma levels fall.

LO 2Circadian rhythm of cortisolThe daily curve and what it means for dosing and testing

By the end: you can draw the 24-hour cortisol curve, give its normal range, and apply it to replacement dosing, short anti-inflammatory courses, and blood-draw timing.
Q1. What does the daily cortisol curve look like?
  • Release follows a circadian rhythm run through the HPA axis.
  • Peak: early morning (~8 AM), at the end of the sleep cycle. Lowest: around midnight.
  • Normal plasma levels fluctuate between about 2–20 µg/100 mL (µg/dL) over 24 h.
  • Why a morning surge? It drives gluconeogenesis so you can wake after an overnight fast without eating (energy + wakefulness).
CheckRoughly how many-fold can normal cortisol differ between 8 AM and midnight?
Up to ~10-fold (≈20 vs ≈2 µg/dL) across the full range; a 3-fold gap between a morning and an evening sample is completely normal.
Q2. What are the therapeutic implications of the rhythm?

1 · Dosing

  • Replacement therapy: give cortisol (hydrocortisone) in the AM and late afternoon → mimics the natural peak and decline.
  • Short anti-inflammatory courses: take steroids in the morning (e.g., a 5–7-day methylprednisolone taper after surgery; prednisone in the morning).
  • Evening dosing → high cortisol when it should be lowest → insomnia and more HPA suppression (it blunts the night-time CRH/ACTH rise that builds the morning surge).

2 · Testing

  • Adhere to a constant time for blood draws when measuring cortisol.
  • An 8 AM value and a 9 PM value can't be compared; they differ by design.
CheckA post-op patient is given a 6-day methylprednisolone taper. When should he take each dose?
In the morning — it works with the natural peak, causes less insomnia and less suppression. A course of several days carries little risk of serious toxicity.
Step 1 HY
  • Screening for Cushing syndrome exploits the rhythm: loss of the normal late-night trough → ↑ late-night salivary cortisol.
  • Morning (8 AM) cortisol is the standard screening sample for adrenal insufficiency (low when it should be highest).
Step 1 trap

A "low" evening cortisol is not adrenal insufficiency, and a "high" morning value is not Cushing syndrome. Always ask what time the sample was drawn.

Memory tool

"Rise and shine, cortisol's prime." Steroids go with breakfast, never with the bedtime snack.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A patient on hydrocortisone replacement takes both doses at bedtime and complains of insomnia and morning fatigue. Fix it.
Larger dose on waking, smaller dose in late afternoon. That reproduces the circadian curve: energy in the morning, low cortisol at night.
A2Cortisol is 18 µg/dL at one visit and 6 µg/dL at the next with no change in therapy. What is the most likely explanation?
Different draw times (e.g., 8 AM vs 9 PM). Draw at a constant time to compare.
A3Why would an evening dose of a glucocorticoid cause more HPA suppression than the same dose in the morning?
At night the axis is gearing up (↑ CRH/ACTH) for the morning surge; drug present then blocks that rise. In the morning, endogenous cortisol is already high and feedback is already on.

LO 3Uses outside the adrenalAnti-inflammatory, immunosuppressive and antineoplastic indications

By the end: you can list the non-endocrine indications, group them by the action used, and contrast them with the endocrine (replacement) use.
Q1. Which activities of glucocorticoids are used therapeutically?

Non-endocrine disorders

  • Anti-inflammatory activity → ↓ prostaglandins / leukotrienes, ↓ cytokines
  • Immunosuppressive activity → ↓ cytokines and chemokines, ↓ leukocyte activation + survival
  • Antineoplastic activity → at high doses, anti-tumor action against lymphomas (and leukemias)

Endocrine disorders

  • Replacement therapy for adrenal insufficiency (cortisol ± aldosterone) → LO 9
CheckWhich of the three non-endocrine actions explains why steroids are in lymphoma regimens?
Antineoplastic — at high doses GCs suppress lymphocyte proliferation and survival, so lymphoid malignancies respond.
Q2. What are the specific clinical indications?

Allergic disorders

Allergic reactions, rhinitis, severe contact dermatitis (e.g., poison ivy).

Asthma

Inhaled for control; oral bursts for exacerbations. Also ↑ β₂-receptor expression (LO 4).

Arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis and other inflammatory arthritis (oral or intra-articular).

Inflammatory bowel disease

Ulcerative colitis, Crohn disease flares.

Skin disorders

Dermatitis, psoriasis → topical active steroids (LO 5).

Autoimmune diseases

Lupus flares, vasculitis, autoimmune cytopenias.

Leukemia / lymphoma

High-dose antineoplastic use.

  • Common thread: disease driven by inflammation, immune activation or lymphoid proliferation.
CheckAsthma and psoriasis: indications or contraindications for steroids?
Indications. Contraindications are conditions a steroid side effect would worsen (ulcer, HTN, infection, diabetes, glaucoma, psychosis, osteoporosis — LO 7).
Step 1 HY
  • GCs induce lymphocyte (T-cell) apoptosis → used in CLL, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, ALL regimens (e.g., the "P" in R-CHOP = prednisone).
  • Other boards indications: sarcoidosis, acute gout (oral or intra-articular), Crohn, transplant rejection, cerebral edema from tumors (dexamethasone), fetal lung maturity (betamethasone).
Buzzwords
"lymphoma, high dose" → antineoplastic use"poison ivy, single large dose" → few side effects"lupus flare, few days" → short course, low risk

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1Classify each use: (a) prednisone in Hodgkin lymphoma, (b) hydrocortisone in Addison disease, (c) inhaled steroid in asthma.
(a) antineoplastic, (b) endocrine replacement, (c) anti-inflammatory.
A2A kidney transplant recipient takes prednisone. Which of the three non-endocrine actions is being used?
Immunosuppression — ↓ cytokines (e.g., IL-2) and ↓ T-cell activation and survival prevent rejection.
A3A patient with an ulcerative colitis flare also has an active untreated infection. Why is this a dilemma?
UC is an indication, but active infection is a contraindication: immunosuppression worsens infection and masks its signs.

By the end: you can trace a GC from plasma to DNA, list which genes go up and which go down, explain the lipocortin-1 / PLA₂ and IκBα / NF-κB mechanisms, and explain why steroids are safe in aspirin-sensitive asthma.
Q1. How does a glucocorticoid reach and change genes?

  • Unliganded GR is held in the cytoplasm by heat-shock protein 90 (hsp90).
  • Two kinds of DNA site:
    • +GRE → ↑ expression of anti-inflammatory genes
    • nGRE (negative GRE) → ↓ expression of pro-inflammatory genes
  • Because new proteins must be made (or stop being made), onset is delayed (hours) and effects outlast plasma levels.
CheckWhy doesn't IV hydrocortisone relieve an asthma attack within minutes?
It acts by changing gene expression; the effect needs hours. Fast relief comes from a β₂-agonist.
Q2. Which genes go up and which go down?

↑ Increased (+GRE) — anti-inflammatory

  • Lipocortin-1 (annexin A1) → inhibits PLA₂
  • β₂-adrenoceptor → more targets for β₂-agonists
  • Secretory leukocyte inhibitory protein (SLPI)
  • Clara cell protein (CC10)
  • IL-1 receptor antagonist
  • IL-1R2 (decoy receptor)
  • IκB-α → holds NF-κB in the cytoplasm

↓ Decreased (nGRE) — pro-inflammatory

  • Cytokines: IL-1 to IL-6, IL-11 to IL-13, TNF-α, GM-CSF, stem cell factor
  • Chemokines: IL-8, RANTES, MIP-1α, MCP-1/3/4, eotaxin
  • iNOS
  • COX-2
  • Cytoplasmic PLA₂
  • Endothelin-1
  • NK₁ / NK₂ receptors
  • Adhesion molecules: ICAM-1, E-selectin
  • IL-1 is blunted twice: IL-1 receptor antagonist blocks the receptor; IL-1R2 binds IL-1 without signaling.
  • IκB-α traps NF-κB, the master switch for TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, chemokines, adhesion molecules, COX-2 and iNOS.
  • ↓ chemokines + ↓ adhesion molecules → fewer leukocytes recruited to inflamed tissue.
CheckWhy might adding an inhaled steroid improve the response to an albuterol inhaler?
GCs ↑ β₂-adrenoceptor expression → more receptors for albuterol, plus less airway inflammation.
Q3. How do steroids shut down the arachidonic acid pathway, and how is that different from NSAIDs?
  • Lipocortin-1 inhibits phospholipase A₂ → ↓ arachidonic acid release → ↓ prostaglandins, TXA₂ and leukotrienes (both branches).
  • GCs also ↓ expression of cytoplasmic PLA₂ and COX-2 → PLA₂ is hit twice (less activity + less enzyme); less COX-2 → fewer inflammatory prostaglandins.
  • NSAIDs act only at COX → leukotrienes are spared (or increased by shunting).
CheckAn asthmatic with aspirin hypersensitivity needs an anti-inflammatory. Ibuprofen, hydrocortisone, acetaminophen or indomethacin?
Hydrocortisone. Ibuprofen and indomethacin block COX → shunt to leukotrienes → bronchospasm. Acetaminophen isn't anti-inflammatory. GCs block PLA₂ upstream of both COX and lipoxygenase.
Step 1 HY
  • One-line boards mechanism: "glucocorticoids inhibit NF-κB" → ↓ cytokines → suppress both B- and T-cell function.
  • Lipocortin-1 = annexin A1.
  • Aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (Samter triad): asthma + nasal polyps + aspirin sensitivity.
Step 1 trap

Loss of COX-2 lowers prostaglandins, not leukotrienes. Leukotriene reduction comes from blocking PLA₂ (lipocortin-1), the step upstream of lipoxygenase.

Memory tool

"Steroids cut the hose; NSAIDs kink one branch." PLA₂ is the main faucet for all eicosanoids.

Q4. How do steroids suppress immune cells?
  • GCs are immunosuppressants because they inhibit cytokine and chemokine levels.
  • They ↓ activation, proliferation and survival of most WBCs (T cells, B cells, macrophages, eosinophils).
  • Macrophages are most potently inhibited — about 3× more GRs than lymphocytes → ↓ phagocytosis, ↓ antigen presentation, ↓ IL-1 / TNF-α.
  • At high doses → anti-tumor actions (lymphomas).

CheckA patient on high-dose prednisone has WBC 14,000 with neutrophilia but no fever. Infection?
Probably not — steroid demargination raises circulating neutrophils. Look for the paired ↓ lymphocytes and eosinophils.

Blocking IL-2 production and T-cell function → steroids can reactivate TB and cause candidiasis and other opportunistic infections (e.g., Pneumocystis at high chronic doses).

ApplyLO 4 application questions

A1A mutation prevents GR from releasing hsp90. Predict the response to dexamethasone.
No response. GR can't dimerize or enter the nucleus → no GRE binding → no change in gene expression.
A2Name two GC-induced proteins that blunt IL-1, and one transcription factor GCs keep out of the nucleus.
IL-1 receptor antagonist and IL-1R2 (decoy); NF-κB (via ↑ IκB-α).
A3Why are steroids better than NSAIDs at lowering leukotriene-driven inflammation?
They block PLA₂ (lipocortin-1 + ↓ cPLA₂), upstream of 5-lipoxygenase; NSAIDs act only at COX.
A4Which leukocyte would you expect to be most sensitive to a low dose of glucocorticoid, and why?
Macrophage — ~3× more GRs than lymphocytes.

A2A man eating a bag of black licorice daily has BP 168/100 and K⁺ 2.9. Predict renin and aldosterone.

CheckWhich is longest-acting, and for how long?
Dexamethasone (and betamethasone), 36–72 h.
Q2. What makes dexamethasone long-acting, and why does duration matter?

Dexamethasone PK

1 · Does not bind CBG

2 · Long half-life (metabolically stable: 9α-F, 16-CH₃)

3 · Hepatic metabolism, primarily CYP3A4

What prolongs action

6α-methyl → prolongs plasma + biological t½ (methylprednisolone). Ring substitutions that slow metabolism; not parking on CBG.

Why it matters

Longer-acting = more HPA suppression per dose (no daily "off" window) → harder to wean. Short-acting drugs suit replacement and alternate-day regimens.

  • Because dexamethasone doesn't ride on CBG, its free level does not shift with pregnancy/estrogen (↑ CBG) or sepsis (↓ CBG).
CheckA pregnant patient's CBG doubles. Whose free plasma level changes least: hydrocortisone, prednisolone or dexamethasone?
Dexamethasone — it doesn't bind CBG.
Step 1 HY
  • Boards phrase it as: short = hydrocortisone, cortisone; intermediate (12–36 h) = prednisone, prednisolone, methylprednisolone, triamcinolone (fludrocortisone often listed here too); long = dexamethasone, betamethasone.
  • CYP3A4 inducers (rifampin, phenytoin, carbamazepine) ↑ dexamethasone clearance; low- vs high-dose dexamethasone suppression tests work up Cushing syndrome.
Step 1 trap

For your course, prednisone/prednisolone sit in the short (8–12 h) group and fludrocortisone is 8–12 h. If a board-style question says "intermediate-acting," it is using the boards grouping, not a contradiction of the potency data.

Memory tool

"Dex lasts for days." 36–72 h ≈ 1.5–3 days. "Tri = middle child" (triamcinolone, 12–36 h).

↓ Dexamethasone levels — rifampin induces CYP3A4 → faster clearance → may need a higher dose.

LO 8Minimizing harm and taperingDose, schedule, route, stress cover and weaning

By the end: you can sort dosing situations by risk, list the side-effect-sparing measures, explain stress dosing, and read the HPA recovery curve.
Q1. When are steroids dangerous and when are they fairly safe?

Few side effects

  • A single dose, even a large one, has few toxic effects (e.g., poison ivy).
  • Short-term (several days) therapy is unlikely to cause harm (e.g., lupus flare, spinal cord injury).

Serious side effects

  • Chronic treatment at doses exceeding replacement → ↑ likelihood of serious side effects.
  • Abrupt cessation after prolonged therapy → life-threatening adrenal insufficiency.
  • Toxicity tracks duration × dose above physiologic replacement, not one big dose.
CheckOne 40 mg dose of methylprednisolone vs 10 mg prednisone daily for 6 months — which is riskier?
The 6-month course: chronic supraphysiologic exposure causes the serious effects and HPA suppression.

  • Inhaled steroids → oropharyngeal candidiasis (thrush) and dysphonia → use a spacer and rinse the mouth.
  • Long-term steroid users: consider calcium + vitamin D ± bisphosphonate, and Pneumocystis prophylaxis at high doses.
Step 1 trap

A patient on chronic steroids with pneumonia may have only a low-grade fever and few local signs. "Looks well" ≠ no infection.

Q3. What about adrenocortical hyperfunction (too much cortisol)?

Iatrogenic Cushing syndrome

  • Cause: chronic supraphysiologic GC therapy (hyperglycemia, protein loss, central fat, HTN, osteoporosis).
  • Management: lowest effective dose, steroid-sparing/local delivery, alternate-day regimens, and gradual taper — never abrupt withdrawal (the same drug has suppressed the patient's own axis).

Endogenous hypercortisolism

  • Diagnose: ↑ 24-h urine free cortisol, ↑ late-night salivary cortisol, failed low-dose dexamethasone suppression.
  • Definitive: surgery (pituitary or adrenal tumor).
  • Drugs that ↓ cortisol: ketoconazole (steroidogenesis inhibitor), metyrapone (11β-hydroxylase inhibitor); mifepristone blocks GR.
CheckA patient with steroid-induced Cushing syndrome wants to stop prednisone tomorrow. Your advice?
Taper, don't stop. Her adrenal is atrophied; abrupt withdrawal risks adrenal crisis.
Step 1 HY
  • Low-dose dexa: no suppression → Cushing syndrome. High-dose dexa: suppresses → pituitary adenoma (Cushing disease); no suppression → ectopic ACTH or adrenal tumor.
  • Exogenous Cushing: ↓ ACTH, ↓ endogenous cortisol, atrophic adrenals.

"Primary = Pigmented + Potassium high → Pair of drugs" (hydrocortisone + fludrocortisone). Secondary = Single drug.

1A 52-year-old man has headaches and palpitations. BP is 172/104 mm Hg. Serum K⁺ is 2.8 mEq/L and HCO₃⁻ is 32 mEq/L. Plasma renin and aldosterone are both low. He says he eats a large bag of black licorice every day. Which mechanism best explains his findings?

E would cause Cushing disease with high cortisol features, not isolated MR excess with this history.

2A 34-year-old woman with asthma and nasal polyps had severe bronchospasm after taking aspirin for a headache. She now has painful inflammatory arthritis of the wrist. Which agent is the safest choice to reduce her joint inflammation?

D. Glucocorticoids induce lipocortin-1, which inhibits PLA₂ upstream of both COX and lipoxygenase, so they don't shunt arachidonic acid to leukotrienes. A, B and E are COX inhibitors that can trigger aspirin-exacerbated bronchospasm. C is analgesic/antipyretic but has essentially no anti-inflammatory activity.

3A pharmacist is asked to compound a cream for a patient with atopic dermatitis. Which corticosteroid would have no therapeutic effect if applied to the skin?

A. Prednisone is an 11-keto prodrug that needs hepatic 11β-HSD1 to become prednisolone; skin cannot activate it (topical activity 0). B, C, D and E are already in the active 11β-hydroxyl form and work topically.

8A 45-year-old woman started high-dose prednisone 3 days ago for a lupus flare. She is afebrile. CBC shows WBC 15,200/µL with 88% neutrophils and reduced lymphocytes and eosinophils. Which mechanism best explains the rise in circulating neutrophils?
