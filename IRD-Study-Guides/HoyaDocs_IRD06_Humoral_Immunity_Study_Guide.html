Q1. What kind of B cell leaves the bone marrow, and how did it get there? (quick review)

Development happens in the bone marrow; the heavy chain rearranges first, then the light chain.

  • RAG1/2 cut at recombination signal sequences → NHEJ joins the ends. The cut-out DNA is lost as excision circles.
  • Positive selection happens at the pre-B stage: a working μ chain + surrogate light chain signals → proliferation → each daughter rearranges its own light chain (κ first, then λ).
  • Negative selection at the immature B (IgM) stage:
    • Strong, cross-linking self-binding → receptor editing (new light-chain rearrangement) → if that fails, apoptosis.
    • Soluble self-antigen → anergy (leaves, but unresponsive).
    • No or very weak self-binding → survives → periphery (weakly self-reactive "clonally ignorant" cells are allowed out; they add diversity).
  • Result: mature, naïve B cells expressing IgM and IgD leave the marrow and circulate through secondary lymphoid organs until they meet antigen.
CheckTrue or false: B cells only undergo negative selection.
False. Pre-B cells undergo positive selection via the pre-B receptor (μ + surrogate light chain); immature IgM+ B cells then undergo negative selection.
Step 1 HY
  • Mature naïve B cells co-express IgM and IgD by alternative splicing.
  • Excision circles (TRECs/KRECs) are measured in newborn SCID screening.
  • Transitional B cells finish maturing in the spleen and need BAFF survival signals; mature follicular B cells up-regulate CD21 (CR2).
Memory tool

"IgMom and IgDad" → both parents present on the naïve B cell. Heavy chain is "Heavy first": it rearranges before the light chain.

Q2. Where does a naïve B cell actually find its antigen, and on which cells?
  • Only a tiny fraction of the ~half-billion circulating B cells fits any one pathogen → B cells don't hunt through blood. They sit in secondary lymphoid organs (lymph node, spleen, MALT/GALT) and antigen is brought to them.
  • Two "display" cells:
    • Subcapsular sinus (SCS) macrophages: catch opsonized antigen from afferent lymph on their complement receptors; low endocytic/degradative activity → antigen stays intact on the surface → handed to B cells and carried into the follicle to coat FDCs.
    • Follicular dendritic cells (FDCs): sit in follicles / germinal centers; get coated with antigen that migrated to the node → more chances for a rare naïve B cell to find its match. FDCs are not myeloid; "dendritic" just describes their branches.
  • Key point: this is NOT antigen presentation on MHC (that is how T cells see antigen). The intact antigen is just stuck to the surface, so the BCR can bind native linear or conformational epitopes. These cells deliberately don't internalize/degrade all their surface antigen.
CheckWhy would MHC presentation be useless for a naïve B cell?
The BCR recognizes intact, native antigen (including 3-D conformational epitopes). Chopped peptide on MHC is what TCRs read; a BCR raised against the folded protein surface would not bind it.
Step 1 HY

B cell co-receptor CR2 (CD21)–CD19–CD81 binds C3d on opsonized antigen → lowers the threshold for BCR activation. CD21 is also the EBV receptor.

Step 1 trap

FDC ≠ conventional DC. Conventional DCs process antigen onto MHC to prime T cells (cDC2 → CD4). FDCs are stromal (not bone-marrow myeloid) and just hold intact antigen for B cells.

  • The TFH cell was already primed by a dendritic cell (conventional type 2 DC → CD4 T cells) showing the same antigen.
  • Linked recognition: the B cell and its helper T cell must recognize the same antigen, but not necessarily the same epitope.
    • Example: BCR binds a virus surface protein → B cell processes the whole particle → shows an internal peptide on MHC II to the TFH.
    • Lets a B cell make antibody to a non-peptide part of a modified protein (sugar, hapten) as long as it is attached to a protein.
    • The epitope the B cell presents can differ from the one it recognized.
  • CD40 on the B cell binds CD40 ligand (CD40L, CD154) on the TFH → required for germinal center formation, class switching and most SHM. Without it, the B cell can only make low-affinity IgM.
  • Signals the B cell receives:
    • BCR itself → ITAMs on Igα/Igβ → transcription factors such as NF-κB.
    • TFH → TCR–MHC II, CD40L–CD40 (→ NF-κB, Bcl-2 survival), IL-21 (→ STAT3), plus IL-4, IL-6, TGF-β, IFN-γ.
    • IL-21 = the most potent cytokine regulating B cells: drives proliferation / germinal center formation and multiple TH-dependent processes, including switching (IgG1, IgG3, IgA).
    • These cytokines act on the adjacent B cell (paracrine) and on the T cell itself (autocrine).
CheckA B cell's BCR binds a carbohydrate on a bacterial glycoprotein. What does its helper T cell recognize?
A peptide from the protein part of the same glycoprotein, on the B cell's MHC II. Same antigen, different epitope = linked recognition.
Step 1 HY: conjugate vaccines

Linked recognition is the basis of conjugate vaccines (Hib, pneumococcal PCV, meningococcal): polysaccharide linked to a protein carrier → sugar-specific B cell presents carrier peptide to TH cells → class switch + memory. Same idea as hapten–carrier experiments.

Memory tool

Two detectives, same case, different clues. B cell = clue on the outside of the case file (surface epitope); T cell = clue inside (processed peptide). Both must work the same case.

Q4. B cells live in follicles and T cells in the paracortex. How do they ever meet?
  • Before antigen, B and T cells stay in their own regions (B → follicle, T → T zone).
  • After antigen, both change chemokine-receptor expression → migrate to the border between their zones (cortex–paracortex border in the node; follicle–PALS border in the spleen).
    • Activated B ↑ CCR7 + EBI2; activated T ↑ CXCR5.
  • This hugely increases the probability of linked recognition.
CheckA drug blocks chemokine-receptor switching on activated T cells. Which step of the humoral response stalls?
T cells stay in the T zone → they never reach the follicle border → no linked recognition / CD40L help → no germinal center, little switched antibody.
Q5. Once helped, what are the B cell's two possible fates? What is a germinal center?

  • A B cell activated without T cell help (T-independent) becomes only an IgM-secreting plasma cell (see LO 2).
  • Germinal center (GC) = the activated B cell proliferates inside the follicle → turns a primary follicle into a secondary follicle:
    • Dark zone: rapidly dividing centroblasts → SHM (highly mitotic; little surface Ig: no IgD, little IgM).
    • Light zone: centrocytes tested against antigen on FDCs, helped by TFH.

  • In the GC, CD40–CD40L + TFH cytokines are what let B cells become long-lived memory cells and high-affinity plasma cells.
CheckWhy does the body bother with a primary focus if the germinal center makes better antibody?
Speed. The primary focus gives a fast first wave of (low-affinity) IgM within days while the germinal center spends 1–2+ weeks perfecting high-affinity, switched antibody.
Step 1 HY: GC zones
  • Re-entry to the dark zone depends on CXCR4 (ligand CXCL12); light-zone cells are CXCR5+ CXCR4− (CXCL13).
  • Germinal centers = pale secondary follicles in the cortex; reactive follicular hyperplasia = big, polarized GCs with tingible-body macrophages.
Memory tool

Dark = Divide (research lab building prototypes). Light = Look/test (testing room). Centroblasts blast off (divide); centrocytes get checked.

Q6. What happens when CD40–CD40L signaling is missing? (the opening case)

Case: 6-month-old boy, developmental delay, frequent respiratory infections and unusual fungal + bacterial infections. Normal T and NK cells, B cells slightly ↑. IgG, IgA, IgE low; IgM elevated. Which protein is reduced on his T cells? → CD40 ligand.

Hyper-IgM syndrome CD40L

Defect: inherited defect in the CD40L gene (on TFH) → no CD40–CD40L signaling. Most often X-linked recessive → affected boys.

Consequence: no germinal centers → no class switch (and most SHM fails) → B cells make only IgM.

Labs: ↓ IgG, IgA, IgE; normal/↑ IgM; normal T cell numbers.

Infections: especially fungal, parasitic, mucosal (no IgA for mucosa, no IgE for parasites, little IgG): oral-mucosal Leishmania tropica, cutaneous cryptococcosis, recurrent respiratory infections.

Why not the other answers?

CD3 / TCR defect → cripples all T cell signaling (severe combined picture), not an isolated switch problem.

CD4 / CD8 defects → change T cell subsets, not the B cell switch step.

Only CD40L explains normal T cell numbers + failure of class switching with the hyper-IgM pattern.

CheckHyper-IgM patients have lots of IgM. Why aren't they protected?
IgM is low affinity, stays mostly in blood, can't reach mucosa (no IgA), can't arm mast cells/eosinophils (no IgE), and there is little opsonizing, tissue-penetrating IgG.
Step 1 HY: hyper-IgM
  • ↓↓ IgG, IgA, IgE with normal/↑ IgM; severe pyogenic infections early + opportunists (Pneumocystis, Cryptosporidium, CMV; CD40L also activates macrophages).
  • No germinal centers; nodes are not enlarged.
  • Rarer autosomal recessive forms: CD40 (B cell side) or AID deficiency. AID deficiency → giant germinal centers + lymphadenopathy.
Step 1 trap

Ask which cell carries the defect. CD40L (CD154) is on the T cell (X-linked); CD40 is on the B cell. "Reduced on the surface of T cells" → CD40L. A defect inside the B cell's DNA editing (with normal CD40L) → AID.

"↑IgM, ↓IgG/IgA/IgE, boy" → X-linked hyper-IgM (CD40L)"Pneumocystis in an infant with high IgM" → CD40L deficiency"giant germinal centers, high IgM" → AID deficiency

A3A boy with recurrent sinopulmonary infections has ↑ IgM, ↓ IgG/IgA/IgE, and his activated T cells fail to bind a soluble CD40-Fc probe. Diagnosis and inheritance?

Receptor editing (new light-chain rearrangement); if still self-reactive → apoptosis (negative selection).

LO 2T-dependent vs T-independent activationTFH help, iNKT help, TI-1, TI-2

By the end: you can fill in the four-pathway comparison table, explain why LPS gives polyclonal IgM at high dose, why capsules need BAFF for IgG2, why infants under ~2 can't respond to plain polysaccharide, and what iNKT help can and can't do.
Q1. What makes TFH-dependent activation the "full package," and what can't it see?
  • T cells only see peptides on MHC → TFH help is devoted almost entirely to peptide (protein) antigens.
  • Outcome: germinal center → SHM + class switch → high-affinity IgG / IgA / IgE plasma cells + memory B cells.
  • Through linked recognition, a B cell can still target a non-peptide part (sugar, hapten) if it is attached to a protein.
  • But B cells also make antibody (even switched antibody) against pure non-peptide antigens → several TFH-independent routes exist.
Q2. How does iNKT help work, and why is it "half" a response?
  • iNKT = invariant natural killer T cells: an unusual population of mature double-negative (CD4−CD8−) T cells with an αβ TCR.
  • They recognize glycolipid (lipid) antigens on CD1d, not classical MHC.
  • Help (including IL-21) → strong primary response with class switch to IgG, but little affinity maturation and no memory.
  • This is T cell help, but not TFH help → outcome is CSR only. Shows that class switching does not require affinity maturation.
CheckA B cell specific for a bacterial glycolipid switches to IgG but forms almost no memory. Who helped it?
An iNKT cell recognizing the glycolipid on CD1d: CSR yes, affinity maturation/memory no.
Q3. What are TI-1 antigens, and why does dose matter?
  • TI-1 antigens = B cell mitogens, e.g., bacterial LPS, signaling through TLRs.
  • High concentration → stimulates any B cell to proliferate → polyclonal, non-specific antibody of many specificities.
  • Low concentration → only B cells whose BCR binds the mitogen respond (the BCR concentrates the mitogen on that cell so it gets enough TLR signal) → antigen-specific IgM.
  • Usually no affinity maturation or class switch, but rapid (no need to recruit T cell help) → an important early humoral response.
  • TI-1 antigens activate both immature and mature B cells → infants respond.

High-dose LPS

Many clones (any BCR) activated → polyclonal IgM of unrelated specificities.

Low-dose LPS

Only the LPS-binding clone expands → TI-1 antigen-specific IgM.

Q4. What are TI-2 antigens, what is BAFF for, and why don't babies respond?
  • TI-2 antigens = highly repetitive epitopes, mainly on encapsulated bacteria (capsular polysaccharides, some glycolipids). They are not mitogens; no intrinsic B-cell-stimulating activity.
  • Activate mature naïve B cells by cross-linking many BCRs on the same cell (extent of cross-linking is what matters).
  • B cells still need some help: cytokines IL-2, IL-3, IFN-γ from NK cells or non-linked T cells.
  • Default product = IgM. BAFF released by dendritic cells → class switch to IgG2.
    • Human IgG2 is aimed mainly at T-independent antigens and matters most against encapsulated bacteria.
  • Only mature B cells respond; repetitive epitopes inactivate immature B cells (extensive cross-linking).
  • This response does not occur in children younger than ~2 years (most of their B cells are immature) → drives vaccine choice.
  • TI responses occur mainly in the splenic marginal zone (and extrafollicular sites); B cells proliferate without forming germinal centers → mostly IgM, minimal affinity maturation, no memory.
CheckA 15-month-old gets plain pneumococcal capsular polysaccharide vaccine. Why is the response poor, and what fixes it?
Capsule = TI-2; the TI-2 / BAFF–IgG2 response isn't present before ~2 years. A conjugate vaccine (polysaccharide + protein carrier) recruits TFH help → switch, affinity maturation, memory.
Step 1 HY
  • Marginal-zone B cells (spleen) and B-1 cells are the main TI-2 responders → asplenic patients are vulnerable to encapsulated bacteria (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, N. meningitidis) → give pneumococcal, Hib, meningococcal vaccines.
  • Conjugate vaccines: Hib, PCV, MenACWY. Plain polysaccharide PPSV23 is for older children/adults.
  • LPS → TLR4 (with CD14).
Step 1 trap

TI-1 ≠ polysaccharide. TI-1 = mitogen (LPS); TI-2 = repetitive capsule. And BAFF here comes from dendritic cells and gives IgG2, not IgG1.

Memory tool

TI-1 = 1 signal is enough (mitogen) → works at any age. TI-2 = needs age 2 and gives IgG2.

LO 3SHM, class switching and affinity maturationOne enzyme (AID), two DNA targets, one selection loop

By the end: you can define affinity maturation, state what AID does and where, list the repair pathways that turn U into mutations, order the CSR steps, fill in the TFH cytokine → isotype table, and predict what SHM vs CSR each change.
Q1. What is affinity maturation, and how does the germinal center select for it?
  • Affinity maturation = the progressive increase in antibody affinity for antigen during a response, produced by somatic hypermutation (SHM) of the V regions + selection of B cells whose BCR binds better.
  • Iterative loop: mutate in the dark zone → test in the light zone → repeat. Depends on CD40–CD40L with TFH cells.
Effect of a V-region mutationFate of the B cell
↑ AffinityCaptures more antigen → TFH help → survives, proliferates, mutates further
↓ AffinityCan't capture/present antigen → no help → apoptosis
No changeOutcompeted by higher-affinity clones
CheckFollowing V-region sequences of GC B cells from day 6 to day 16, what do you expect to see?
Mutations accumulate in V regions; clones with affinity-lowering mutations disappear (negative selection), and clones with improved binding expand and keep mutating.
Memory tool

Affinity maturation = small-scale Darwinian evolution: random variation (SHM) + selection (light zone). Like writing many drafts of an essay and keeping only the best.

Step 1 trap

SHM itself is random: it makes affinity go up, down or stay the same. The increase comes from selection. "SHM always improves binding" is wrong.

Q2. What does AID do, and how does SHM turn its damage into mutations?
  • Activation-induced (cytidine) deaminase (AID) initiates both SHM and CSR: it deaminates C → U in DNA.
    • SHM: targets the rearranged V(D)J (hypervariable) regions.
    • CSR: targets the switch (S) regions.
    • AID is the only protein in these processes unique to the immune system (expressed in activated B cells); everything else is hijacked DNA-repair machinery.

  • Each repair pathway normally fixes DNA faithfully; here it is subverted to generate diversity.
  • Mutations land mostly in the hypervariable (CDR) loops but also in framework regions. The constant region is untouched → isotype unchanged.
  • V(D)J recombination + SHM together determine the idiotype.
  • Not strictly T-dependent: B cells can do a low level of T-independent SHM, even in the bone marrow before antigen (possibly TLR-triggered) → minor contribution to the repertoire.
CheckWhich DNA-repair pathways turn AID's uracils into mutations?
Base excision repair, mismatch repair, translesion synthesis.
Q3. How does class switch recombination physically change the antibody?
  • CSR changes the heavy-chain constant region → new isotype → new effector function.
  • It does NOT change the variable region → same antigen specificity and affinity. Only the heavy chain switches; the light chain is unchanged.
  • Steps: AID (C → U in switch regions, non-coding DNA upstream of each CH gene) → uracil-N-glycosylase removes U (base excision repair) → AP endonuclease nicks → nicks on both strands = double-strand breaks → NHEJ joins two switch regions → intervening DNA lost as a circle.
  • The deletion is permanent: a cell that switched to IgA can't go back to IgM or to upstream IgG genes.
  • Timing: CSR and SHM run more or less in parallel. CSR can begin before SHM, even before GCs form, and CSR is not required for SHM. Class switching happens mainly in the primary response; memory B cells are already switched and do further SHM on re-exposure.
  • IgD is not a class-switch product; it's co-expressed with IgM on naïve B cells by alternative RNA splicing.
CheckA clone first secretes IgM against epitope X, later IgA against epitope X. Did the switch change the light chain? The affinity?
Neither. CSR swaps only the heavy-chain constant region (new effector function, same V region). Any affinity gain came from SHM.
Memory tool

Same grip, new tool. V region = the hand's grip on the target (specificity); constant region = the tool attached to the handle (what happens next). CSR changes the tool, SHM tightens the grip.

Step 1 trap
  • RAG = V(D)J recombination in the marrow (before antigen). AID = SHM + CSR in the periphery (after antigen). AID deficiency leaves V(D)J intact → B cells present, IgM made.
  • "IgD is a class-switched isotype" → wrong (splicing).

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1A boy with recurrent sinopulmonary infections has enlarged lymph nodes, high IgM, very low IgG/IgA/IgE and normal CD40L on his T cells. Sequencing shows loss of the enzyme that deaminates cytosine in B cell DNA. Which two processes fail, and which earlier process is normal?

A4TFH cells in the gut lamina propria release TGF-β, IL-10 and IL-21. Which isotype dominates?
IgA: all three promote the switch to IgA (TGF-β is the classic driver).

LO 4What antibodies doFab-only jobs vs complement and Fc-receptor jobs

By the end: you can list the four antibody functions, say which need only the Fab end, explain why IgM fixes complement better than IgG, fill in the Fc receptor table, and describe ADCC and IgE-armed mast cells.
Q1. What are the four ways antibodies help, and which part of the antibody does each use?

Neutralization

Fab only. Blocks toxin / virus / bacterial adhesin binding. No other immune component needed.

Complement activation

Fc binds C1q → classical pathway. IgM and IgG.

Opsonization + Fc-receptor cells

Fc binds Fc receptors → phagocytosis, ADCC (NK), mast cell / eosinophil activation.

  • These roles overlap: one antibody can neutralize, fix complement and opsonize at once.
Q2. How does neutralization work, and what antibody property matters most?
  • Antibodies bind viral, bacterial or toxin epitopes and physically block their interaction with host cells ("molecular gloves").
    • Toxins: antibody covers the binding portion → toxic subunit can't enter.
    • Viruses: block attachment and membrane fusion → no infection.
    • Bacteria: block adhesins → no colonization.
  • Works without complement, Fc receptors or other cells.
  • Most important feature of a neutralizing antibody = high affinity (low-affinity antibody falls off and lets the toxin bind).
  • IgG = main neutralizing antibody in tissues (high affinity, diffuses easily through extracellular fluid).
CheckA child is exposed to tetanus toxin. Which antibody function protects her, and does it need complement?
Neutralization by high-affinity (mostly IgG) antibody; no complement or Fc receptor needed.
Step 1 HY
  • Secretory IgA neutralizes at mucosal surfaces and does not activate complement/inflammation. Lymphocytes primed in GALT (e.g., Peyer's patches) get gut-homing receptors → oral vaccines induce gut IgA; injected ones don't.
  • Passive immunity: tetanus immune globulin for unvaccinated wounds; maternal IgG crosses the placenta.
Q3. Why is IgM so much better than IgG at starting complement?
  • IgG and IgM both activate the classical pathway by binding C1q → C1r → C1s (serine protease) → cascade.
  • IgM is pentameric (polyvalent): free IgM in solution is planar and does not activate; once bound to a surface it bends into a "staple" form exposing several C1q sites → one IgM molecule is enough.
  • IgG needs ≥ 2 closely spaced molecules (cross-linking) to bind one C1q.
  • "Natural" IgM exists in circulation before any exposure, binds many microbes → rapid C1q binding and complement activation during the early innate response (inflammation, opsonization, MAC).
CheckHow many IgM vs IgG molecules does C1q need on a bacterial surface?
One surface-bound IgM (staple form) vs at least two neighboring IgG.
Step 1 trap

IgM in solution does not activate complement; only antigen-bound (staple) IgM does. Otherwise circulating IgM would trigger complement all the time.

Memory tool

"GM makes classic cars" → IgG and IgM fix the classical pathway. IgM = a five-armed grappling hook: one is enough.

Q4. What are Fc receptors, and what does each one do?
  • Opsonization = tagging a pathogen for phagocytosis. Phagocytes need Fc receptors that bind the antibody's constant (Fc) region.
  • Isotype-specific Fc receptors are on many cell types; signals can be stimulatory or inhibitory depending on receptor and cell.
  • Fc receptors and complement receptors cooperate: IgG + C3b on a bacterium bind FcγR + CR1 → phagosome → lysosome fusion → killing.

The two key opsonins are IgG and C3b. IgM is not a direct opsonin (no phagocyte Fcμ receptor); it opsonizes indirectly by fixing complement → C3b.

  • Lets the specificity of antibody direct an innate killer. Classic targets: virus-infected cells, tumor cells coated with therapeutic antibody.

IgE and mast cells

  • Mast cells, eosinophils and basophils can be activated by IgE specific for pathogens or (sometimes) allergens.
  • Mast cells are coated with IgE all the time: long-lived IgE stays bound to high-affinity FcεRI even without antigen → ready immediately.
  • Multivalent antigen cross-links bound IgE → rapid degranulation (histamine and other mediators).
  • Mast cells (and eosinophils) attack large pathogens and parasites that can't be phagocytosed, and help maintain immune homeostasis.
  • Mast cells also carry an inhibitory IgG receptor (FcγRIIB): IgG1 binding inhibits mast cell activity → the response to an antigen depends on the relative amounts of IgE vs IgG against it.
CheckIs a mast cell coated with IgE only after allergen exposure?
No. IgE sits on FcεRI continuously, even with no antigen bound, so cross-linking triggers degranulation within minutes.
Step 1 HY
  • NK markers: CD16 (binds IgG Fc) and CD56. Many anti-tumor monoclonals (e.g., rituximab, anti-CD20) work partly via ADCC.
  • IgE cross-linking on mast cells/basophils = type I hypersensitivity; IgE + eosinophils fight helminths (major basic protein).
Step 1 trap

NK cells kill two ways. ADCC needs CD16 + IgG. "Missing self" killing uses inhibitory receptors for MHC I (NK cells have no MHC II receptors). Losing CD16 abolishes ADCC but spares missing-self killing.

ApplyLO 4 application questions

A1NK cells from a patient lack CD16. Antibody-coated virus-infected cells survive in vitro, but MHC I-negative cells are killed normally. Which mechanism is defective?
ADCC. CD16 (FcγRIII) binds IgG Fc; missing-self killing uses different receptors.
A2A patient has both IgE and IgG1 against the same allergen after immunotherapy. How does the IgG1 change mast cell responses?
Dampens them. IgG1 engages inhibitory FcγRIIB on mast cells (and can intercept allergen); net response depends on IgE vs IgG ratio.
A3Within hours of a first-ever infection, complement is being activated on a bacterium via the classical pathway. Where did the antibody come from?
Natural IgM: present before exposure, binds many microbes, and one surface-bound pentamer recruits C1q.
A4An antibody against a bacterial toxin binds with low affinity. Will it neutralize well? Why?
Poorly. Neutralization depends on high affinity; a low-affinity antibody dissociates and lets the toxin reach its receptor.

LO 5B cell memory and repeat exposureWhy the second response is faster, bigger and better

By the end: you can compare naïve vs memory B cells, draw the primary vs secondary antibody curve, explain why the IgM spike happens only once, and explain how existing antibody blocks naïve B cells through FcγRIIB.
Q1. How do naïve and memory B cells differ?

Naïve B cell

  • Half-life of only days; constantly replaced from the bone marrow.
  • Activated only during a primary exposure.
  • Makes the IgM spike, then (with help) switched antibody.

Memory B cell

  • Lives decades or a lifetime.
  • Made in the GC (needs CD40–CD40L + TFH cytokines).
  • Already class-switched; high affinity; does further SHM on re-exposure.
  • T-independent and iNKT responses make little or no memory; no CD40L (hyper-IgM) → no GC → no switched memory.

CheckA nurse gets a hepatitis B booster years after her series. Predict isotype, speed and affinity.
Mostly class-switched IgG from memory B cells, faster and higher, with higher affinity; no big IgM spike.
Step 1 HY
  • IgM = first antibody of the 1° response → IgM serology = acute/recent infection (e.g., anti-HAV IgM, anti-HBc IgM).
  • IgG = main antibody of the 2° response → IgG alone = past infection or immunization.
Step 1 trap

Don't say "class switching only happens with the first exposure" or "memory cells re-switch from IgM." Switching happens mainly in the primary response; memory B cells are already switched and mainly add more SHM.

Memory tool

"M for Mmm, first; G for Gone-before." IgM = Maiden voyage; IgG = Got it before.

Q3. On re-exposure, how does existing antibody shut out new naïve B cells but boost memory B cells?

Primary · naïve B

Binds bare pathogen → activated → plasma cell making low-affinity IgM.

Secondary · naïve B

Binds pathogen already coated with IgG → BCR + FcγRIIB1 co-engaged → ITIM signal dominates → inhibited, no new IgM.

Secondary · memory B

Same FcγRIIB1 engaged, but strong high-affinity BCR signal overrides it → activated → high-affinity IgG / IgA / IgE + more SHM.

  • Result: the body focuses energy on expanding and further maturing the existing, already-optimized memory response.
  • This is why the IgM spike occurs only after the first exposure and every later exposure gives class-switched antibody of increasing affinity.
  • Net effect = "the same Fcγ receptor is inhibitory to naïve B cells but (effectively) stimulatory to memory B cells": it is an ITIM receptor whose brake only wins against a weak BCR signal.
CheckWhich receptor on naïve B cells reads "antibody is already here"?
FcγRIIB1 (CD32), the inhibitory, ITIM-bearing IgG receptor.
Step 1 HY

FcγRIIB-deficient mice develop lupus-like autoimmunity → the receptor is a peripheral B cell tolerance checkpoint.

Buzzwords
"no IgM spike on re-exposure" → naïve B blocked via FcγRIIB"faster, larger, higher affinity" → secondary response"long-lived, switched" → memory B cell

ApplyLO 5 application questions

A1In a knockout mouse lacking FcγRIIB, what happens when antigen already coated with IgG reaches naïve B cells?
Naïve B cells are no longer suppressed → exaggerated, poorly regulated activation (and lupus-like autoimmunity over time).
A2A patient's serum against virus X shows high IgM and low-affinity IgG. First or repeat exposure?
First (primary) exposure. An IgM spike plus low affinity = naïve B cell response; a repeat exposure would show high-affinity switched IgG with little IgM.
A3A third dose of a protein vaccine is given. Compared with dose 2, predict affinity.
Higher. Each exposure lets memory B cells do further SHM and selection, so affinity keeps climbing.
A4Why do adults vaccinated with plain polysaccharide not develop the strong booster pattern seen with protein vaccines?
TI-2 antigens don't make germinal centers or memory B cells → each dose acts like a primary response.

1A 7-month-old boy has Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia after several episodes of otitis media. His maternal uncle died in infancy of infection. Lymph nodes are small. Flow cytometry shows normal numbers of CD3+, CD19+ and CD56+ cells. Serum IgM is elevated; IgG, IgA and IgE are very low. A lymph node biopsy shows no germinal centers. A defect in which molecule is most likely?

D. X-linked (maternal uncle) hyper-IgM: no CD40L → no germinal centers, no class switch, and impaired macrophage activation (Pneumocystis). A and C cause autosomal recessive hyper-IgM; AID deficiency gives large GCs and lymphadenopathy. B: BTK deficiency → absent B cells and all isotypes low. E: RAG defects → no T or B cells (SCID).

2A 4-year-old girl has recurrent sinusitis and marked cervical lymphadenopathy. IgM is high; IgG and IgA are nearly absent. Her T cells express normal CD40L, and biopsy shows giant germinal centers. Sequencing reveals biallelic loss of an enzyme that deaminates cytosine in B cell DNA. Which process is still intact in her B cells?

A. AID deficiency blocks CSR and SHM (and therefore affinity maturation and any switch, including IgG4). V(D)J recombination is RAG-dependent and happens before antigen exposure, so B cells develop and make IgM normally.

3An 18-month-old is vaccinated against Haemophilus influenzae type b with capsular polysaccharide covalently linked to tetanus toxoid. Two years later she has high-affinity IgG against the capsule. Which event was essential for this outcome?

C. Linked recognition: the capsule-specific B cell internalizes the conjugate and shows carrier (toxoid) peptides to TFH cells → GC, switch, affinity maturation, memory. A: repetitive epitopes inactivate immature B cells. B: TI-2/BAFF responses are poor before ~2 y and make no memory. D: MHC presents peptides, not polysaccharides. E: TLR4/LPS is TI-1.

4A 26-year-old man who had a splenectomy after a motorcycle accident develops fulminant pneumococcal sepsis. Loss of which B cell response most directly explains his susceptibility?

E. Capsules are TI-2 antigens; splenic marginal-zone B cells make the IgM (and BAFF-driven IgG2) response, and the spleen clears opsonized bacteria. C: LPS is a TI-1 mitogen of gram-negatives, not the pneumococcal capsule. A, B, D are unrelated to encapsulated-organism defense.

5A 60-year-old woman with E. coli urosepsis has a sharp rise in serum IgM. Much of this IgM binds antigens unrelated to E. coli, including red cell and DNA antigens. Which mechanism best explains this?

B. At high concentration a TI-1 mitogen (LPS) activates B cells regardless of BCR specificity → polyclonal IgM. A would give specific, switched antibody. C (TI-2) still activates only cells whose BCR binds the antigen. D requires a germinal center and weeks. E is not a feature of acute sepsis.

6A 10-year-old with allergic asthma has lung-draining lymph node TFH cells secreting large amounts of IL-4 and IL-13 in response to dust-mite antigen. Which change in his allergen-specific B cells is most expected?

C. IL-4 + IL-13 drive switching to IgE and IgG4 (IL-4 also IgG1). A: IgA needs TGF-β (± IL-10, IL-21). B: IgG2 is mainly the BAFF/T-independent product. D: IgD comes from alternative splicing, not CSR. E: CSR changes only the heavy chain.

7In a germinal center, a B cell acquires a point mutation in a CDR that lowers its BCR affinity for antigen. In the light zone it captures little antigen from follicular dendritic cells. What is the most likely fate of this cell?

A. Low-affinity cells can't present enough antigen on MHC II → no CD40L/IL-21 help → apoptosis. B and E are fates of high-affinity winners. C is a bone-marrow tolerance mechanism for immature B cells. D: switching never goes back to IgM and doesn't change affinity.

8Researchers coat bacteria with equal molar amounts of either pentameric IgM or monomeric IgG specific for the same surface antigen, at low density. C1q binding is far greater with IgM. Which feature best explains this?

E. One bound IgM pentamer (staple form) can engage C1q; IgG needs ≥2 neighboring molecules, which is unlikely at low density. A: IgM per-site affinity is usually lower. B: bacteria don't have Fc receptors. C: planar IgM in solution does not activate complement. D: IgG does activate the classical pathway.

9A patient with B cell lymphoma receives an anti-CD20 monoclonal IgG1. In vitro, lymphoma cells coated with the drug are killed by the patient's NK cells, but killing stops when an antibody that blocks a specific NK receptor is added. Which receptor was blocked?

B. ADCC: IgG Fc binds CD16 on NK cells → perforin/granzymes. A: CD56 is an NK marker, not an Fc receptor. C: FcεRI binds IgE on mast cells/basophils. D: blocking inhibitory MHC I receptors would increase, not stop, killing. E: FcγRIIB is inhibitory and found on B cells/mast cells.

10A 30-year-old with bee-venom allergy completes 3 years of venom immunotherapy. Re-sting causes only a mild local reaction. Venom-specific IgE is still detectable, but venom-specific IgG has risen sharply. Which mechanism best explains the reduced mast cell response?

D. Mast cells carry an inhibitory IgG receptor; IgG engagement dampens activation, and the net response depends on IgE vs IgG levels. A/B: IgE stays bound to FcεRI continuously. C: not how blocking IgG works. E: antibodies don't neutralize histamine.

11A nurse who completed hepatitis B vaccination 8 years ago receives a booster. Two weeks later, anti-HBs is mostly high-affinity IgG with almost no IgM. Which mechanism best explains the absence of a new IgM response?

C. Antibody-coated antigen co-engages the BCR and inhibitory FcγRIIB1 on naïve B cells; memory B cells override it and expand. A: naïve B cells are constantly replenished from marrow. B, D, E are not real mechanisms.

12Mice are immunized with a synthetic bacterial glycolipid. B cells specific for it rapidly produce class-switched IgG, but few germinal centers form, affinity barely increases, and a second dose gives no booster effect. Help from which cell best explains this pattern?

A. iNKT cells (double-negative, αβ TCR) see glycolipids on CD1d and drive a strong primary response with CSR, but little affinity maturation and no memory. B gives GCs and memory. C: TI-2 help would give mostly IgM. D: CD8 T cells don't help B cells. E: FDCs hold intact antigen, not on MHC.

13An unvaccinated farmer steps on a rusty nail. He is given human tetanus immune globulin. Protection depends mainly on which property of the infused antibody?

E. Neutralization needs only the Fab and works best with high affinity, blocking toxin binding to neurons. A, B, D are Fc-dependent functions not required to neutralize a soluble toxin. C: immune globulin is IgG (monomer).

14A B cell clone first secretes IgM against an influenza hemagglutinin epitope and later secretes IgA against the same epitope. Comparing the two antibodies, which statement is correct?

B. CSR joins Sμ to Sα and deletes the intervening constant genes (including Cμ) while keeping the same VDJ. A: the light chain is unchanged. C: affinity changes come from SHM. D: AID and NHEJ mediate CSR; RAG does V(D)J. E: the deletion is permanent.
