Q1. What is a hypersensitivity reaction, and how are the four types organized?
  • Hypersensitivity = an exaggerated or inappropriate adaptive immune response that damages the host's own tissues.
    • The machinery is normal (IgE, IgG, complement, T cells); the target or intensity is wrong (harmless allergen, self, drug, foreign serum).
    • Requires prior sensitization: the first meeting primes the response; damage shows on re-exposure.
  • Classified into four types by what does the damage:
    • Types I–III = antibody-mediated (I = IgE; II and III = IgG, sometimes IgM).
    • Type IV = T-cell-mediated: the only one with no antibody.
    • Type number roughly tracks speed: I fastest (minutes) → IV slowest (days).

CheckWhich hypersensitivity type can be transferred to a naive animal with serum, and which only with T cells?
Types I–III transfer with serum (they are antibody-driven). Type IV transfers only with T cells; serum alone does nothing because there is no antibody involved.
Memory tool

ACID = Anaphylactic (I) · Cytotoxic (II) · Immune complex (III) · Delayed (IV).

Speed ladder: I = seconds–minutes, II = hours–days, III = hours (Arthus) to 1–2 weeks (serum sickness), IV = 1–3 days.

Step 1 trap

Don't classify by organ or symptom (a rash or nephritis can be any type). Classify by the mediator + where the antigen sits: IgE → I; antibody on a fixed antigen → II; soluble antigen–antibody complexes → III; no antibody → IV.

Q2. Type I, step one: how does a person become sensitized to an allergen?
  • First exposure = sensitization → no symptoms. It only "arms" the system.

How the allergen gets noticed

  • Protease allergens break in: house-dust-mite Der p 1 is an enzyme that cleaves occludin in epithelial tight junctions → crosses the airway mucosa → taken up by dendritic cells.
    • Allergen proteases also trigger protease-activated receptors on epithelium → cytokines that attract DCs.
  • Non-protease allergens are recognized by DC innate receptors: TLR4 with MD-2 and CD14, plus C-type lectins (DC-SIGN, dectin-2, mannose receptor).
    • Some hijack these: Der p 2 mimics MD-2 and engages TLR4; others carry bound LPS or mannose glycans.
  • The DC presents allergen peptide on MHC II to a naive CD4⁺ T cell.
    • MHC presentation alone is not enough to activate a naive T cell; it also needs co-stimulatory signals. In an allergic person the APC supplies them.
    • The naive T cell becomes a TH2 cell.
  • TH2 cells → IL-4 → B-cell class switch to IgE; TH2 cells also recruit eosinophils.
  • Secreted IgE is "obsessed with" mast cells: it binds the high-affinity Fcε receptor (FcεRI) on mast cells and basophils → cell is armed.

TH2 and mast-cell cytokines

CheckWhy does a person have no symptoms the first time they meet an allergen?
The first exposure is sensitization: the allergen drives TH2 cells and IgE class switching, and IgE loads onto mast cells. There is no preformed IgE on mast cells yet to be cross-linked, so nothing degranulates.
Q3. Type I, step two: what happens on re-exposure, and why are there early and late phases?
  • Trigger = cross-linking: a multivalent allergen binds ≥ 2 IgE molecules on adjacent FcεRI receptors → receptor aggregation → signaling → degranulation.
    • A single occupied IgE does nothing; receptor clustering is the signal.
  • IgE paradox: IgE has the lowest serum concentration of all immunoglobulins, yet causes anaphylaxis.
    • Most IgE is not free; it is bound tightly to FcεRI on mast cells and basophils → tissues are "locked and loaded."
    • Free serum IgE half-life ≈ 2 days; cell-bound IgE persists weeks–months.

Early phase · minutes

  • Release of preformed granule mediators, mainly histamine
  • → smooth-muscle contraction, vasodilation, ↑ vascular permeability
  • e.g., sudden trouble breathing, hives

Late phase · 8–12 h

  • Recruited eosinophils and TH2 cells
  • Newly made leukotrienes and cytokines
  • → sustained inflammation; explains persistent airway inflammation in asthma after the first bronchospasm

  • Preformed (histamine, tryptase, heparin) act in minutes; newly synthesized lipid mediators and cytokines sustain the late phase.
  • Effector cells: mast cells (and basophils), eosinophils, TH2 cells.
    • Eosinophils are recruited by IL-5 and eotaxin; their granule proteins damage tissue (e.g., airway epithelium).
    • Eosinophils also secrete TH2 cytokines and the enzyme IDO (indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase), which inhibits TH1 cells → keeps the response TH2-polarized.
  • Wheal-and-flare = the hallmark immediate (< 30 min) skin reaction (skin-prick testing):
    • Wheal = raised pale edema from ↑ vascular permeability.
    • Flare = surrounding redness from vasodilation.
  • Triggers (typically soluble peptide antigens, not bound to cells):
    • Breathed / eaten: food, dander, pollen, mold, dust mite, drugs; injected: bee stings.
    • Skin contact: latex, lotions, soaps.
    • Route shapes the picture: inhaled → rhinitis/asthma; oral → GI symptoms/urticaria; IV/systemic → anaphylaxis.
CheckOn a skin-prick test, which part of the lesion reflects leaky vessels and which reflects dilated vessels?
Wheal (raised, pale center) = edema from ↑ permeability. Flare (red halo) = vasodilation. Both come from dermal mast-cell histamine within minutes.
Step 1 HY
  • Anaphylaxis Rx: IM epinephrine first (α₁ vasoconstriction, β₂ bronchodilation); antihistamines and steroids are adjuncts.
  • Serum tryptase = marker of mast-cell activation (confirms anaphylaxis).
  • Biologics: omalizumab = anti-IgE; mepolizumab/reslizumab = anti-IL-5; dupilumab = anti-IL-4Rα (blocks IL-4 and IL-13).
  • Atopy (asthma, allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis) is an inherited tendency to make IgE.
Step 1 trap
  • A first bee sting rarely causes anaphylaxis; the second one does (sensitization first).
  • Wheal ≠ flare: wheal = permeability/edema, flare = vasodilation/erythema.
  • Low serum IgE does not mean "no allergy": IgE lives on mast cells.
Memory tool

"Locked and loaded": IgE pre-loads the mast-cell "gun"; the allergen pulls the trigger only when it bridges two IgE (two fingers on the trigger).

IL-4 → IgE, IL-5 → eosinophils ("5 rhymes with eos-in-hive").

Q4. Type II: what is the target antigen, and what are the five ways bound antibody injures or alters a cell?
  • Mediator: IgG (more rarely IgM) that binds an antigen fixed on a cell surface or matrix.
    • Origin: self-reactive B cells that escaped tolerance, or a normal response to a drug-modified cell.
    • Injury is tissue-specific: limited to wherever that antigen lives.
  • Two antigen origins:
    • Intrinsic = a normal host molecule (e.g., type IV collagen in Goodpasture, ACh receptor).
    • Extrinsic = a foreign molecule (e.g., penicillin) that attaches to a host cell and acts as a hapten.
    • Triggers are small antigens on a cell surface or a receptor.

Q5. How do the Coombs tests detect type II antibodies, and how does Rh disease work?

Direct Coombs

  • Isolate the patient's RBCs → wash → add Coombs reagent (anti-human globulin, e.g., rabbit anti-human Ig).
  • Agglutinates (clumps) → antibody/complement is already bound to the RBCs, where it shouldn't be.

Indirect Coombs

  • Patient's serum + reagent RBCs carrying a known antigen → then Coombs reagent.
  • Agglutinates → free antibody is present in the serum.

Rh incompatibility: hemolytic disease of the newborn (erythroblastosis fetalis)

1st pregnancyRh⁻ mother meets Rh⁺ fetal RBCs, mainly at delivery
→
SensitizedSlow primary response (mostly IgM, does not cross placenta) + memory B cells
→
Next Rh⁺ babyAnti-Rh IgG crosses the placenta → destroys fetal RBCs (type II)
  • First pregnancy usually spared: sensitization happens at delivery and the first response is IgM.
  • Testing: mother → indirect Coombs on her serum (+ Rh⁺ RBCs + anti-human Ig). Newborn → direct Coombs on the baby's RBCs (coated with maternal IgG). Positive = agglutination.
  • RhoGAM = anti-Rh(D) IgG given to the Rh⁻ mother → coats and clears fetal Rh⁺ RBCs before her B cells respond → prevents B-cell activation and memory-cell formation. Passive antibody blocks active sensitization, so it must be given before she is sensitized.
  • ABO-incompatible transfusion is also type II: the recipient has preformed antibodies to the A/B carbohydrate antigens on donor RBCs → antibody binds the RBC surface → complement-mediated hemolysis.
CheckA newborn is jaundiced and anemic. Which Coombs test, on which sample, shows maternal antibody on his cells?
Direct Coombs on the baby's RBCs. The maternal IgG is already bound to his cells, so adding anti-human globulin clumps them.
Step 1 HY
  • RhoGAM timing: ~28 weeks and within 72 h of delivery of an Rh⁺ baby (also after bleeding, abortion, amniocentesis).
  • Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction: anti-ABO (IgM, IgG) → intravascular hemolysis within minutes–24 h → fever, hypotension, flank pain, hemoglobinuria, DIC.
  • Other transfusion reactions: allergic/anaphylactic (type I; anaphylaxis classically in IgA-deficient recipients with anti-IgA), febrile nonhemolytic (cytokines from donor WBCs), delayed hemolytic (anamnestic IgG to minor antigens, extravascular).
  • Hyperacute graft rejection (minutes) = preformed antibody → type II.
Step 1 trap
  • The mother is tested with the indirect test on her serum, not her RBCs (her cells are Rh⁻ and carry no antibody).
  • Drug-induced hemolysis = type II (drug is a hapten on the RBC), not type I, even though penicillin also causes type I anaphylaxis.
Memory tool

Direct = antibody Directly on the RBC. Indirect = antibody In the serum, waiting.

Q6. Type III: how do immune complexes form, deposit, and destroy tissue?
  • Antigen is soluble (not fixed on a cell): IgG binds it in the circulation → antigen–antibody (immune) complexes.
    • Lupus example: after APCs engulf and present soluble DNA/nucleoprotein from dying cells to helper T cells, plasma cells class-switch from IgM to IgG → anti-DNA IgG binds its antigen.
  • Size matters: large complexes are cleared by phagocytes; small complexes formed in antigen excess escape clearance, float around longer and lodge in high-filtration sites.
  • Deposited in blood vessel walls / tissue basement membranes → complement activation (C5a) → neutrophil recruitment.
  • Neutrophils try to phagocytose the deposits, fail, and "save face" by releasing lysosomal enzymes and ROS → vasculitis, tissue destruction.
  • Complement is consumed → serum C3 and C4 FALL. Low levels track active disease (low complement + high anti-dsDNA = active lupus nephritis).
CheckDuring a lupus flare, will serum C3 and C4 be high or low? Why?
Low. Deposited immune complexes activate the classical pathway and consume C3 and C4 faster than the liver replaces them. Falling C3/C4 signals active disease.

Serum-sickness timeline

  • Antibody against the foreign protein appears after ~1 week → while antigen is still in excess, small soluble complexes form that are poorly cleared → deposit → fever, rash, lymphadenopathy, vasculitis, arthritis, nephritis.
  • As antibody rises to antibody excess, large complexes are cleared → symptoms resolve (self-limited once the foreign antigen is gone).
  • Re-administration of foreign serum: a first dose of horse-derived serum induces antibodies against the horse proteins (an anti-isotypic response). Giving the same serum again → those antibodies form immune complexes with it → serum sickness (type III), with faster onset because antibody is preformed.
  • Modern causes: chimeric monoclonal antibodies (e.g., infliximab, rituximab), penicillin, sulfa drugs.
CheckWhy do serum-sickness symptoms fade on their own after a couple of weeks?
Antibody keeps rising until it is in excess; then complexes become large and are cleared by phagocytes, and the foreign antigen is eliminated. No more small complexes → no more deposition.
Step 1 HY
  • Type III renal disease → granular ("lumpy-bumpy") immunofluorescence and subepithelial humps (PSGN); type II anti-GBM → linear IF.
  • Serum sickness: fever + urticaria + arthralgia + proteinuria + ↓ C3 ~5–10 days after a chimeric mAb or antivenom.
  • Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (farmer's lung, bird fancier's) = mixed type III/IV to inhaled organic antigens.
Step 1 trap
  • Complement in active type III disease is LOW (consumed), not high.
  • Arthus vs type I vs type IV at an injection site: type I = wheal in minutes; Arthus = firm, hemorrhagic, neutrophilic in hours; type IV = lymphocyte/macrophage induration at 48–72 h.
  • Type II vs III both use IgG: ask whether the antigen is fixed (II) or soluble (III).
Memory tool

Type III = 3 things stuck together: antigen + antibody + complement.

Lupus drinks complement: "the flare uses up C3 and C4."

Q8. Type IV: why is it delayed, and which T cells do the damage?
  • T-cell mediated, no antibody. Develops over 24–72 h (1–3 days) because T cells and macrophages must be recruited and activated.
  • Can be transferred to another animal by T cells, not serum.
  • APCs carry antigen to CD4⁺ T cells (→ TH1 and TH17) and to CD8⁺ T cells.

TH1 arm

  • APC releases IL-12 → TH1
  • IFN-γ activates macrophages, which:
    • release IL-1 and TNF-α → endothelium expresses leukocyte-recruiting (adhesion) receptors
    • release enzymes and ROS → tissue damage
  • Macrophage-rich lesions: induration, granulomas

TH17 arm

  • APC releases IL-6 (+ TGF-β) → TH17
  • IL-17 recruits and activates neutrophils
  • Neutrophils cause damage similar to macrophages

CD8⁺ CTL arm

  • Kills targets directly:
    1. Perforin + granzymes released from cytotoxic granules → granzymes enter target → apoptosis
    2. Fas ligand on the CTL binds Fas on the target → apoptosis
  • TNF binding TNFR1 = another death-receptor route
  • e.g., β-cell destruction in type 1 DM, acute cellular graft rejection
CheckA lesion biopsy shows mainly macrophages and CD4⁺ T cells; another shows mainly neutrophils with T cells. Which TH subset dominates each?
Macrophage-rich = TH1 (IFN-γ). Neutrophil-rich = TH17 (IL-17).

CheckWhy does poison ivy rash appear two days after the hike rather than within minutes?

Step 1 HY
  • False-negative PPD with anergy: HIV, sarcoidosis, steroids/immunosuppression, overwhelming TB.
  • IGRA (interferon-γ release assay) is not affected by prior BCG; PPD can be.
  • Contact allergy is diagnosed with a patch test (read at 48–96 h).
Step 1 trap
  • Measure PPD induration, not erythema.
  • Poison ivy / nickel dermatitis = type IV, not type I, despite the itch. Latex can do both: immediate urticaria/anaphylaxis (I) or delayed glove dermatitis (IV).
Memory tool

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A man got horse-derived antivenom after a snake bite last year and receives it again today. Several days later he has fever, urticaria, joint pain, proteinuria and low C3. Explain.
Serum sickness (type III). The first dose triggered an anti-isotypic antibody response to horse proteins; on re-exposure, those antibodies form immune complexes with the foreign serum → deposit in joints, kidney and skin → complement consumed → inflammation.
A2A boy is stung by a bee and has only local swelling. Two years later, a second sting causes hives, wheeze and hypotension within minutes. Why the difference?
Type I needs sensitization. Sting 1 produced venom-specific IgE that armed his mast cells; sting 2 cross-linked that preformed IgE → systemic degranulation → anaphylaxis. Treat with IM epinephrine.
A3A patient on high-dose IV penicillin develops anemia with a positive direct Coombs test. Name the type and mechanism.
Type II, extrinsic (hapten) antigen. Penicillin binds the RBC membrane; penicillin-specific IgG coats the cells → complement activation and opsonization → splenic phagocytosis/lysis.
A4Hours after his fourth tetanus booster in a short period, a man has a firm, red, swollen area at the injection site. Biopsy: neutrophils, complement and fibrinoid necrosis of small vessels. Diagnosis?
Arthus reaction (local type III). Injected antigen meets high titers of IgG → complexes in dermal vessel walls → complement → neutrophil influx. Not type I (no wheal in minutes) and not type IV (onset in hours, neutrophilic).
A5Predict: a patient on high-dose prednisone gets a PPD placed despite known prior TB exposure. What might the reading show, and why?
Possible false-negative (no induration). The reaction depends on memory TH1 cells and macrophage recruitment; steroids suppress both (anergy). An IGRA or clinical evaluation is needed.

Q3. What do you do with diseases that use more than one type?

Rheumatoid arthritis III + IV

III: rheumatoid factor (autoantibody to IgG)–IgG complexes in the joint → complement, neutrophils.

IV: TH1/TH17 cells and macrophages in the synovium → TNF, IL-1, IL-6.

SLE III (also II, IV)

Predominantly III: anti-DNA/nucleoprotein complexes → nephritis, rash, arthritis.

II components: autoantibody cytopenias. Some IV contribution.

Hashimoto thyroiditis IV

Has antibodies (anti-TPO, anti-thyroglobulin), but T-cell destruction drives gland loss → classify as IV.

Type 1 diabetes IV

Islet autoantibodies are markers; CD8⁺ CTLs (perforin/granzyme, Fas–FasL) destroy β cells.

  • Rule: classify by what causes the tissue damage, not by which antibodies are measurable.
  • Autoimmune diseases are never type I.
CheckHashimoto patients have high anti-TPO antibody. Why isn't it classed as type II?
The antibodies are diagnostic markers; the gland is destroyed mainly by T cells (CD8⁺ killing, TH1), so the damaging mechanism is type IV.
Step 1 HY
  • Goodpasture target = α3 chain of type IV collagen (anti-GBM) → hemoptysis + hematuria, linear IF.
  • PSGN: 2–4 weeks after strep pharyngitis or impetigo, cola urine, periorbital edema, HTN, ↓ C3, granular IF.
  • Boards phrase type II receptor diseases as "non-cytotoxic" or "antibody-mediated cellular dysfunction."
Step 1 trap
  • Neutrophils ≠ automatically type III: TH17 (type IV) and type II complement-mediated inflammation also bring neutrophils. Look for who called them.
  • Linear vs granular IF is the fastest II vs III discriminator in a renal vignette.
  • Some sources list SLE under type II; teach it as predominantly III with II components.
Memory tool

Ask in order: "E? Fixed? Soluble? T?" → I, II, III, IV ("EFST: Every Fact Sorts Types").

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A woman has fluctuating weakness that worsens through the day; her antibodies cause acetylcholine receptors to be internalized and degraded. Type and subtype?
Type II, non-cytotoxic (antibody-mediated cellular dysfunction) → myasthenia gravis. Graves is the stimulating counterpart (anti-TSH receptor).
A2A young man has hemoptysis and hematuria; kidney biopsy shows linear IgG along the GBM. Type, and is the antigen intrinsic or extrinsic?
Type II, intrinsic antigen: autoantibody against type IV collagen of glomerular and alveolar basement membranes (Goodpasture syndrome).
A3Two weeks after impetigo, a 7-year-old has cola-colored urine, periorbital edema and hypertension. C3 is low; biopsy shows granular deposits. Type?
Type III: post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. Strep antigen–antibody complexes deposit in glomeruli → complement consumption → neutrophilic inflammation.
A4A research islet biopsy from a child with new polyuria and weight loss shows CD8⁺ T cells destroying β cells. Type?
Type IV: CTL-mediated (perforin/granzyme, Fas–FasL) destruction in type 1 diabetes. Islet autoantibodies are markers, not the main effectors.
A5Synovial fluid in RA contains rheumatoid factor–IgG complexes and the synovium is packed with TH1/TH17 cells and macrophages. Which types?
Both III and IV. Complexes → complement and neutrophils (III); T cells and macrophages → TNF, IL-1, IL-6 (IV).

Q2. Which diseases are type II, and what is the antigen in each?

Autoimmune hemolytic anemia

IgG (± drug hapten) on RBCs → opsonization, complement. Direct Coombs +. Penicillin = extrinsic antigen.

Hemolytic disease of the newborn

Maternal anti-Rh IgG crosses placenta → fetal RBC destruction. Prevent with RhoGAM.

ABO transfusion reaction

Preformed anti-A/B antibody → complement-mediated hemolysis of donor RBCs.

Goodpasture syndrome

Intrinsic antigen: type IV collagen of glomerular and alveolar basement membranes → hematuria + hemoptysis, linear IF.

Myasthenia gravis non-cytotoxic

Anti-AChR blocks and internalizes receptors → fatigable weakness.

Graves disease non-cytotoxic

Anti-TSH receptor stimulates → hyperthyroidism.

Pemphigus vulgaris

Anti-desmoglein (desmosomes) → intraepidermal blisters.

Bullous pemphigoid

Anti-hemidesmosome → subepidermal blisters.

Rheumatic fever / heart disease

Molecular mimicry: anti-strep antibody cross-reacts with cardiac myosin.

CheckWhich two type II skin diseases target structural adhesion proteins, and at what level do they split the skin?
Pemphigus vulgaris (desmoglein → intraepidermal split) and bullous pemphigoid (hemidesmosome → subepidermal split).
Q3. Which diseases are type III?

Systemic lupus erythematosus

IgG against DNA and nucleoproteins from dying cells → complexes in kidney (lupus nephritis), skin, joints, serosa, vessels → ↓ C3/C4.

Post-streptococcal GN

Strep antigen–antibody complexes in glomeruli after pharyngitis or impetigo; granular deposits, ↓ C3.

Serum sickness

Antibodies to a foreign serum (e.g., anti-snake serum for a human bite), chimeric mAbs, drugs → systemic complexes.

Arthus reaction

Local complexes at an injection site (repeat boosters) → neutrophils, fibrinoid necrosis.

Rheumatoid arthritis

RF–IgG complexes in joints (type III) plus T-cell synovitis (type IV).

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis

Inhaled spores, fungi, dried fecal proteins → Arthus-like lung reaction.

CheckWhat single lab value links SLE, PSGN and serum sickness?
Low serum C3 (often C4 too): all three consume complement through deposited immune complexes.
Q4. Which diseases are type IV?

Contact dermatitis

Poison ivy (urushiol), nickel, chromate, latex gloves → Langerhans cells → TH1; vesicles at 1–3 days.

Mantoux / PPD test

Memory TH1 + macrophages → induration at 48–72 h.

Type 1 diabetes

CD8⁺ CTLs destroy β cells (perforin/granzyme, Fas–FasL).

Hashimoto thyroiditis

T-cell destruction of the thyroid; anti-TPO/anti-thyroglobulin are markers.

Graft rejection (acute cellular)

Host T cells recognize donor MHC → CTL killing, TH1 inflammation.

Celiac disease (gluten sensitivity)

Gliadin-specific, HLA-DQ2/DQ8-restricted CD4⁺ TH1 cells and intraepithelial CD8⁺ T cells damage villi. Anti-tTG/endomysial IgA = diagnostic markers.

CheckGluten triggers celiac disease. Is it a food "allergy" in the type I sense?
No. Celiac damage is type IV (T cells against gliadin). A true wheat allergy with hives/anaphylaxis would be type I (IgE).

Step 1 HY

Pemphigus vs pemphigoid

  • Pemphigus vulgaris: flaccid blisters, oral involvement, Nikolsky +, net-like (intercellular) IF.
  • Bullous pemphigoid: tense blisters, elderly, usually no oral lesions, Nikolsky −, linear IF at the basement membrane.
Step 1 trap
  • Rheumatic fever is type II (mimicry), while PSGN after the same organism is type III.
  • "Arthritis" alone does not mean one type: RA = III + IV; serum sickness arthralgia = III.
  • Penicillin can cause type I (anaphylaxis), II (hemolysis), III (serum sickness-like) and IV (rash): read the mechanism.
Memory tool

Type II = "antibody to a thing you can point at": RBC, GBM, AChR, TSH-R, desmoglein, hemidesmosome, cardiac myosin.

Type III = "SAP": SLE, Arthus, PSGN (+ serum sickness).

Buzzwords
"Nikolsky +, oral erosions" → pemphigus (II) "tense bullae, elderly" → pemphigoid (II) "hemoptysis + hematuria" → Goodpasture (II) "anti-dsDNA, ↓C3" → SLE (III) "HLA-DQ2/DQ8" → celiac (IV)

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1Two days after hiking, a woman has linear, itchy vesicles on her forearms where she brushed plants. Which skin cell first presented the antigen, and what type of reaction is this?
Langerhans cells presented urushiol-modified proteins → TH1-mediated type IV contact dermatitis. Onset over 1–3 days = delayed.
A2An Rh⁻ woman delivers an Rh⁺ baby. What should she receive, and what does it prevent?
RhoGAM (anti-Rh(D) IgG) within 72 h. It clears fetal Rh⁺ RBCs before her B cells respond, preventing anti-Rh memory and type II HDN in the next Rh⁺ pregnancy.
A3A 70-year-old has tense blisters on the trunk, no oral lesions, and linear IgG/C3 at the basement membrane. Type and target?
Type II, bullous pemphigoid: antibody to hemidesmosomes → subepidermal blisters.
A4A woman develops an itchy rash under her new watch band 2 days after starting to wear it. Type and likely sensitizer?
Type IV contact dermatitis to nickel, a small metal ion hapten that binds skin proteins. Confirm with a patch test.
A5Which of these is the odd one out by type: Goodpasture, myasthenia gravis, rheumatic fever, lupus nephritis?
Lupus nephritis (type III, immune complexes). The others are type II (fixed-antigen antibodies).

1A 32-year-old beekeeper is stung on the hand. Within 5 minutes she develops generalized hives, throat tightness, wheezing and a blood pressure of 78/40 mm Hg. She had a mild local reaction to a sting last summer. Which event most directly initiated her reaction?

C. Type I anaphylaxis: prior sting sensitized her; now multivalent venom cross-links preformed IgE on FcεRI → degranulation within minutes. A is type II; B is wrong (IgE doesn't neutralize histamine, and most IgE is cell-bound); D is type IV (24–72 h); E is type III (hours to days).

2Ten minutes into a transfusion of packed RBCs, a 58-year-old man develops fever, chills, flank pain and hypotension; his urine turns red-brown. A clerical error is found: type A blood was given to a type O recipient. Which mechanism best explains the hemolysis?

A. ABO mismatch is type II: preformed anti-A antibodies (IgM, IgG) bind donor RBCs → classical complement → MAC → intravascular hemolysis, hemoglobinuria. B would be an allergic transfusion reaction; C describes type IV (RBCs lack MHC I); D is a type III idea; E describes febrile nonhemolytic reaction, which lacks hemolysis.

3A 26-year-old health-care worker has a PPD placed. At 72 hours there is 15 mm of firm induration. Which process is chiefly responsible for the induration?

E. Delayed-type (type IV) hypersensitivity: memory TH1 cells recognize mycobacterial antigen and release IFN-γ → macrophages and T cells accumulate → induration at 48–72 h. A is type I (minutes); B and D are type III/Arthus (hours, neutrophilic); C is type II ADCC.

4Eight days after starting a chimeric monoclonal antibody for Crohn disease, a 35-year-old man develops fever, an urticarial rash, polyarthralgia, lymphadenopathy and mild proteinuria. Which laboratory finding is most expected?

B. Serum sickness (type III): antibodies to the foreign (mouse) portions form immune complexes → deposit → complement consumed → low C3/C4. A is the opposite. C points to type II RBC antibody. D is anti-GBM (type II). E marks acute mast-cell degranulation (type I).

5A 29-year-old woman has double vision and ptosis that worsen toward evening and improve with rest. Her serum contains antibodies to the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor. How do these antibodies produce disease?

D. Myasthenia gravis = type II, non-cytotoxic (antibody-mediated cellular dysfunction): anti-AChR blocks and down-regulates receptors. E describes the stimulating form (Graves, TSH-R). A, B, C are types I, III and IV.

6A 27-year-old Rh-negative woman at 12 weeks of her second pregnancy had an Rh-positive first child and did not receive prophylaxis. Which test best determines whether she has anti-Rh antibodies?

A. Free antibody in the mother's serum is detected by mixing her serum with Rh⁺ reagent RBCs and anti-human globulin (indirect Coombs). B would be negative: her Rh⁻ RBCs carry no anti-Rh. C mislabels the test (direct = on RBCs). D tests IgE. E reflects immune-complex disease.

7A 23-year-old man has hemoptysis and rising creatinine. Renal biopsy shows crescentic glomerulonephritis with smooth linear IgG along the glomerular basement membrane. Which statement best describes his disease mechanism?

C. Goodpasture syndrome: autoantibody to the α3 chain of type IV collagen (an intrinsic, fixed antigen) in glomerular and alveolar basement membranes → linear IF. B would give granular deposits. E has the right type but the wrong (extrinsic) antigen. A and D do not fit.

8A 40-year-old farm worker who received several tetanus boosters over the past few years gets another after a laceration. Six hours later there is a painful, firm, hemorrhagic swelling at the injection site. Biopsy shows neutrophils and fibrinoid necrosis of dermal vessels. What is the best explanation?

E. High titers of preformed IgG meet injected toxoid locally → complexes in vessel walls → complement, C5a, mast-cell FcγRIII engagement → neutrophil influx and vessel necrosis within hours. A occurs within minutes and lacks necrosis. C peaks at 48–72 h with mononuclear cells. D is systemic and takes days. B is not a recognized mechanism here.

9A 30-year-old woman with chronic diarrhea and iron-deficiency anemia has villous atrophy and intraepithelial lymphocytosis on duodenal biopsy. She is HLA-DQ2 positive and has anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA. Which hypersensitivity type mainly causes the mucosal damage?

B. Celiac disease: DQ2/DQ8-restricted CD4⁺ TH1 cells against deamidated gliadin plus intraepithelial CD8⁺ T cells damage villi. Anti-tTG IgA is a diagnostic marker, not the main effector (C). A describes true wheat allergy. D and E are not the mechanism.

10Two days after clearing brush, a 19-year-old develops streaks of itchy vesicles on his forearms. Which cell first presented the sensitizing antigen in his skin?

D. Urushiol binds skin proteins; epidermal dendritic cells (Langerhans cells) present the haptenated peptides to TH1 cells (type IV). Keratinocytes (B) amplify later with IL-1, TNF-α and chemokines but are not the initial APC. A is type I; C and E are not APCs for this response.

11A 24-year-old with allergic asthma has an early wheeze after cat exposure that resolves, then a second episode of airway narrowing 9 hours later with sputum eosinophilia. A monoclonal antibody against which cytokine would most directly reduce the cell driving the second episode?

A. The late phase (8–12 h) is driven by recruited eosinophils; IL-5 controls eosinophil production and activation (mepolizumab/reslizumab). B and D drive TH1 (type IV); C is TH17; E is T-cell growth factor.

12A 45-year-old man collapses with hypotension and diffuse urticaria minutes after an IV antibiotic. He is resuscitated with IM epinephrine. Which serum test drawn within a few hours best confirms mast-cell degranulation?

E. Tryptase is a mast-cell granule enzyme released with histamine; a raised level confirms anaphylaxis. A tracks immune-complex disease; B detects antibody on RBCs; C is nonspecific; D detects TB-specific T-cell memory.

13A 9-year-old has cola-colored urine, periorbital edema and hypertension 3 weeks after impetigo. Serum C3 is low and biopsy shows granular "lumpy-bumpy" IgG and C3 deposits. Which mechanism best explains the glomerular injury?

C. Post-streptococcal GN is type III: circulating complexes deposit → complement consumed (↓C3) → neutrophils. A is Goodpasture (linear IF). B is rheumatic fever (type II mimicry). D and E do not fit granular deposits or low complement.
