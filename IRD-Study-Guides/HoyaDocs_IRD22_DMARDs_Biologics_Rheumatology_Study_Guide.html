LO 1Why we use DMARDs and biologics, and how to pick oneDrug category, molecular structure and the disease's dominant pathway

By the end: you can define a DMARD, sort any drug into conventional / biologic / targeted small molecule, decode a biologic's name into its structure, match diseases to their dominant cytokine pathway, and explain why five anti-TNF drugs that are equal in RA behave differently in Crohn disease, psoriasis and uveitis.
Q1. What is rheumatoid arthritis, and why can't we just treat the pain?
  • Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) = an idiopathic (cause unknown) inflammatory arthritis; affects about 1 in 100 people.
  • Early disease can look almost normal: tender, swollen PIP and MCP joints.
  • Left untreated → synovial inflammation (pannus) erodes cartilage and bone → fixed deformity (ulnar deviation, subluxed MCPs) and disability.
  • Pain relievers (NSAIDs, analgesics) make the patient feel better but do not stop the erosions. Because the underlying problem is an overactive immune response, the fix is immunosuppression.
  • DMARD = Disease-Modifying Anti-Rheumatic Drug → it changes the course of the disease (less damage over time), not just symptoms.
  • Most of these drugs work by weakening or "poisoning" the immune response. That is why infection is the shared toxicity of almost all of them.
  • Remember the person behind the pathway diagram: we treat human beings, not arrows and Y-shaped symbols. Every choice weighs benefit vs toxicity for that patient.
CheckA patient's joint pain is fully controlled with naproxen, but X-rays a year later show new erosions. What went wrong?
An NSAID treats symptoms only. The immune-driven synovitis kept eroding bone. She needs a DMARD (usually methotrexate first) to modify the disease.

It is a small molecule taken by mouth, made by chemical synthesis. A biologic is a protein from living cells that must be injected. Tofacitinib is a targeted small molecule.
Q3. What exactly is a "biologic," and what does that mean for the patient?
  • Biologic = a therapeutic protein derived from living material (cells or tissues) used to treat or cure disease.
  • What it means for the patient:
    • You can't swallow it → a protein would be digested in the gut → it must be injected (IV, IM or SC).
    • Expensive → made by living cells, complex to produce.
    • Foreign protein → the immune system may react to it (anti-drug antibodies, LO 3).
  • Biosimilars = "generic" biologics. Because living-cell synthesis is hard to copy exactly, copies are similar, not identical.
    • First US biosimilar: a filgrastim (G-CSF) copy, 2015. Infliximab, etanercept and adalimumab biosimilars followed from 2016; many are now approved.
  • How do you pick one? Four factors: Diagnosis · Effectiveness · Cost · Toxicity.
CheckWhy is a cheaper copy of adalimumab called a "biosimilar" and not a "generic"?
A generic small molecule is chemically identical. A protein made in living cells cannot be reproduced exactly (folding, glycosylation), so the copy is only highly similar.

CheckRituximab works in RA and GPA but fails in ankylosing spondylitis. Why does that make sense?
RA and GPA are B-cell / autoantibody-driven. AS is driven by TNF and IL-17, not B cells, so depleting B cells hits the wrong engine. The target must match the disease biology.
Step 1 HY
  • IL-17A blockers: secukinumab, ixekizumab. IL-12/23 (p40): ustekinumab. IL-23 (p19): guselkumab, risankizumab, tildrakizumab.
  • IL-17 blockers can unmask or worsen IBD and ↑ mucocutaneous Candida (IL-17 is the anti-fungal cytokine).
  • IL-23 from dendritic cells sustains Th17 cells → IL-17 → keratinocyte proliferation in psoriasis.
Step 1 trap

"Psoriatic arthritis" and "psoriasis" are not the same target list. Joints respond to TNF blockers; skin responds best to IL-17 / IL-23 (IL-17/23 > TNF).

Q6. How was the first anti-TNF drug designed, and what did early trials teach us?
  • "We copied nature." Cells normally shed soluble TNF receptors (cut off by metalloproteinases) that act as a sponge for excess TNF.
    • Etanercept = a man-made version of that sponge (TNF receptor 2 + IgG1 Fc).
    • When shedding fails (TRAPS, TNF receptor-associated periodic syndrome) → unopposed TNF → recurrent fevers.
  • Anti-TNF "sponge" trials across diseases:

  • A pathway that drives one disease can be irrelevant, or even protective, in another. Anti-TNF drugs can actually cause an MS-like demyelinating illness or a lupus-like illness.
CheckA patient with RA also has multiple sclerosis. Why is an anti-TNF drug a poor choice?
TNF blockade worsened MS in trials and can trigger demyelination. Choose a different target (e.g., IL-6, abatacept, rituximab).
Step 1 HY

Avoid TNF inhibitors in demyelinating disease (MS) and decompensated heart failure. They can also cause drug-induced lupus (anti-histone / anti-dsDNA).

Step 1 trap

Don't say all TNF blockers "act as soluble TNF receptors." Only etanercept does; infliximab, adalimumab and golimumab are whole antibodies, and certolizumab is an antibody fragment.

Q8. Why do we respect the complexity of immune pathways? (The TGN1412 lesson)
  • TGN1412 = a CD28 superagonist antibody. In mice and non-human primates it seemed to activate regulatory T cells, ↑ IL-10 and give a net down-regulation of T cells.
  • First-in-human trial: 8 healthy volunteers (6 drug, 2 placebo), injections 10 minutes apart, each dose only 1/500th of the highest monkey dose.
  • Within minutes of the last dose: headache, fever, pain, vomiting → all 6 drug recipients went to the ICU with multiorgan failure: lung infiltrates, DIC, renal failure.
  • Cause: a cytokine storm (massive T-cell activation → TNF, IFN-γ, IL-6 …).
  • Lesson: the pathways biologics target are more complex than we understand, and animal data may not predict humans.
CheckWhy give each volunteer's dose 10 minutes apart rather than all at once, and why did that fail here?
Staggering is meant to catch a reaction in one subject before others are dosed. The first patient fell ill about 5 minutes after the last volunteer was dosed, so everyone had already received the drug and the staggering protected no one.
Buzzwords
"CD28 superagonist" → TGN1412 cytokine storm"copied nature / TNF sponge" → etanercept"only monoclonals work in Crohn" → structure affects function

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A 38-year-old has extensive plaque psoriasis with mild finger arthritis. Which cytokine pathways should the biologic target for the skin?
IL-17 and/or IL-23. Psoriatic skin is dominated by IL-17/IL-23 (more than TNF); TNF blockers help the joints but are second-tier for skin.
A2A 30-year-old man with ankylosing spondylitis has recurrent anterior uveitis. His doctor wants an anti-TNF. Which agent is the poorer choice?
Etanercept. All anti-TNFs help AS joint pain, but only monoclonal antibodies (adalimumab, infliximab) control the uveitis.
A3A 74-year-old woman with giant cell arteritis needs to come off high-dose prednisone. Which biologic, and why that one?
Tocilizumab (anti-IL-6 receptor). GCA and PMR are IL-6-dominant diseases.
A4A woman with SLE has rash and arthritis despite hydroxychloroquine. Which biologic targets the dominant lupus survival signal for B cells?
Belimumab (anti-BLyS/BAFF). It removes a B-cell survival signal; it helps skin and joint disease.
A5Predict: a new drug strongly activates CD28 on all T cells. What is the danger in humans?
Cytokine storm (the TGN1412 disaster): uncontrolled T-cell activation → fever, hypotension, DIC, lung infiltrates, renal failure, multiorgan failure.

"J-S-D-A: Joints, Serology, Duration, Acute phase" = 5 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 10. Need 6 to call it.

Q2. What does "treat to target" actually mean?
  • Make the right diagnosis → start a treatment → measure the response → change therapy until things are better.
  • Goal = low disease activity AND low toxicity (toxicity should be absent; the patient should be satisfied).
  • Why: consistent suppression of disease activity is linked to better long-term outcomes.
  • Three areas of consensus when choosing the first drug:
    • There is no best drug.
    • We don't know which drug to start with.
    • No treatment is a bad idea. (So start something reasonable and measure.)
  • You don't need to know everything about a biologic to use it. Know its key side effects, its half-life, and measure the response in your patient.
CheckA patient is "a bit better" on methotrexate but still has 6 swollen joints after 4 months. Treat-to-target says…?
Not at target → adjust: optimize the MTX dose or add a drug (e.g., a biologic), then re-measure. "Somewhat better" is not the goal; low disease activity is.
Memory tool

"Measure like a thermometer, adjust like a thermostat." The score is the thermometer reading; you keep turning the dial until the room (disease) is cool.

Q3. How do we measure whether RA is responding?
  • ACR20 response = ≥20% improvement in swollen joints AND tender joints, PLUS ≥20% improvement in at least 3 of these 5:
    1. Patient global assessment
    2. Physician global assessment
    3. Patient-reported pain
    4. ESR or CRP
    5. Disability
  • ACR50 / ACR70 = the same rules with 50% and 70% improvement.

RAPID3: patient-reported function, pain and global; no joint count.
Step 1 trap

ACR70 ≠ remission. ACR scores are percent improvement from where the patient started; a very sick patient can improve 70% and still have active disease. Remission is defined by an absolute low score (e.g., DAS).

Q4. When one drug isn't enough, is combining better?
  • Etanercept + methotrexate beats either drug alone: higher ACR20/50/70 responses, more remissions and less radiographic progression (erosions, joint-space narrowing).
  • So escalating from one drug → combination therapy is a standard treat-to-target move.
  • Bonus: methotrexate also lowers anti-drug antibody formation against the biologic (LO 3). Biologics should be given with an immunosuppressant, e.g., methotrexate + anti-TNF in RA.
CheckGive two reasons to keep methotrexate going when you add adalimumab.
(1) Combination is more effective than monotherapy. (2) MTX reduces anti-drug antibodies, protecting the biologic from secondary failure.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A 52-year-old has swelling in both knees and both ankles for 3 months, negative RF and ACPA, and a raised CRP. Definite RA?
No: score 3. 2–10 large joints = 1, serology = 0, ≥6 weeks = 1, abnormal CRP = 1. She may still meet criteria later (cumulatively over time).
A2An RA patient did well on adalimumab for a year but now flares despite perfect adherence. Next steps?
Secondary failure (likely ADAbs): ↑ dose or shorten the interval, switch to another anti-TNF, or switch target (tocilizumab, abatacept, rituximab, JAK inhibitor).
A3A patient with "RA" never improved on two different TNF inhibitors. Next step?
Primary failure: switch to a different class / target and review the diagnosis; the disease may not be TNF-driven, or may not be RA.
A4After 3 months of therapy, swollen joints fell 30%, tender joints fell 25%, and pain, CRP and physician global each improved by 20–40%; disability is unchanged. ACR20?
Yes. ≥20% in swollen and tender counts plus ≥20% in 3 of the 5 other domains (pain, CRP, physician global).
A5RA is well controlled on infliximab, but she develops new numbness and optic neuritis. What type of failure, and what now?
Toxicity-limited failure: drug-induced demyelination. Stop the TNF blocker and change to a different target.

LO 3Anti-drug antibodiesWhy patients make them, what they do, and how to prevent them

By the end: you can explain how anti-drug antibodies (ADAbs) cause secondary failure, list the risk factors (stop–start dosing, low dose, no co-immunosuppression, host variation, non-human structure), rank biologics by immunogenicity, and name the prevention strategies.
Q1. What are anti-drug antibodies, and how do they make a biologic fail?
  • Biologics are foreign proteins. They may slip past the immune system, or be recognized as foreign.
  • If recognized → the patient makes human anti-drug antibodies (ADAbs).

  • ADAbs are associated with loss of efficacy (first shown in Crohn disease, also in RA).
CheckName the two ways ADAbs reduce a drug's effect.
(1) Neutralize the drug (block its binding site). (2) Accelerate clearance (immune complexes are removed faster) → lower drug levels.
Q2. What did early Crohn disease experience teach us about dosing?
  • Infliximab was first given as 3 separate doses for fistulizing Crohn; later doses were triggered by disease activity (only when it flared).
  • Compared with regular, scheduled dosing, this stop–start (episodic) dosing produced more antibodies to the drug.
  • Co-administration of immune-suppressing drugs like methotrexate or azathioprine significantly reduced anti-drug antibodies.
CheckWhy would dosing "only when it flares" be more immunogenic than regular dosing?
Each restart is like a new vaccination: the drug appears, disappears, and re-appears, with troughs of low drug level that favor an immune response rather than tolerance.
Memory tool

Stop–start = booster shots. Every restart "re-vaccinates" the patient against the drug.

Q3. Who is most likely to make ADAbs, and against which drugs?
  • Infliximab (IV, mouse/human chimeric): ADAb rates range from 6% to 61%. ADAbs are associated with:
    • Lower dosing strategies
    • Intermittent dosing strategies
    • Non-use of immunosuppressives (no MTX/AZA)
    • Host immune response variation (genetic predisposition)
  • Contrast etanercept (human IgG constant region + human TNF receptor 2): much lower ADAb rates.
CheckInfliximab vs etanercept: which is more likely to lose effect from ADAbs, and why?
Infliximab: it carries a mouse variable region (chimeric) → highly immunogenic (6–61%). Etanercept is built from human parts.
Step 1 HY

Chimeric antibodies (infliximab, rituximab) can cause serum sickness (type III hypersensitivity: fever, rash, arthralgia, low complement ~1–2 weeks after dosing) as well as infusion reactions.

Buzzwords
"6–61%" → infliximab ADAb rate"~50%" → pegloticase ADAb rate"episodic dosing" → more ADAbs"lost response after a good year" → secondary failure
Q4. How do we prevent (or overcome) anti-drug antibodies?

Dose consistently

  • Avoid stop–start or flare-triggered dosing
  • Avoid low-dose strategies

Add an immunosuppressant

  • Methotrexate (or azathioprine) with the biologic
  • Delays / reduces ADAbs

Design the drug

  • Humanized / fully human antibodies
  • PEGylation to shield the protein
  • Some patients will still make ADAbs (host variation). If they do: ↑ dose or shorten the interval to out-compete the antibodies, or switch drug or target.
CheckA patient on infliximab alone asks why you're adding weekly methotrexate "if the infliximab is working." Answer?
MTX reduces anti-drug antibodies so the infliximab keeps working (and the combination is more effective).
Q5. What is the most extreme example of ADAbs in this topic?
  • Pegloticase (gout): a uricase enzyme. Humans lack a functional uricase, so it is a foreign protein.
  • PEGylated to extend half-life and reduce immunogenicity, yet ~50% of patients still form ADAbs, which develop quickly → loss of efficacy.
  • How you spot it: serum uric acid rises again. Urate no longer low = the drug is no longer working.
CheckThree months into pegloticase, a patient's uric acid climbs back to 8 mg/dL. What's going on?
Anti-drug antibodies are clearing the enzyme → loss of effect. Stop the drug (ADAbs also raise the risk of infusion reactions).
Step 1 trap

ADAbs explain secondary failure (worked, then stopped), not primary failure (never worked). A drug that never worked points to the wrong target or wrong diagnosis.

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1Crohn patients received infliximab either on a fixed schedule or only when they flared. Which group made more antibodies to the drug?
The flare-triggered (episodic) group. Stop–start dosing is more immunogenic, and ADAbs were linked to loss of efficacy.
A2Which patient is at highest ADAb risk: (a) infliximab + MTX on schedule, (b) infliximab alone, low dose, given intermittently, (c) etanercept alone on schedule?
(b). Chimeric drug + low dose + intermittent dosing + no immunosuppressant = every risk factor at once.
A3Predict the effect of PEGylating a protein drug on its half-life and immunogenicity.
Half-life ↑ (bigger, shielded, slower clearance) and immunogenicity ↓ (PEG masks epitopes), as in pegloticase and certolizumab pegol.
A4An RA patient whose infliximab response faded is found to have high anti-infliximab antibody titers. What are the two broad options?
Try to overcome them (↑ dose / shorten interval, add MTX), or switch to a less immunogenic drug on the same target (e.g., adalimumab, etanercept) or to a new target.

Q1. How do the "anti-proliferative" DMARDs starve lymphocytes?
  • Lymphocytes dividing in response to antigen need lots of new DNA → they depend on de novo nucleotide synthesis.
  • Most other cells can use salvage pathways → this gives these drugs relative lymphocyte selectivity.
CheckMatch: leflunomide, methotrexate, mycophenolate, azathioprine → DHODH, DHFR, IMPDH, de novo purine synthesis.
Leflunomide → DHODH (pyrimidines) · Methotrexate → DHFR · Mycophenolate → IMPDH (guanosine) · Azathioprine → de novo purine synthesis (via 6-MP).
Step 1 trap

"Which drug blocks pyrimidine synthesis?" → leflunomide (DHODH). Methotrexate blocks DHFR, which depletes THF and so cuts purines and thymidylate; don't label it simply a "pyrimidine inhibitor."

Memory tool

"LEF-t side = pyrimidines" (Leflunomide → DHODH). "MyCophenolate makes no G" (IMPDH → GMP). "AZA = A-Z of purines".

Q2. Methotrexate is the cornerstone. What must you know to use it safely?

How it's used

  • Low dose, once weekly, oral or IM
  • Give with folic acid or leucovorin to reduce side effects
  • Well tolerated: toxicity is common but manageable; lowest discontinuation rate of the DMARDs
  • Antimetabolite and raises adenosine

Toxicity + monitoring

  • Liver enzyme elevations are common; scarring is not → reduce dose and recheck
  • Lung toxicity: idiopathic, usually early (questioned by recent meta-analysis outside RA)
  • Cytopenias
  • Pregnancy category X
  • Monitor CBC + comprehensive metabolic panel (follow liver enzymes)
  • Do not use in renal insufficiency: cleared by kidney → toxic levels develop quickly
CheckAn RA patient with CKD stage 4 is "a perfect methotrexate candidate" per a colleague. Your concern?
MTX is renally cleared; with renal insufficiency it accumulates → marrow suppression, mucositis, hepatotoxicity. Avoid it.
Step 1 HY
  • Leucovorin (folinic acid) bypasses DHFR → "leucovorin rescue" of normal cells.
  • MTX toxicity also: mucositis/stomatitis, megaloblastic anemia. MTX is an abortifacient (ectopic pregnancy).
Buzzwords
"weekly, with folic acid" → methotrexate"cholestyramine washout" → leflunomide"check TPMT first" → azathioprine"bull's-eye maculopathy" → hydroxychloroquine
Q3. What are the "softer" older DMARDs and their one big warning each?

Hydroxychloroquine lupus

Antimalarial; lupus benefit noted in the 1890s. Mechanism unclear (lysosomal pH ↑, TLR signaling ↓).

Standard therapy in lupus; used with other agents in RA. Relatively safe.

⚠ Retinal toxicity → periodic eye exams.

Sulfasalazine RA combo

Mechanism unclear; moderately helpful alone → usually in combination.

Common: GI (nausea, vomiting, anorexia, pain), CNS (headache, fever, light-headedness). Rare: rash, ↑ LFTs, dose-related leukopenia/thrombocytopenia, agranulocytosis.

⚠ Allergy is frequent: many people are sulfa-sensitive.

Minocycline RA, mild

Tetracycline; may act on metalloproteinases; ? antimicrobial benefit. Safe but low potency.

Dizziness, vestibular toxicity, nausea; drug-induced hepatitis.

⚠ Rare drug-induced lupus-like disease with +P-ANCA, fever, arthritis, skin vasculitis.

CheckWhich of these three needs an ophthalmologist on the care team?
Hydroxychloroquine: retinal toxicity is dose- and duration-related and may be irreversible, so periodic eye exams catch it early.
Step 1 HY

  • Sulfasalazine (5-ASA + sulfapyridine) is also used in ulcerative colitis; causes reversible oligospermia.
  • Classic drug-induced lupus: hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid, minocycline, anti-TNF; anti-histone antibodies.
Q4. Azathioprine, leflunomide, mycophenolate: what separates them, especially in pregnancy?

Azathioprine

Prodrug of 6-MP → purine synthesis ↓ + DNA incorporation. Moderately effective.

Used more in lupus and vasculitis (and autoimmune lung disease) than RA; dropped from RA guidelines in 2021.

⚠ GI intolerance, bone marrow suppression; ↑ infection (mostly viral), ↑ malignancy. TPMT deficiency → severe myelosuppression → check TPMT activity first.

✓ Can be used in pregnancy.

Leflunomide

DHODH inhibitor → ↓ pyrimidines. Alternative to MTX in RA.

Follow liver enzymes.

Very long half-life: hepatobiliary secretion + reabsorption → removed with cholestyramine.

✗ Contraindicated in pregnancy.

Mycophenolate mofetil

Noncompetitive, selective, reversible IMPDH inhibitor → ↓ de novo guanosine in T and B cells.

Lupus nephritis, transplant. Not helpful for RA joint disease.

Renally cleared; 80–94% oral bioavailability; 98% albumin-bound.

Q6. How do abatacept, rituximab and belimumab target the adaptive arm?

Abatacept: T-cell costimulatory blockade

  • CTLA-4-Ig fusion protein binds CD80/86 (B7) on the APC → the T cell gets signal 1 (MHC–TCR) without signal 2 (CD28) → no full T-cell activation.
  • Clinical profile: IV or SC · slow onset · well tolerated · moderately effective.

Rituximab: B-cell depletion

  • Chimeric anti-CD20 mAb → kills CD20⁺ B cells by complement-mediated lysis and antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) (± apoptosis).
  • Prolonged effect: B cells stay depleted for 6 months or more.
  • Works in: RA, GPA (granulomatosis with polyangiitis), some poly/dermatomyositis, lupus, anti-NMO antibody syndrome, myasthenia gravis, Graves ophthalmopathy, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, ITP, pemphigus.
  • Does not work in: psoriatic arthritis or ankylosing spondylitis.
  • Side effects: infusion reactions (mild to severe) · hepatitis B reactivation → check hepatitis B core antibody, treat if positive · progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) from JC virus reactivation (rare but fatal).

Belimumab: starving B cells

  • Anti-BLyS/BAFF (B-lymphocyte stimulator) mAb. BLyS keeps B cells alive → blocking it removes the survival / activation signal → B cells die.
  • Lupus: the first new lupus drug in about 50 years. Helps skin and joint disease; appears to reduce long-term internal organ disease.
CheckWhy does serum IgG fall only modestly after rituximab even though B cells disappear?
Plasma cells lack CD20, so existing long-lived plasma cells keep secreting antibody.
Step 1 trap
  • Abatacept is CTLA-4-Ig (a CTLA-4 mimic that blocks costimulation). An anti-CTLA-4 antibody (ipilimumab) does the opposite: it removes the brake and unleashes T cells for cancer.
  • Rituximab kills CD20⁺ cells; belimumab starves them of a survival signal.
Step 1 HY
  • Rituximab is also first-line for B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma and CLL.
  • PML (JC virus) also follows natalizumab and AIDS (CD4 <200): non-enhancing white-matter lesions, progressive focal deficits.
Memory tool

"AbataCEPT interCEPTs B7." "RituXimab = X out CD20." "BeLyMumab blocks BLyS."

Q7. IL-1 vs IL-6 blockers: same family, different jobs and toxicities?

IL-1 and IL-6 drive CRP production and are involved in many inflammatory conditions.

IL-1 inhibitors: anakinra, canakinumab, rilonacept

Key idea: block IL-1–mediated inflammation.

  • Uses: RA (modest benefit), recurrent pericarditis, systemic JIA (Still disease) and other IL-1-dependent orphan diseases, acute gout flares (costly)
  • Toxicity: injection-site reactions (very common, painful with anakinra), ↑ bacterial infections, TB (screen; lower risk than TNF), neutropenia, hypersensitivity, headache / flu-like symptoms, possible hepatotoxicity

IL-6 inhibitors: tocilizumab, sarilumab

Key idea: IL-6 blockade controls systemic inflammatory states.

  • Uses: RA, giant cell arteritis + PMR, systemic-onset JIA, COVID-19 "inflammatory" phase, CAR-T cytokine release syndrome, Castleman disease
  • Toxicity: ↑ infection (esp. respiratory; screen for TB), ↑ liver enzymes, neutropenia / thrombocytopenia, ↑ lipids (cholesterol, TG), GI perforation (esp. with diverticulitis), infusion / injection reactions
  • Because IL-6 drives CRP, a patient on tocilizumab can have a serious infection with a normal CRP (masked).
CheckDays after CAR-T infusion for lymphoma, a patient has fever 40 °C, hypotension and hypoxia. Which cytokine blocker?
Tocilizumab: cytokine release syndrome is driven largely by IL-6.
Step 1 trap

IL-6 blockers are not "the well-tolerated one." That label belongs to abatacept. IL-6 inhibitors carry a long list: infection, LFTs, cytopenias, lipids, GI perforation.

Step 1 HY

IL-1 is the main driver of autoinflammatory periodic fever syndromes (CAPS, FMF). Urate crystals → NLRP3 inflammasome → caspase-1 → IL-1β → neutrophil influx (gout flare).

"painful injection-site welts" → anakinra"diverticulitis + perforation" → IL-6 blocker"CAR-T cytokine storm" → tocilizumab"recurrent pericarditis, Still disease" → IL-1 blocker
Q8. JAK inhibitors: how do they work, and why don't they cause SCID?
  • Tofacitinib (also baricitinib, upadacitinib) = oral targeted small molecules that block Janus kinases.
  • No JAK → no STAT phosphorylation → ↓ cytokine-driven gene expression → many cytokines (including those that steer CD4⁺ T-cell differentiation) are muted at once.
  • Selectivity varies: tofacitinib hits JAK1/3 (and 2); others are more JAK1-selective.
  • Congenital JAK3 absence → SCID ("bubble boy"). The drugs give only partial inhibition, so this level of immune suppression is not reached.
  • Side effects: immune suppression (infection) and ↑ cholesterol.
  • Uses: RA, psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, atopic dermatitis, alopecia areata, ulcerative colitis.
CheckA needle-phobic RA patient fails methotrexate. Which targeted option avoids injections?
A JAK inhibitor (e.g., tofacitinib), which is taken orally.
Step 1 HY

JAK inhibitor boxed warnings: serious infection (esp. herpes zoster reactivation), thrombosis (DVT/PE), malignancy, major cardiovascular events. "-tinib" = kinase inhibitor.

Step 1 trap

JAK inhibitors are listed with biologics by target, but they are not biologics: oral, small, synthetic. Don't pick them as "must be injected."

Q9. Pegloticase and IVIG: the two odd ones out

Pegloticase (gout)

  • Gout = deposits of uric acid
  • Uricase converts uric acid → allantoin (soluble, excreted)
  • PEGylated to extend half-life and reduce immunogenicity
  • High ADAb rate (~50%) → loss of efficacy; monitor serum urate

IVIG

  • Pooled polyclonal IgG from thousands of donors, given IV
  • Replacement in antibody deficiency; immunomodulation (Kawasaki, ITP, Guillain-Barré, myasthenic crisis, dermatomyositis)
  • Works by Fc-receptor blockade, neutralizing autoantibodies, scavenging complement, and saturating FcRn (↑ IgG clearance)
  • AEs: infusion reactions, headache / aseptic meningitis, thrombosis, renal injury, anaphylaxis in IgA deficiency
CheckWhy can a patient with selective IgA deficiency have anaphylaxis to IVIG?
They may have anti-IgA antibodies (IgE/IgG); trace IgA in the pooled product triggers the reaction.
Step 1 HY
  • Pegloticase is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency (urate oxidation makes H₂O₂ → hemolysis, methemoglobinemia).
  • Other gout drugs: allopurinol / febuxostat (xanthine oxidase inhibitors), probenecid (↓ proximal tubule urate reabsorption); flares → NSAIDs, glucocorticoids, colchicine (tubulin → ↓ neutrophil chemotaxis), IL-1 blockers.
