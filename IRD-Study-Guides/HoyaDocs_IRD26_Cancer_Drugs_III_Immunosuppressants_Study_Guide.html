LO 1How the immune system sees (and misses) cancerNeoantigens, mutational load and the suppressive tumor microenvironment

By the end: you can explain why CD8⁺ T cells are the main anti-tumor effectors, rank cancers by mutational load, walk through the cancer-immunity cycle, list how tumors escape, and explain why all of this makes immunotherapy possible.
Q1. What is the "immune" way of thinking about why cancer happens?
  • Cells pick up genetic mutations over time (with ageing) → most mutant cells are found and destroyed.
  • Cancer = a failure of the immune system to eradicate these mutated cells.
    • Two questions follow: why did it fail? and can we fix it? → that fix is immunotherapy.
  • Immunotherapy does not attack the tumor itself; it helps the patient's own immune system kill tumor cells. In a significant fraction of patients the responses are long-lasting and apparently curative, which classic chemotherapy rarely achieves in metastatic disease.

Cancer immunoediting: the three E's

CheckWhy do tumors arising in immunocompromised hosts tend to be strongly immunogenic?
With no immune pressure, there is no selection for evasion mutations, so the tumor keeps all its "visible" antigens (it never had to be edited).

CheckWhy would a cancer with 10× more non-synonymous mutations be a better target for T cells?
More amino-acid changes → more neoantigens → more chances that some peptide is presented on MHC I and recognized as foreign.
Q3. Which cancers carry the most mutations?
  • Highest: melanoma (UV damage) and lung cancers (tobacco: small cell, adeno, squamous).
  • Lowest: pancreas, breast, prostate.
  • Why it matters → more mutations = more neoantigens = better response to checkpoint inhibitors (LO 3).
Memory tool

"Sun and smoke make the most mistakes." Melanoma (UV) and lung (tobacco) top the mutation chart; the "quiet" trio is P-B-P: pancreas, breast, prostate.

Step 1 trap

Intuition says "more mutations = harder to treat." For immunotherapy it is the reverse: more mutations → more foreign targets → better response.

Q4. How does a T cell get from "antigen exists" to "tumor cell dead"? (the cancer-immunity cycle)
  1. Antigen release from dying cancer cells.
  2. Presentation by dendritic cells (APCs capture and process the antigen).
  3. Traffic to the lymph node.
  4. T-cell priming and activation: TCR binds MHC–peptide (signal 1) + CD28 binds B7 (CD80/86) on the APC (signal 2). ← CTLA-4 brakes here.
  5. Activated effector T cells infiltrate the tumor.
  6. T-cell killing: TCR recognizes MHC–neoantigen → perforin + granzyme. ← PD-1/PD-L1 brakes here. Killing releases more antigen → next round.
CheckAt which step of the cycle does each checkpoint act?
CTLA-4 → step 4 (priming in the lymph node, early). PD-1/PD-L1 → step 6 (killing in the tumor, late).
Q5. How do tumors switch the killers off? (escape and the tumor microenvironment)
  • NK cells and CD8⁺ T cells both kill tumor cells, and both carry activating and inhibitory receptors. When both are engaged, the inhibitory signal wins.
    • Tumors exploit this: they engage inhibitory receptors (e.g., PD-L1 → PD-1) to switch the killers off.
  • The tumor microenvironment (TME) is full of suppressor cells: tumor-associated macrophages, cancer-associated fibroblasts, Tregs, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). Together they:
    • inhibit cytolytic granule release
    • block the anti-tumor M1 macrophage phenotype
    • inhibit tumor-antigen presentation
    • inhibit B- and T-cell responses
  • Tumor cells also upregulate PD-L1 on their surface.

Tumor evasion strategies (memorize the verbs)

Hide

↓ MHC I, ↓ adhesion and costimulatory molecules → T cells can't see the cell.

Subvert / suppress

Release TGF-β, IL-10, IDO; recruit Tregs → immunosuppressive cytokines.

Defend

Directly deactivate tumor-specific T cells (e.g., PD-L1 → PD-1 exhaustion).

Shield

Physical barrier (stroma, privileged site) keeps tumor-infiltrating T cells from reaching tumor cells.

Overwhelm

Tumor cells simply outnumber the T cells.

Treated as self / modulate

Antigen shown without costimulation → anergy or Tregs; antibody pressure selects antigen-loss variants.

Inflamed vs non-inflamed tumors

Inflamed ("hot")

  • Immune cells arrive at the tumor but are switched off.
  • Best candidates for checkpoint inhibitors → just release the brake.

Non-inflamed ("cold")

  • Immune cells do not arrive at the tumor.
  • Checkpoint blockade alone works poorly → needs new strategies (vaccines, combinations; area of research).
CheckA tumor clone loses all MHC class I. Which killer cell is now its main threat?
NK cells: losing MHC I removes the NK inhibitory signal ("missing self"), so NK cells are freed to kill.
Step 1 HY
  • Missing self: ↓ MHC I escapes CD8⁺ T cells but triggers NK cells (KIR inhibitory receptors no longer engaged).
  • Tumor markers AFP (hepatocellular, yolk sac) and CEA (colorectal) are TAAs, used for monitoring, not screening.
  • Immunodeficient / transplant patients get more virus-driven cancers (EBV lymphoma, HPV, Kaposi sarcoma HHV-8).
Step 1 trap

CEA, AFP, HER2 are not tumor-specific: they occur on normal (fetal or adult) cells too. True TSAs come from mutant proteins, viral proteins, or silent genes like MAGE.

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A tumor biopsy shows dense CD8⁺ T cells packed at the tumor edge, yet the tumor is growing. Inflamed or non-inflamed, and what is the most promising drug class?
Inflamed ("hot") tumor → checkpoint inhibitor. The T cells arrived but are switched off (likely PD-L1/PD-1); removing the brake lets them kill.
A2Which would you expect to carry more neoantigens: a smoker's squamous cell lung cancer or a pancreatic adenocarcinoma? Why?
Squamous lung cancer. Tobacco mutagens produce a very high mutational load; pancreatic cancer is among the lowest.
A3A tumor secretes large amounts of TGF-β and IL-10 and recruits Tregs. Which evasion strategy is this?
Subverting / suppressing the immune response with immunosuppressive cytokines and regulatory cells.
A4Why does the anti-tumor response depend mainly on MHC class I rather than MHC class II?
Tumor cells are mostly non-APCs: they show their own (mutated) cytosolic proteins on MHC I, which is read by CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells that kill directly.

LO 2How checkpoint inhibitors workAnti-CTLA-4, anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 release the brakes

By the end: you can draw both checkpoints, say which cell carries each molecule, place each brake in time and space (lymph node vs tumor), name the drug for each target, and explain why they are combined.
Q1. What is an "immune checkpoint," and why does the body have them?
  • T-cell activation is controlled by positive costimulatory signals (accelerators, e.g., CD28) and negative co-inhibitory signals (brakes, e.g., CTLA-4, PD-1).
  • Brakes exist to limit the response and prevent autoimmunity.
    • Proof: PD-1 knockout mice develop autoimmunity: nephritis, arthritis, diabetes, myocarditis, dilated cardiomyopathy → PD-1 is a physiological negative regulator.
  • Many checkpoints are known (on T cells, APCs and cancer cells), but the approved drugs target two: CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1.
  • Checkpoint inhibitors are antibodies that block the brakes, not drugs that stimulate the accelerator.
Q2. How do CTLA-4 and PD-1 each brake a T cell?

CTLA-4 = early brake

  • After priming, activated T cells upregulate CTLA-4. Also constitutive on Tregs.
  • CTLA-4 binds B7 (CD80/86) on the APC with higher affinity than CD28 → steals B7 → no signal 2 = early co-inhibition.
  • Location: lymph node, priming phase.
  • Ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4) → priming restored + Treg suppression blunted (less IL-10, TGF-β).

PD-1 / PD-L1 = late brake

  • Activated T cells upregulate PD-1.
  • Tumor cells (and some immune cells) upregulate PD-L1 → binds PD-1 → (via SHP2) dampens TCR signaling → T-cell exhaustion = late co-inhibition.
  • Location: tumor microenvironment, effector phase.
  • Nivolumab, pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) or atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) → T cells kill again.

CheckWhich of these molecules sits on the tumor cell: CTLA-4, PD-1, PD-L1, CD28?
Only PD-L1. CTLA-4, PD-1 and CD28 are all on T cells (CTLA-4 also on Tregs).
Step 1 trap
  • CTLA-4 is not on tumor cells; it is on activated T cells and Tregs, and competes with CD28 for B7.
  • PD-1 is on the T cell, not the APC; PD-L1 is the ligand on tumor cells.
  • Ipilimumab vs abatacept: ipilimumab is an antibody that blocks CTLA-4 → ↑ T-cell activation (cancer). Abatacept is a CTLA-4–Ig fusion protein that binds B7 → blocks CD28 costimulation → ↓ T-cell activation (rheumatoid arthritis). Same molecule, opposite direction.
Memory tool
  • "CTLA-4 = Clamps T cells in the Lymph node, Ahead (early)."
  • "PD-1 = Peripheral Death": the brake used out in the tissue/tumor, late.
  • Drug names: IPIlimumab → Initial brake; NIVO / PEMBRO → PD-1; ATEZO → Attaches to the ligand (L1).
Buzzwords
"outcompetes CD28 for B7" → CTLA-4"T-cell exhaustion" → PD-1/PD-L1"releases the brakes" → checkpoint inhibitor"CTLA-4–Ig" → abatacept (suppresses)
Q3. What do the drugs achieve, and why give two together?
  • Blocking the brakes gives a sustained T-cell response: enhanced priming (anti-CTLA-4) + restored effector killing in the tumor (anti-PD-1/PD-L1), with ↑ cytokine production and ↑ T-cell proliferation.
  • Anti-CTLA-4 + anti-PD-1 are usually given together: they hit different checkpoints (early vs late) → enhanced efficacy.
    • Approved combinations: melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, MSI-H colorectal cancer.
    • Trade-off: combination ↑ survival over either drug in melanoma but also ↑ immune-related adverse events (LO 4).
  • Checkpoint inhibitors are also being combined with chemotherapy, targeted drugs and vaccines; the best sequence is still being tested in trials.
CheckWhy is combining ipilimumab with nivolumab more logical than combining nivolumab with pembrolizumab?
Nivolumab and pembrolizumab hit the same target (PD-1). Ipilimumab + nivolumab block two different brakes (CTLA-4 early, PD-1 late).
Step 1 HY
  • Checkpoint inhibitors: ipilimumab (CTLA-4); nivolumab, pembrolizumab, cemiplimab (PD-1); atezolizumab, durvalumab, avelumab (PD-L1).
  • All are monoclonal antibodies (-mab), given IV; their main toxicity class is autoimmunity, not myelosuppression.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A biopsy shows CD8⁺ T cells expressing PD-1 at the tumor edge and tumor cells strongly expressing PD-L1, yet the tumor grows. Which drug class fits best?
Anti-PD-1 (nivolumab, pembrolizumab) or anti-PD-L1 (atezolizumab). T cells are present but switched off by PD-L1/PD-1; blocking the interaction lets them kill.
A2Predict the effect of ipilimumab on regulatory T cells in a tumor-draining lymph node.
Treg suppression is blunted. Tregs carry CTLA-4 and use it (with IL-10, TGF-β) to restrain priming; blocking it lets more effector T cells be primed.
A3A rheumatologist and an oncologist both prescribe drugs "involving CTLA-4." Explain the opposite effects.
Abatacept (CTLA-4–Ig) mimics CTLA-4 and soaks up B7 → ↓ costimulation → immunosuppression for RA. Ipilimumab blocks CTLA-4 → more B7 free for CD28 → ↑ T-cell activation against cancer.
A4PD-1 knockout mice develop myocarditis and nephritis. What does that predict about anti-PD-1 therapy in patients?
Blocking PD-1 removes a normal brake on self-reactive T cells → expect autoimmune toxicity (e.g., myocarditis, thyroiditis) in some patients.

LO 3Clinical uses and predictors of responseWhich cancers, and which patients, respond to checkpoint blockade

By the end: you can list the main indications for each checkpoint inhibitor, quote the landmark melanoma and Hodgkin results, and use TMB, mismatch-repair status, PD-L1 and the microbiome to predict who responds.
Q1. How dramatic can a response be?
  • Case: metastatic melanoma with a bulky, necrotic chest-wall mass → one dose of ipilimumab + nivolumab → 3 weeks later the tumor had "disappeared".
  • In trials of the combination, 53% of patients had ≥80% tumor shrinkage.
  • Stage IV melanoma 5-year survival: ~16% (2004) → >50% (2024).
  • Checkpoint inhibitors are approved for >20 advanced cancers and are in trials (often in combinations) for most others.
Q2. Which cancers does each drug treat?

Pembrolizumab (PD-1)Melanoma, NSCLC, bladder, cervical, head & neck, Hodgkin, MSI-H / dMMR solid tumors (tumor-agnostic)

CheckWhich chromosomal region is amplified in Hodgkin RS cells, and what does it encode?
9p24.1, which carries PD-L1, PD-L2 (and JAK2).
Q4. What predicts response? (TMB and mismatch repair)
  • Tumor mutational burden (TMB): more mutations → more neoantigens → better response to anti-PD-1 (and anti-CTLA-4).
    • Melanoma patients with >100 mutations had clearly longer survival on ipilimumab than those with ≤100.
    • NSCLC with high mutation load (smoking-type C→A transversions) and metastatic prostate cancers with high TMB responded better.
    • Across 27 tumor types, objective response rate to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 rises with median coding mutations per megabase.
  • DNA mismatch-repair deficiency (dMMR) = MSI-high → hypermutated tumors → respond very well to anti-PD-1.
    • Includes colorectal, pancreatic, endometrial, ovarian cancers (e.g., Lynch syndrome; loss of MLH1, MSH2).
  • PD-L1 expression on the tumor (Hodgkin = extreme example) favors anti-PD-1/PD-L1.
CheckMetastatic pancreatic cancer rarely responds to anti-PD-1. Which minority of pancreatic cancers does?
The mismatch-repair-deficient (MSI-H) ones: hypermutation creates enough neoantigens.
Q5. How can gut bacteria change the response?
  • A diverse gut microbiome trains the immune system → better response and survival after immunotherapy.
  • Being tested: fecal microbiota transplantation to "upgrade" low-diversity patients; broad-spectrum antibiotics around treatment may blunt responses.
Q6. The scan got worse after starting immunotherapy. Is the drug failing?
  • Not necessarily: pseudoprogression = apparent tumor enlargement early in treatment caused by T cells and NK cells infiltrating the tumor, not true growth → often followed by shrinkage.
  • Practical point: confirm with a repeat scan before stopping a checkpoint inhibitor.
Step 1 HY
  • Pembrolizumab was the first tumor-agnostic approval: any MSI-H / dMMR solid tumor.
  • Lynch syndrome (HNPCC): germline MMR mutation (MLH1, MSH2) → MSI-H colorectal + endometrial cancer → checkpoint-responsive.
  • Hodgkin RS cells: CD15⁺/CD30⁺, EBV-associated, PD-L1 high.
Step 1 trap

New growth on a scan 2–6 weeks into checkpoint therapy may be pseudoprogression (immune infiltrate), not resistance. Don't pick "stop therapy, switch drugs" if the stem stresses a well patient with early mild enlargement.

Memory tool

The 4 "M"s of response: Mutations (TMB), Mismatch repair loss, Marker PD-L1, Microbiome.

Buzzwords
"MSI-high" → anti-PD-1"9p24.1, Reed–Sternberg" → nivolumab"fecal transplant" → microbiome boosts response"tumor grows then shrinks" → pseudoprogression

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1A 52-year-old with metastatic colorectal cancer has an MSI-high tumor with loss of MLH1. Which immunotherapy, and why?
Anti-PD-1 (pembrolizumab or nivolumab). Mismatch-repair deficiency → very high mutational burden → many neoantigens → strong T-cell response once PD-1 is blocked.
A2Why is mismatch-repair-proficient metastatic pancreatic cancer usually unresponsive to anti-PD-1, while melanoma often responds?
Pancreatic cancer has a low mutational burden → few neoantigens. Melanoma (UV-induced) has one of the highest → many targets once the brake is off.
A3Two melanoma patients start anti-PD-1. One just finished a long course of broad-spectrum antibiotics. Who is predicted to do worse?
The antibiotic-treated patient: reduced gut-microbiome diversity is linked to shorter progression-free survival on anti-PD-1.
A4A young man with Hodgkin lymphoma relapses after chemotherapy and stem-cell transplant. Which checkpoint inhibitor has an ~87% response here, and why?
Nivolumab (anti-PD-1). RS cells overexpress PD-L1/PD-L2 (9p24.1 amplification, EBV) and depend on the PD-1 brake.

LO 4Immune-related adverse events (irAEs)"Almost every -itis": what checkpoint blockade does to normal tissue

By the end: you can explain why irAEs happen, list the classic organ patterns for anti-PD-1 vs anti-CTLA-4, rank irAEs by frequency and lethality, and outline first-line management.
Q1. Why does releasing the brakes cause autoimmunity?
  • The unleashed T cells also recognize non-mutated (shared, normal) antigens → some tumor "antigens" are really ordinary self proteins also found on healthy cells.
  • The normal brake against autoimmunity (CTLA-4, PD-1) is pharmacologically removed → nothing stops self-reactive T cells.
  • Result: destruction of tissue around the tumor and autoimmune inflammation anywhere ("the drug gave me almost every '-itis' you can get": uveitis, arthritis-like joint pain, pneumonitis, hepatitis).
  • Overall, severe adverse effects are surprisingly few compared with chemotherapy, but some are lethal.
  • irAEs are a sign the immune system is active: patients who develop irAEs often have better tumor responses (more toxicity ↔ more drug activity).
CheckClassic chemotherapy toxicity hits gut, marrow and hair. What is the fundamentally different toxicity mechanism of checkpoint inhibitors?
Chemo kills rapidly dividing cells; checkpoint inhibitors cause immune-mediated (autoimmune) inflammation of organs.

  • Checkpoint diabetes is autoimmune (type 1-like): abrupt, DKA, absent C-peptide, not insulin resistance.

  • PD-1 = "TLC-JD": Thyroid, Lung (pneumonitis), Cardiac (myocarditis), Joints (arthralgia), Diabetes.

ApplyLO 4 application questions

A1Six weeks into ipilimumab for melanoma, a man has headache, fatigue and hypotension; ACTH, cortisol and TSH are low. Diagnosis?
Autoimmune hypophysitis, the characteristic anti-CTLA-4 irAE. Treat with glucocorticoids and hormone replacement.
A2A patient on pembrolizumab develops polyuria, polydipsia and ketoacidosis with absent C-peptide. Another develops fatigue, weight gain and high TSH. Explain both.
Autoimmune diabetes and primary hypothyroidism, typical anti-PD-1 irAEs: T cells attack β-cells and thyroid.
A3Three weeks into nivolumab + ipilimumab, a woman has chest pain, arrhythmia and markedly elevated troponin. Why is this an emergency?
Likely immune-mediated myocarditis: uncommon but the most lethal irAE; risk is higher with combination blockade. Stop the drug, high-dose steroids.
A4Two months into ipilimumab, a patient has 8 bloody stools a day; colonoscopy shows diffuse inflammation. Diagnosis and next step?
Immune-mediated colitis/enteritis (anti-CTLA-4). Hold the drug, give glucocorticoids; other immunosuppressants (e.g., infliximab) if refractory.

LO 5CAR T cells, BiTEs and cancer vaccinesEngineering, bridging or educating T cells to find the tumor

By the end: you can describe how CAR T cells are made and built, list their uses and toxicities, explain how a BiTE bypasses MHC, describe therapeutic cancer vaccines, and contrast all of these with checkpoint inhibitors and tumor-targeting antibodies.
Q1. How are CAR T cells made and given? (adoptive cell therapy)
1 · Harvestpatient's own T cells (leukapheresis)

  • CAR-T = cell-based, antigen-specific, targeted immunotherapy.
  • Older adoptive approach: expand tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) from a resected tumor with IL-2 and re-infuse (melanoma). These are naturally tumor-specific T cells; CAR-T instead engineers the specificity.
Q2. What is a chimeric antigen receptor, and why does it need neither MHC nor an APC?
  • CAR = one fusion protein with three parts:
    • scFv (single-chain antibody fragment, e.g., anti-CD19) → binds intact surface antigen directly → no MHC needed.
    • CD28 costimulatory domain → built-in signal 2 → no B7 / APC needed; required for proliferation and persistence.
    • CD3ζ → TCR signaling domain = signal 1.
  • Limitation: an antibody fragment sees only surface proteins, not intracellular peptides.
CheckA tumor has lost all MHC I. Would a CD19 CAR-T still recognize it?
Yes. The scFv binds CD19 on the cell surface directly; MHC is irrelevant.
Q3. What are anti-CD19 CAR T cells used for, and what goes wrong?
  • Tisagenlecleucel (CTL019; first approved 2017): refractory B-cell ALL (children / young adults), diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, follicular lymphoma. Other CD19 CAR-T products also cover CLL (lisocabtagene) and more.
  • Efficacy: 70–90% complete responses in refractory ALL.

Step 1 HY
  • CRS is driven largely by IL-6 → tocilizumab (anti-IL-6 receptor). Same drug is used in RA, giant cell arteritis and COVID-19 hyperinflammation.
  • CD19 = pan-B-cell marker (pre-B through mature B; lost on plasma cells) → targeting it wipes out normal B cells too.
  • Myeloma CAR-T targets BCMA (plasma cells lack CD19).
Step 1 trap

CD19 is on B cells (and B-cell tumors), not on T cells. The CAR is on the T cell; the CD19 target is on the leukemia. Fever + hypotension on day 2–3 after CAR-T is CRS, not sepsis-first thinking (though infection must be excluded).

Memory tool

CAR = "a Car with 3 parts": steering wheel (scFv picks the target), engine (CD28 keeps it running), ignition (CD3ζ starts it). No driver's license (MHC) required.

Q4. How do bispecific T-cell engagers (BiTEs) work?
  • Blinatumomab = bispecific antibody fragment: one arm binds CD3 on any T cell, the other CD19 on B-cell tumor cells → bridges them → MHC-independent tumor lysis (used in B-ALL).
  • Teclistamab (anti-CD3 × anti-BCMA) works the same way in multiple myeloma.
  • Bispecific antibodies act as immune-cell recruiters; an "off-the-shelf" drug, not a personalized cell product.
CheckWhy can blinatumomab kill a leukemia that has lost MHC I, while a checkpoint inhibitor cannot help there?
Blinatumomab links CD3 directly to CD19 → T-cell activation without TCR–MHC recognition. Checkpoint inhibitors only boost the native TCR, which needs MHC I–peptide.
Step 1 HY

Tebentafusp is a soluble bispecific that pairs an engineered TCR (recognizes an MHC-presented intracellular peptide, gp100 on HLA-A*02:01) with an anti-CD3 arm (uveal melanoma). Unlike CARs or antibodies, which see only surface proteins, it can target intracellular antigens. It is a TCR fusion protein, not a CAR.

The vaccine primes new tumor-specific T cells; anti-PD-1 stops the tumor's PD-L1 from switching those T cells off once they arrive.

LO 6The three-signal model and where drugs hit itSketching T-cell activation for immunosuppression

By the end: you can draw signals 1, 2 and 3 from memory, trace TCR → calcineurin → NFAT → IL-2 → IL-2R → mTOR → cell cycle → proliferation, and place every immunosuppressant on the drawing.
Q1. What are the three signals a T cell needs?

Signal 1 · antigen

  • MHC–peptide on APC → TCR/CD3.
  • → ↑ Ca²⁺ → calcineurin → NFAT (+ AP-1, NF-κB) → IL-2 gene.

Signal 2 · costimulation

  • B7 (CD80/86) on APC → CD28 on T cell.
  • Without it → anergy. Also CD40–CD154.

Signal 3 · growth

  • IL-2 → IL-2R (CD25) (autocrine).
  • → mTOR → cell cycle (G1→S) → nucleotide synthesis → proliferation (clonal expansion).
CheckIs IL-2 made in signal 1 or used in signal 3?
Both: signal 1 (via calcineurin/NFAT) turns on IL-2 production; signal 3 is the T cell responding to IL-2 through CD25 → mTOR.

Step 1 HY
  • Calcineurin is a Ca²⁺/calmodulin-dependent phosphatase: it dephosphorylates NFAT so NFAT can enter the nucleus.
  • Lymphocytes depend on de novo purine synthesis (little salvage) → why mycophenolate and azathioprine are fairly lymphocyte-selective.
  • Belatacept (CTLA-4-Ig) is used for kidney transplant; abatacept for RA.
Memory tool

Trace the T cell's day as "See → Shake → Grow": See antigen (signal 1, calcineurin drugs), Shake hands with B7 (signal 2, CTLA-4-Ig), Grow on IL-2 (signal 3, anti-CD25 and mTOR drugs) → then copy DNA (antimetabolites).

Step 1 trap

Calcineurin inhibitors block IL-2 production; mTOR inhibitors block the response to IL-2. A stem asking "which drug leaves IL-2 secretion intact but stops proliferation?" → sirolimus (or basiliximab), not cyclosporine.

ApplyLO 6 application questions

A1In a lab experiment, T cells are stimulated in the presence of cyclosporine and then given excess exogenous IL-2. Do they proliferate?
Largely yes. Cyclosporine blocks IL-2 production (signal 1), but the IL-2R → mTOR pathway (signal 3) is intact, so added IL-2 bypasses the block.
A2Same experiment with sirolimus instead. Does added IL-2 rescue proliferation?
No. mTOR, downstream of the IL-2 receptor, is blocked, so the cells stay arrested in G1.
A3A T cell receives signal 1 but its CD28 is blocked by an abatacept-like drug. What is the outcome?
Anergy (or poor activation): antigen without costimulation tells the T cell to stand down.

A1A kidney-transplant recipient on azathioprine starts allopurinol for gout and develops severe pancytopenia. Why?
Allopurinol inhibits xanthine oxidase, which inactivates 6-MP (azathioprine's active metabolite) → 6-MP accumulates → marrow suppression.
A2A transplant patient on cyclosporine has rising creatinine, hypertension and gum overgrowth. Which drug could replace it to spare the kidney, and how does it work?
Sirolimus (mTOR inhibitor; blocks IL-2 signaling), which is not nephrotoxic, unlike calcineurin inhibitors.
A3A woman with relapsing multiple sclerosis receives a drug that depletes CD52-bearing lymphocytes. A year later she has petechiae and a very low platelet count. Drug and complication?
Alemtuzumab → secondary autoimmunity (immune thrombocytopenia), a known delayed effect.
A4A patient with RA is about to start a TNF-α receptor fusion protein. Name the drug and the mandatory screening test.
Etanercept; screen for latent tuberculosis (PPD or interferon-γ release assay).

1A 61-year-old man with metastatic melanoma begins a monoclonal antibody that enhances T-cell priming in regional lymph nodes by preventing an inhibitory receptor on activated T cells and regulatory T cells from binding CD80/CD86 on dendritic cells. Which drug did he receive?

C. Ipilimumab blocks CTLA-4, which outcompetes CD28 for B7 during priming (early, lymph node). Abatacept is CTLA-4-Ig, which binds B7 and suppresses T cells. Atezolizumab (PD-L1), nivolumab and pembrolizumab (PD-1) act at the tumor in the effector phase.

2A 24-year-old man has classical Hodgkin lymphoma that relapsed after chemotherapy and autologous stem-cell transplant. Tumor analysis shows amplification of chromosome 9p24.1 and EBV in the Reed–Sternberg cells. Which therapy is most likely to induce a response?

A. Nivolumab. 9p24.1 amplification + EBV → JAK/STAT → very high PD-L1/PD-L2 on RS cells; anti-PD-1 gives ~87% response in refractory Hodgkin. CD19 CAR-T (B) and blinatumomab (C) target CD19, which RS cells usually lack. Trastuzumab targets HER2; sirolimus is an immunosuppressant.

3A 47-year-old woman with metastatic endometrial cancer has a tumor with loss of MSH2 protein on immunohistochemistry. Her oncologist predicts an excellent response to pembrolizumab. Which feature best explains this prediction?

E. Mismatch-repair deficiency (MSI-H) → hypermutation → many neoantigens on MHC I → strong response once PD-1 is blocked. Losing MHC I (A) would make anti-PD-1 fail. HER2 is a trastuzumab target. CTLA-4 is on T cells, not tumor cells. Low microbiome diversity predicts a worse response.

4Seven weeks after starting ipilimumab for melanoma, a 58-year-old man has headache, fatigue, nausea and blood pressure 88/56 mm Hg. Serum sodium is 129 mEq/L. Morning cortisol, ACTH and TSH are all low. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

B. Hypophysitis, the classic anti-CTLA-4 irAE: low ACTH and low TSH = central (pituitary) failure. Primary adrenal failure (A) would have high ACTH. Thyroiditis (D, more typical of anti-PD-1) gives high TSH. Metastasis would not explain the multi-axis hormone deficit. Myocarditis presents with chest pain, arrhythmia, troponin.

5A 66-year-old man receiving pembrolizumab for non-small cell lung cancer develops a dry cough and progressive dyspnea over 2 weeks. He is afebrile. O₂ saturation is 89% on room air, and CT shows new bilateral ground-glass opacities. Cultures are negative. In addition to holding pembrolizumab, which is the most appropriate treatment?

D. Immune-mediated pneumonitis (characteristic of anti-PD-1) is treated by stopping the drug and giving glucocorticoids. Antibiotics don't treat autoimmune inflammation; adding ipilimumab would worsen irAEs; levothyroxine treats hypothyroidism; tocilizumab is first-line for CAR-T cytokine release syndrome.

6A 9-year-old girl with relapsed B-cell ALL receives autologous T cells engineered to express an anti-CD19 receptor. Two days later she has a temperature of 40.2 °C, BP 78/40 mm Hg and hypoxia; serum IL-6 is markedly elevated and blood cultures are pending. Which drug most specifically targets the mechanism of this reaction?

A. Tocilizumab (anti-IL-6 receptor) treats cytokine release syndrome (~80% after CD19 CAR-T). Basiliximab blocks CD25; infliximab blocks TNF-α; alemtuzumab would destroy the CAR-T cells (and all lymphocytes); nivolumab would further activate T cells.

7A 16-year-old achieved complete remission of refractory B-ALL after anti-CD19 CAR T-cell therapy. Ten months later he relapses, and flow cytometry shows the leukemic blasts are CD19-negative. He also has had three episodes of pneumonia, and serum IgG is low. Which mechanism explains both findings?

C. The CAR sees only CD19 → CD19-loss variants escape, while normal CD19⁺ B cells are killed → B-cell aplasia and hypogammaglobulinemia (give IVIG). MHC I loss (B) is irrelevant to an scFv-based CAR. Exhaustion would not make blasts CD19-negative. The cells are autologous, so GVHD (E) is not expected.

8A drug used for relapsed B-cell ALL is a small fusion protein with two antibody-derived binding sites. It can kill leukemic cells even when they express no MHC class I. Which best describes its mechanism?

E. Blinatumomab, a bispecific T-cell engager (CD3 × CD19) → MHC-independent lysis. A and C are checkpoint inhibitors (need native TCR–MHC). B describes an antibody–drug conjugate (inotuzumab). D describes a cancer vaccine, which also depends on MHC.

9A 45-year-old man is maintained on an oral drug after kidney transplantation. Over 2 years he develops hypertension, rising creatinine, hirsutism and marked gingival overgrowth. The drug's immunosuppressive effect depends on forming a complex with which intracellular protein?

B. Cyclophilin. Gingival hyperplasia + hirsutism + nephrotoxicity = cyclosporine, which binds cyclophilin and the complex inhibits calcineurin. FKBP binds tacrolimus and sirolimus. mTOR is sirolimus's target; IMPDH is mycophenolate's; TPMT inactivates 6-MP.

10A 52-year-old liver-transplant recipient taking azathioprine and prednisone is started on a drug for recurrent gout. Three weeks later he has fever, mouth ulcers, WBC 1,100/µL, hemoglobin 7.9 g/dL and platelets 32,000/µL. Inhibition of which enzyme caused this?

D. Xanthine oxidase. Allopurinol (or febuxostat) blocks the enzyme that inactivates 6-mercaptopurine → 6-MP accumulates → pancytopenia. Colchicine or NSAIDs (B) would not do this. DHFR is methotrexate's target. HGPRT actually activates 6-MP.

11A kidney-transplant recipient is switched from tacrolimus to a drug that also binds FKBP12 but leaves IL-2 transcription intact while arresting T cells in G1 after IL-2 receptor stimulation. Which adverse effect is most characteristic of the new drug?

A. Hyperlipidemia. The drug is sirolimus (mTOR inhibitor, signal 3), which is not nephrotoxic but causes hyperlipidemia, cytopenias and poor wound healing. Gingival hyperplasia = cyclosporine; nephrotoxicity = calcineurin inhibitors; TB reactivation = anti-TNF; hemorrhagic cystitis = cyclophosphamide.

12At the time of kidney transplantation, a patient receives a chimeric monoclonal antibody as induction therapy. It binds the α chain of a receptor on activated T cells, preventing a cytokine from driving clonal expansion. What is the target?

C. CD25 (IL-2Rα), the target of basiliximab (-ximab = chimeric). CD3 = old anti-CD3 mAb; CD20 = rituximab; CD52 = alemtuzumab (depletes cells); IL-6R = tocilizumab.

13A 39-year-old woman with rheumatoid arthritis not controlled on methotrexate will start a biologic that is a fusion of the extracellular domain of a cytokine receptor with the Fc portion of human IgG1, acting as a decoy. Which test must be done before starting?

E. The drug is etanercept (TNF-α receptor–Fc fusion that soaks up TNF-α). TNF maintains granulomas, so latent TB must be excluded. TPMT is for azathioprine/6-MP; HLA-B*5701 for abacavir; IL-6 levels and CD19 counts are not pre-treatment requirements.

14A metastatic tumor clone acquires a mutation that abolishes surface expression of MHC class I. Which immune effector cell is most likely to recognize and kill this clone?

B. NK cell. Loss of MHC I removes the NK inhibitory signal ("missing self") → NK activation. CD8⁺ T cells need MHC I–peptide; CD4⁺ T cells see MHC II on APCs; Tregs suppress; plasma cells secrete antibody and do not kill directly.
