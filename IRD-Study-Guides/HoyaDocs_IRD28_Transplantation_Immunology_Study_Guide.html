LO 1Types of transplantationWho gave the graft decides whether it is attacked

By the end: you can name the four graft types, label each as syngeneic or allogeneic/xenogeneic, predict the outcome of any inbred-mouse graft, and contrast first-set with second-set rejection.
Q1. What are the four kinds of graft, and which are "genetically identical"?
  • Syngeneic = donor and recipient are genetically identical → identical histocompatibility antigens → nothing looks foreign → accepted.
    • Autograft = your own tissue moved from one site to another (skin graft, autologous bone marrow / peripheral blood stem cells).
    • Isograft = between two different but genetically identical individuals (identical twins, inbred mouse strains).
  • Allogeneic = donor and recipient are genetically different → foreign antigens → rejected unless suppressed.
    • Allograft = between different members of the same species (human → human kidney). The usual clinical transplant.
    • Xenograft = between two different species (pig → human). Genetically different, so it behaves like an allograft (rejected); the formal term is xenogeneic.

CheckA man receives a kidney from his HLA-matched (non-twin) brother. Syngeneic or allogeneic?
Allogeneic (an allograft). Only an identical twin is genetically identical. A matched sibling still differs at minor antigens (LO 2) and needs immunosuppression.
Memory tool

Auto = self · Iso = identical ("iso-twin") · Allo = "other" (same species) · Xeno = "foreigner" (other species). Syngeneic = "same genes".

Step 1 trap

"Matched sibling" ≠ isograft. HLA-identical siblings still reject via minor histocompatibility antigens. Only monozygotic twins share everything.

Q2. What are the "laws of transplantation"? (inbred mouse grafts)

Rule: a graft is accepted only if the recipient already expresses every histocompatibility antigen of the donor. MHC alleles are codominantly expressed, so an F1 hybrid (A×B) shows both A and B antigens.

CheckWhy can an F1 (A×B) mouse accept skin from either parent, but neither parent accepts F1 skin?
The F1 co-expresses A and B antigens, so either parent's tissue looks like self. Each parent lacks the other parent's antigens, which are present on F1 skin → rejected.
Q3. What happens to an accepted graft versus a first-time and a repeat allograft?

Autograft (accepted)

  • Days 3–7: revascularization
  • Days 7–10: healing (neutrophils here are normal wound healing)
  • Days 12–14: resolution

First-set rejection

  • First exposure to donor antigens → primary adaptive, mainly T-cell (cell-mediated) response
  • Days 3–7 revascularization → days 7–10 cellular infiltration (lymphocytes, monocytes) → days 12–14 thrombosis + necrosis
  • Rejected in ≈ 14 days (≈ 2 weeks)

Second-set rejection

  • Second graft sharing the same donor antigens → memory (secondary) response
  • Days 3–4 infiltration → days 5–6 thrombosis + necrosis
  • Rejected in ≈ 6 days (≈ 1 week); cellular + humoral arms
CheckLymphocytes from a mouse that rejected a strain A graft are transferred into a naive mouse of the same strain. That mouse gets a strain A graft. How fast is it rejected, and what does this prove?
≈ 6 days (second-set speed). Moving cells (not serum) transfers the memory → rejection is a cell-mediated response (adoptive transfer).
Step 1 trap

First-set rejection is not innate. It is a primary adaptive (T-cell) response on first exposure; second-set is the memory response. Both are adaptive; second-set proves memory + specificity (only grafts sharing the first donor's antigens are rejected faster).

Memory tool

"Two weeks first time, one week second time." Memory halves the clock: 14 d → 6 d.

Buzzwords
"identical twin donor" → isograft, accepted"pig valve / organ" → xenograft"rejected faster the second time" → second-set, memory"spleen cells transfer rejection" → cell-mediated

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A burn patient receives skin from his own thigh. At day 9 the biopsy shows neutrophils. Rejection?
No, an accepted autograft. Neutrophils at days 7–10 are part of normal healing; self tissue has self antigens.
A2Strain B mouse rejects strain A skin at day 14. A month later it receives strain A skin again and strain C skin at the same time. Predict both outcomes.
Strain A: second-set (~6 days). Strain C: first-set (~14 days). Memory is specific to the antigens already seen.
A3A woman donates a kidney to her identical twin. Does the twin need maintenance immunosuppression?
No alloimmune response is expected. An isograft shares all ABO, HLA and minor antigens.
A4Parent A (A strain) receives skin from its own F1 (A×B) offspring. Outcome?
Rejected. The F1 graft carries B antigens that parent A has never had as self.

Q3. What are the HLA molecules, and why is a perfect match so hard to find?
  • HLA genes sit on chromosome 6 and are needed to start any T-cell response (TCR binds HLA + peptide).
  • Polymorphic = many alleles of each gene in the population (HLA-B is the most polymorphic locus; class I loci overall more polymorphic than class II; among class II, DRB1 leads).
  • Polygenic = each person has several class I and class II genes. Alleles are codominantly expressed (maternal + paternal).
  • → huge number of combinations → unrelated donors rarely match exactly.

Step 1 HY
  • HLA genes are inherited as a haplotype from each parent → a sibling has a 25% chance of being HLA-identical (50% haploidentical).
  • Kidney matching focuses on HLA-A, -B, -DR.
  • Pre-transplant workup = ABO typing + crossmatch (recipient serum vs donor lymphocytes) to catch preformed antibodies.
Memory tool

Rule of 8: MHC I × CD8 = 8 · MHC II × CD4 = 8. Class I = "one-letter" loci A, B, C; class II = "D-letter" loci DR, DQ, DP.

Q4. What are minor histocompatibility antigens?
  • Peptides from normal, polymorphic self proteins (cytoplasmic proteins) that differ in sequence between people, presented by MHC (mainly class I).
  • Made by normal processing: protein → proteasome → TAP → ER → loaded on HLA → surface.
  • Behavior:
    • Each one alone → slow, mild rejection; defined by cell-mediated rejection between MHC-identical strains.
    • Humoral (antibody) responses are generally not detected.
    • Hard to match (polymorphic) but individually don't trigger much response; many together can still cause real rejection or GVHD.
  • Why it matters: HLA-identical donor ≠ identical peptides. The recipient T cell sees shared HLA + a variant peptide → foreign.
CheckWhy do HLA-identical sibling recipients still take immunosuppression?
They still differ at minor histocompatibility antigens, which drive slow T-cell-mediated rejection.
Step 1 trap

ABO antigens are not just on RBCs: they are on endothelium, which is why an ABO-mismatched vascularized organ undergoes hyperacute (not second-set) rejection.

Buzzwords
"HLA-identical sibling, slow T-cell rejection" → minor antigens"polymorphic + polygenic" → why matching is hard"preformed isohemagglutinins" → ABO, hyperacute"passenger leukocytes" → donor DCs, class I + II

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A type O recipient is accidentally given a type A kidney. What happens and when?
Hyperacute rejection within minutes. Preformed anti-A IgM/IgG bind A antigen on graft endothelium → complement → thrombosis.
A2Months after a kidney from her HLA-identical brother, a woman shows slow, T-cell graft damage. What antigens are being recognized?
Minor histocompatibility antigens: variant self-protein peptides on the shared HLA molecules.
A3Which HLA locus would you expect to be the hardest to match in a random donor?
HLA-B, the most polymorphic locus (class I loci are more polymorphic than class II overall).

LO 3Alloimmunity and allorecognitionHow recipient T cells notice someone else's HLA

By the end: you can define alloreactivity, draw the direct, indirect and semi-direct pathways (APC origin, what the TCR sees, timing), name the co-stimulatory signals, list sensitization routes, and trace the DAMP → DC → TH1/TH17 loop.
Q1. What is alloreactivity, and why is it so strong even on first exposure?
  • Alloreactivity (alloimmune response) = the T-cell response against non-self (allogeneic) MHC.
  • It is cross-reactivity: a TCR selected to recognize self MHC + foreign (e.g., viral) peptide is flexible enough to also engage a donor MHC + peptide complex that looks similar.
  • Many clones cross-react this way → a large fraction of the T-cell repertoire is alloreactive → strong rejection without any prior exposure.
CheckA recipient has never been transfused, pregnant or transplanted. Why does she still mount a vigorous response to an allograft?
Her T cells cross-react: TCRs made for self HLA + foreign peptide also recognize donor HLA, and a large share of the repertoire does this.
Q2. What are the three allorecognition pathways?

Right after surgery the graft holds donor parenchymal cells (class I) and donor dendritic cells (class I + II). Over time, recipient DCs and monocytes move in. That sets up three routes. Blue = donor, red = recipient.

PathwayAPCRecipient TCR seesHow it gets thereIntensity over time
DirectDonor DC or parenchymal cellIntact donor HLA (class I or II), no foreign peptide requiredDonor DCs leave the graft and travel to the recipient lymph nodeHigh initially → wanes quickly
IndirectRecipient DCDonor HLA peptide on recipient (self) HLA class IIRecipient DCs enter the graft, endocytose + process donor material, then traffic to regional nodes to meet CD4 T cellsLow initially → rises, stays high throughout rejection
Semi-directRecipient DCIntact donor HLA on a recipient cellDonor cells (dying DCs) release exosomes (exocytosis) carrying donor HLA, which attach to recipient DCsHigh initially → declines gradually

All three pathways contribute to rejection. Direct dominates early because donor passenger DCs are finite; indirect persists because recipient APCs keep sampling the graft for its whole life → chronic rejection.

CheckThree years after transplant, a recipient's T cells respond to donor HLA peptides on her own class II. Which pathway?
Indirect: donor passenger DCs are long gone; recipient APCs keep presenting donor peptides → chronic rejection and help for donor-specific antibody.
Memory tool

Ask two questions: whose APC? and whose HLA molecule?

  • Donor APC + donor HLA → Direct
  • Recipient APC + recipient HLA (donor peptide) → Indirect
  • Recipient APC + donor HLA → Semi-direct ("half and half")
Step 1 trap

"Direct doesn't need APCs" is shorthand: it doesn't need recipient APC processing or a foreign peptide, but it is driven largely by donor DCs (plus donor parenchymal cells). Indirect is the one that looks like ordinary antigen presentation.

Buzzwords
"passenger leukocytes" → direct, early"exosomes / cross-dressing" → semi-direct"donor peptide on self MHC II" → indirect, chronic
Q3. Which co-stimulatory signals activate the alloresponse, and what do activated T cells do?
  • Signal 1 = TCR binds HLA/peptide. Signal 2 (needed for full activation):
    • CD80 (B7) on the APC – CD28 on the T cell. B7 is up-regulated by infection, inflammation and ischemia, all present at transplant.
    • CD40 – CD40 ligand (CD154) between activated CD4 T cells and B cells / macrophages / DCs.
  • CD4 T-cell help via CD40L:
    • B cells → germinal centers, proliferation, plasma cells, class switch IgM → IgG (complement-binding subclasses) → donor-specific antibody (DSA).
    • Macrophages → activation → delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction in the graft.
  • Effector mechanisms that damage the graft (all fueled by IL-2 and IL-4/5/6 from helper T cells):
    • CD8 cytotoxic T cells lyse HLA-bearing cells · DTH CD4 T cells → activated macrophages (lytic enzymes) · antibody + complement lysis · ADCC by NK cells/macrophages via Fc receptors.
CheckWhy does T-cell help underlie antibody-mediated rejection too?
CD40L on CD4 T cells drives B-cell class switching to IgG and plasma-cell differentiation → donor-specific antibodies.
Step 1 HY

Belatacept (CTLA-4–Ig) binds B7 and blocks B7–CD28 co-stimulation → used for kidney transplant maintenance (abatacept is the RA cousin).

Q4. How does a patient become sensitized, and why are memory cells a problem?
  • First-time sensitization: donor DCs carrying class II HLA migrate from the kidney to the lymph node → activate naive TH cells (IL-2) → proliferation → effectors travel back to the graft. Naive cells live mainly in central lymphoid organs, so this happens in the node.
  • Routes of allosensitization (most candidates are already sensitized):
    • Conventional: prior transplant, pregnancy (paternal HLA), blood transfusion.
    • Other: vaccination, infection (cross-reactive memory).

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1In the first week after transplant, which pathway activates the most T cells, and why does it fade?
Direct. Donor passenger DCs stimulate huge numbers of recipient T cells but are finite and are replaced by recipient cells.
A2A recipient DC is found carrying intact donor class I HLA on its surface. Which pathway can it stimulate?
Semi-direct (intact donor HLA on a recipient APC, via exosomes). The same DC can also present processed donor peptides (indirect).
A3Predict the effect of blocking CD40L on donor-specific antibody production.
↓ DSA. Without CD40–CD40L help, B cells can't form germinal centers or class-switch to IgG.
A4A donor kidney sat on ice for 30 hours. Why might early rejection be worse?
Prolonged ischemia → more DAMPs → more DC maturation (↑ MHC, ↑ B7) → stronger T-cell priming.

  • Boards phrase ACR as "vasculitis of graft vessels with dense interstitial lymphocytic infiltrate".

Memory tool

"Minutes, weeks, months" → Preformed Ab, T cells (or new Ab), scar. Only the middle one can be turned around.

Step 1 trap

Neutrophils + thrombosis = hyperacute. ACR is lymphocytic (tubulitis). Hyperacute antibody is preformed; AMR antibody is made after transplant (so it takes days).

Q2. What does hyperacute rejection look like?
  • Occurs within minutes of reperfusion; antibody-mediated; primary targets = antigens on vascular endothelium; complement activation + capillary thrombosis; graft turns mottled and blue on the table.
  • Now rare: ABO matching + pre-transplant antibody screening.
Q3. How do you tell acute cellular from acute antibody-mediated rejection on biopsy?

Acute cellular (ACR)

  • More common: 75–80%
  • Tubulitis: lymphocytes inside tubular epithelium (tubular cells show donor class I)
  • Interstitial inflammation: dense mononuclear infiltrate
  • May involve vessels (vascular rejection)
  • C4d negative

Antibody-mediated (AMR / AHR)

  • Less common: 20–30%
  • C4d ring staining of peritubular capillaries
  • Microvascular inflammation: peritubular capillaritis, glomerulitis, vasculitis
  • Serum: new anti-donor HLA antibody
  • Why C4d? Antibody on endothelium → classical pathway → C4b cleaved to C4d, which stays covalently bound → a durable "footprint" of antibody even after the antibody is gone.
CheckDay-12 biopsy: lymphocytes within tubular epithelium, C4d negative. Diagnosis?
Acute cellular rejection. Tubulitis + negative C4d argues against AMR.
Buzzwords
"graft mottled, cyanotic on unclamping" → hyperacute"tubulitis" → ACR"C4d in peritubular capillaries" → AMR"onion-skin / intimal thickening, fibrosis, years later" → chronic
Q4. What is chronic rejection?
  • Later than 3 months; CMR, AMR or a mixture; insidious, progressive deterioration (slow creatinine rise); irreversible; likely due to continuous immunologic assault.
  • Lymphocytes + macrophages release cytokines → fibroblasts make excess collagen, smooth muscle proliferates → graft arteriosclerosis + interstitial fibrosis, glomeruli and tubules replaced by scar.
  • Main cause of late graft loss; more immunosuppression does not reverse established fibrosis.
Q5. What is graft-versus-host disease, and how is it rejection in reverse?
  • GVHD = immunocompetent donor T cells in the graft recognize recipient (host) HLA and peptides as foreign and attack host tissues.
  • Needs a host that cannot reject the donor T cells (conditioned / immunocompromised).
  • Same three pathways, roles swapped: T cells = donor; APC/HLA = recipient (direct: recipient APC; indirect: donor APC with recipient HLA peptide; semi-direct: donor APC wearing intact recipient HLA).

CheckWhy is GVHD a problem in bone marrow transplants but rarely in kidney transplants?
Marrow contains mature donor T cells and rebuilds the whole immune system from donor cells in a host that can't reject them. A kidney carries few lymphocytes, and the recipient's intact immune system removes them.
Step 1 HY
  • Classic acute GVHD triad: maculopapular rash (palms/soles), jaundice, diarrhea ± hepatosplenomegaly.
  • GVHD also follows lymphocyte-rich solid grafts (liver) and non-irradiated transfusions in immunocompromised patients → use irradiated blood products.
  • Donor T cells also kill residual leukemia: graft-versus-leukemia effect (a benefit).
Step 1 trap

In GVHD the donor T cells are the attackers and the recipient is the target. Don't pick "recipient T cells" or "recipient antibodies" for a rash + diarrhea + jaundice after marrow transplant.

Memory tool

GVHD hits the "SLiG" organs: Skin, Liver, Gut, the ones with rapidly turning-over epithelium hurt most by conditioning.

LO 5Preventing rejection and GVHDMatching, the immunosuppression regimen, and the 10 drugs to know

By the end: you can list four GVHD-prevention strategies, lay out induction + triple maintenance therapy, and give every drug-to-know its brand name, target, regimen role and key toxicity.
Q1. How are GVHD and rejection prevented besides drugs?
  1. Optimal HLA matching (plus ABO compatibility and a negative crossmatch for solid organs).
  2. Immunosuppressive therapy.
  3. Partial T-cell depletion of the stem cell product → fewer donor T cells to attack the host.
  4. Non-myeloablative (reduced-intensity) conditioning → less tissue damage → fewer DAMPs/PAMPs and less cytokine storm → fewer activated host APCs priming donor T cells.
CheckWhat is the downside of removing all donor T cells from a marrow graft for leukemia?
Less GVHD but loss of the graft-versus-leukemia effect (↑ relapse) and poorer engraftment.
Step 1 trap

T-cell depletion is a trade-off: ↓ GVHD but ↑ leukemia relapse and ↑ graft failure. Boards like "which complication increases?" → relapse.

Q2. What does the standard immunosuppression regimen look like?
InductionAt the time of transplant: Campath (alemtuzumab) or Thymoglobulin or Simulect (basiliximab)

  • Triple-drug maintenance = one drug from each of three classes, given for the life of the transplant. Hitting different steps gives more suppression with lower doses (less toxicity) of each.
  • If AMR develops: plasmapheresis (removes antibody), IVIg, rituximab, bortezomib. Best treatment = catch it early.

CheckWhich two drugs to know are given for antibody-mediated rejection, and which cell does each hit?
Rituximab → B cells (CD20); bortezomib → plasma cells (proteasome).
Memory tool
  • Suffixes: -ximab = chimeric (basiliximab, rituximab) · -zumab = humanized (alemtuzumab) · -zomib = proteasome inhibitor.
  • Campath empties the whole lymphocyte "camp" (CD52 on T and B cells). "Simulect = Simply the IL-2 Receptor."
  • Cyclosporine → "cyclophilin"; tacrolimus → "FKBP" (F-tac).
Step 1 HY
  • Both CNIs are nephrotoxic; cyclosporine adds gingival hyperplasia + hirsutism; tacrolimus adds diabetes + neurotoxicity.
  • Azathioprine + allopurinol → pancytopenia (reduce aza dose).
  • Chronic immunosuppression ↑ infection (CMV) and malignancy (skin SCC, post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder).
  • Alemtuzumab also used for CLL and MS; bortezomib is first-line for multiple myeloma.
Step 1 trap
  • Basiliximab targets the IL-2 receptor (CD25), not IL-2 itself.
  • Alemtuzumab is anti-CD52, not CD25.
  • Rituximab doesn't touch plasma cells (they lack CD20) → antibody persists → add bortezomib.

ApplyLO 5 application questions

A1A patient on tacrolimus, mycophenolate and prednisolone asks why she needs three drugs. Match each to the step it blocks.
Tacrolimus → activation (no IL-2); mycophenolate → proliferation (no purines); prednisolone → inflammation (no cytokines). Different steps → more effect at lower, less toxic doses.
A2An AMR patient got rituximab but donor-specific antibody barely falls. Why, and what next?
Long-lived plasma cells lack CD20 and keep secreting. Add bortezomib; plasmapheresis/IVIg remove or neutralize existing antibody.
A3Why does reduced-intensity (non-myeloablative) conditioning lower GVHD risk?
Less tissue damage → fewer DAMPs/PAMPs → less cytokine storm → fewer activated host APCs to prime donor T cells.
A4A kidney recipient on azathioprine is started on allopurinol for gout. Two weeks later: WBC 1,800, platelets 40,000. Explain.
Allopurinol blocks xanthine oxidase, which normally inactivates 6-MP → 6-MP accumulates → marrow suppression.
A5Which induction agent is a polyclonal preparation rather than a monoclonal antibody?
Thymoglobulin (antithymocyte globulin), raised against human thymocytes, binds many T-cell antigens.

1A 46-year-old man with end-stage renal disease receives a deceased-donor kidney. Within 5 minutes of releasing the vascular clamps, the kidney becomes mottled and cyanotic and stops making urine. Biopsy shows widespread capillary thrombi and neutrophils. Which of the following best explains this outcome?

C. Hyperacute rejection: preformed anti-ABO or anti-HLA antibodies bind endothelium → complement → thrombosis within minutes. A and E are T-cell processes that need days. B is GVHD. D (new DSA) takes days and causes acute AMR, not minute-scale failure.

2Twelve days after a living-unrelated kidney transplant, a 38-year-old woman's creatinine rises sharply. Biopsy shows lymphocytes infiltrating tubular epithelium and a dense interstitial mononuclear infiltrate. Peritubular capillary C4d staining is negative. Which mechanism is primarily responsible?

A. Tubulitis + interstitial lymphocytes within 2–21 days with negative C4d = acute cellular rejection, the most common type (75–80%), reversible with more immunosuppression. B would show C4d. C causes hyperacute rejection. D is chronic (> 3 months). E is GVHD, which spares a solid-organ graft.

3A 55-year-old man has a slow rise in creatinine over 3 years after kidney transplantation. Biopsy shows concentric intimal thickening of arteries and interstitial fibrosis. His T cells respond to donor HLA-derived peptides displayed on his own class II molecules. Which allorecognition pathway dominates at this stage?

E. Indirect allorecognition starts low, rises and stays high, and drives chronic rejection. A: donor passenger DCs are long gone; direct is an early pathway. B: semi-direct shows intact donor HLA and wanes over time. C is not one of the alloimmune T-cell pathways. D: tubular cells express class I only.

4A 29-year-old man receives an allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant for acute myeloid leukemia after myeloablative conditioning. Three weeks later he develops a maculopapular rash on the palms and soles, profuse watery diarrhea and a bilirubin of 5 mg/dL. Which of the following is the effector population responsible?

B. Acute GVHD (skin, liver, gut): conditioning damage releases DAMPs/PAMPs, host APCs activate, and immunocompetent donor T cells (TH1/TH17, Tc) attack host tissues. It is type IV hypersensitivity. A would cause graft failure (cytopenias), not this triad. C, D and E are not the drivers.

5A 50-year-old kidney transplant recipient on a maintenance regimen develops gum overgrowth, increased facial hair, hypertension and a rising creatinine. The drug responsible first binds which intracellular protein?

D. Cyclosporine (Neoral) binds cyclophilin → the complex inhibits calcineurin → no NFAT dephosphorylation → no IL-2. Gingival hyperplasia and hirsutism point to cyclosporine. A is tacrolimus (nephrotoxic, but causes diabetes/neurotoxicity rather than hirsutism). B is mycophenolate. C is basiliximab's target (surface, not intracellular). E is bortezomib.

6A kidney recipient is diagnosed with acute antibody-mediated rejection. After plasmapheresis, IVIg and rituximab, donor-specific antibody titers remain high. Which additional agent most directly targets the cells still producing antibody?

B. Long-lived plasma cells lack CD20 (so rituximab misses them) and depend on the proteasome to handle huge immunoglobulin output → bortezomib (Velcade) kills them. A and D act on T-cell IL-2 pathways. C depletes lymphocytes via CD52 at induction. E is a broad anti-inflammatory, not plasma-cell-specific.

7A 62-year-old kidney transplant recipient on azathioprine, tacrolimus and prednisolone develops acute gout and is started on a new drug. Two weeks later his WBC is 1,600/µL and platelets are 35,000/µL. What is the mechanism of this interaction?

A. Allopurinol (or febuxostat) blocks xanthine oxidase, which normally inactivates 6-MP (azathioprine's active form) → toxic accumulation → pancytopenia. B would cause rejection, not cytopenias. C is mycophenolate's mechanism. D and E don't produce this drug-interaction pattern.

8A 34-year-old woman receives a kidney from her HLA-identical older brother. ABO is compatible and the crossmatch is negative. Seven months later she develops slowly progressive, T-cell-mediated graft injury without detectable anti-donor antibody. Which antigens are most likely being recognized?

E. Minor antigens are polymorphic self-protein peptides presented on the shared HLA; each causes slow, cell-mediated rejection, and antibody to them is generally not detected. A, B and C are matched. D describes GVHD roles, not rejection.

9At the time of kidney transplantation, a patient receives a chimeric mouse-human monoclonal antibody that binds the α chain of a receptor upregulated on activated T cells, blocking a growth signal without depleting the cells. Which drug was given?

D. Basiliximab (Simulect) binds CD25, the IL-2 receptor α chain, as induction therapy. A is humanized anti-CD52 and depletes lymphocytes. B is a polyclonal depleting antithymocyte preparation. C targets CD20 on B cells. E is a CTLA-4–Ig fusion protein that blocks B7–CD28, not a monoclonal antibody.

10A 41-year-old woman with three prior pregnancies and multiple blood transfusions is listed for her first kidney transplant. Her rejection is expected to be harder to control. Which property of her alloreactive T cells best explains this?

C. Pregnancy and transfusion sensitize her → alloreactive memory cells: lower activation requirements, less need for co-stimulation, more cytotoxic molecules, found at inflamed sites, and poorly responsive to immunosuppressive drugs. A, B and D describe naive cells. E is not a property of memory cells.

11In an experiment with inbred mice, skin grafts are exchanged between strain A, strain B, and their F1 (A×B) offspring. Which of the following grafts will be rejected?

A. A graft is accepted only if the recipient already has every donor antigen. F1 skin carries B antigens that strain A lacks → rejected. B and C: the F1 co-expresses A and B antigens, so either parent's skin looks like self. D is an isograft and E is an autograft, both accepted.

12To lower the risk of graft-versus-host disease, the donor marrow for a patient with acute leukemia is extensively depleted of T cells before infusion. Which complication is most likely to increase as a result?

E. Donor T cells mediate the graft-versus-leukemia effect; removing them lowers GVHD but raises relapse (and graft failure). A, C and D are solid-organ rejection patterns. B is unrelated to T-cell content.

13A transplant pathologist stains a kidney graft biopsy for C4d and sees ring-like staining of the peritubular capillaries. What does this finding indicate?

B. C4d is a split product of C4, generated when antibody activates the classical pathway; it stays covalently bound, marking antibody-mediated rejection. A and E don't involve C4 cleavage by antibody. C is tubulitis (ACR, C4d negative). D causes arteriolar hyalinosis, not C4d deposition.
