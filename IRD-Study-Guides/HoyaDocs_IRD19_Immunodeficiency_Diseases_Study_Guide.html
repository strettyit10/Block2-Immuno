LO 1Clinical presentations of the immunodeficienciesWho gets sick, when, with what, and which extra clues give it away

By the end: you can sort any immune deficiency into one of five categories, point to where in immune development it breaks, and name its gene, inheritance, typical organisms, age of onset and the non-infectious clue (cord, hair, calcium, heart, granulomas) that clinches it.
Q1. What is an immunodeficiency, and how do primary and secondary forms differ?
  • Immunodeficiency disorder = the immune system's ability to defend the body against foreign substances is compromised or weakened — from genetic mutations, medical treatments, or other factors that impair immune-cell function.
    • Hallmark → ↑ susceptibility to infection: infections that are recurrent, severe, unusual or hard to clear.

Primary (PID)

  • Usually genetic, present from birth → immune system fails to develop or work.
  • Newer name: inborn errors of immunity (IEIs).
  • Concept has expanded → primary immune regulatory disorders (PIRDs): not only infection but autoimmunity, autoinflammation, allergy/severe atopy, lymphoproliferation, malignancy.
  • >400 distinct disorders; once "pediatric only", now also diagnosed in adults.

Secondary

  • Acquired later in life.
  • Causes: infection (HIV/AIDS), drugs (transplant immunosuppressants, steroids), malnutrition, other diseases.
  • Far more common overall than primary.

Secondary — acquired from immunosuppressive drugs (steroids + transplant agents), not an inborn error.
Q2. Where in immune development can the block happen — and how do the five categories differ?

Every primary immunodeficiency = a block at one point on the road from stem cell → effector cell (or a missing plasma protein). Find the block → predict which cells are missing → predict the infections.

CheckTwo infants: A (3 months) has persistent thrush and severe RSV; B (9 months) has repeated pneumococcal otitis and pneumonia. Which arm is broken in each?
A = T-cell defect (early, viral + fungal). B = antibody defect (after maternal IgG fades, encapsulated bacteria). Age of onset + organism type = the two fastest clues.
Step 1 HY
  • The single most common primary immunodeficiency = selective IgA deficiency: often asymptomatic; sinopulmonary/GI infections, Giardia; anaphylaxis to IgA-containing blood products (anti-IgA antibodies).
  • B-cell defects also → enteroviral disease (echovirus meningoencephalitis, vaccine-associated polio) and Giardia.
Memory tool

"Bugs tell you the broken arm":

  • Bacteria (encapsulated) → B cells / complement / spleen
  • Viruses + fungi → T cells
  • Catalase ⊕ + granulomas → phagocyte (CGD)
  • Neisseria → MAC (C5–C9)
  • Everything at once → SCID
Step 1 trap

"Most common PID" depends on the question: category → antibody (B-cell) defects; single disorder → selective IgA deficiency. Most common SCID → X-linked γc. Most common CGD → gp91phox (X-linked).

PhagocytesPhagocytic cell disorders

Q3. Why do phagocyte problems hurt both innate and adaptive immunity, and what are the "too few cells" (quantitative) defects?
  • Phagocytes do innate work (phagocytosis, inflammation) and act as antigen-presenting cells → activate T and B cells (adaptive).
    • Phagocytosis sequence: engulf → phagosome → fuses with lysosomal granules → phagolysosome → digestion → residual body expelled.
  • Two kinds of phagocyte defect:
    • Quantitative → numbers ↓
    • Qualitative → numbers normal, function defective

Neutropenia

  • ↓ number of PMNs (ANC < 1500/µL); congenital or acquired (chemo, drugs, marrow infiltration).
  • → ↑ susceptibility to bacterial infections.

Hyposplenism

  • ↓ number of splenic macrophages → after surgical splenectomy or from hemolytic anemia (sickle cell anemia → autosplenectomy by infarction).
  • Spleen filters blood-borne, opsonized encapsulated bacteria → without it they are cleared poorly.
CheckA 9-year-old with sickle cell disease develops rapid S. pneumoniae sepsis. Quantitative or qualitative phagocyte defect?
Quantitative — functional asplenia removes the splenic macrophage "filter". Her neutrophils themselves work fine.
Q4. A neutrophil has to roll, stick, leave the vessel, fuse its granules, make bleach and get activated. What disease appears when each step fails?

Qualitative phagocyte defects line up along the phagocyte's "to-do list". Walk the journey: get there (LAD) → have working granules (CHS) → make bleach (CGD) → be told to kill (IFN-γ/IL-12).

Leukocyte adhesion deficiencies (LAD)

  • Normal egress from blood: rolling (selectins) → capture → tight adhesion (integrins) → diapedesis → chemotaxis toward the trigger.
  • Basic defect: mutated adhesion proteins → leukocytes cannot stick to vessel walls → cannot reach infected tissue → frequent, severe bacterial and fungal infections.
    • Neutrophils are made and pile up in blood (neutrophilia) but never arrive → no pus.
  • LAD I → tight adhesion fails (CD18 β2-integrin):
    • Delayed umbilical cord separation (cord stump normally falls off by neutrophil-driven inflammation)
    • Periodontitis → rapid loss of tooth-supporting tissue, early tooth loss
    • Ulcerative skin lesions with minimal inflammation, thick scab, no pus
  • LAD II → rolling fails: mutation in a fucosyl transferase → leukocytes lack the fucosylated selectin ligand (sialyl-Lewis X, CD15s) → cannot roll on E-selectin → blocked movement from blood into tissues.
CheckA 3-week-old has omphalitis, the cord is still attached, WBC is 45,000/µL (mostly neutrophils), yet skin biopsies show almost no neutrophils and no pus. Which step fails?
Adhesion / exit from blood → LAD I. Cells are made and circulate but cannot leave vessels. (In CGD neutrophils arrive and form pus — they just can't kill.)
Q5. What goes wrong in Chédiak-Higashi syndrome, and why does it affect hair, platelets and nerves too?
  • A defect of granule formation and content: defective lysosomal trafficking → abnormally giant granules (lysosomes) in neutrophils, macrophages and other granule-containing cells → impaired phagosome–lysosome fusion → poor killing.
    • Electron microscopy → huge tertiary lysosomes in neutrophils/macrophages.
  • One trafficking defect hits every lysosome-related organelle:
    Neutrophil granulesrecurrent severe pyogenic infections
    Melanosomespartial (oculocutaneous) albinism, ash-gray/silvery hair, abnormal / vitiligo-like patchy pigmentation
    Platelet dense granuleseasy bruising and bleeding
    Neural cellsprogressive neurologic dysfunction
  • Classic picture: a 7-month-old boy with facial skin darkening, ash-gray hair, abnormal pigmentation and recurrent infections.
CheckA toddler with silvery hair has recurrent pyogenic infections, nosebleeds and easy bruising. What would the smear show?
Giant cytoplasmic granules in neutrophils → Chédiak-Higashi. Plain albinism would not cause infections or bleeding.
Q6. How does chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) break the oxidative burst, and why are catalase-positive organisms the problem?
  • Defect: genetic defects in the NADPH oxidase complex of phagocytes (neutrophils, macrophages) → cannot make reactive oxygen species (ROS) = no respiratory (oxidative) burst.
  • Normal oxygen-dependent killing (the complex assembles once the phagosome fuses with granules):

  • Why catalase ⊕? Catalase-negative bacteria leak their own H₂O₂ → the CGD phagocyte borrows it (MPO still works) → bug killed. Catalase ⊕ bugs destroy their H₂O₂ → no substrate for bleach → bug survives.
    • Classic organisms: Staphylococcus aureus and Aspergillus (also Serratia, Burkholderia cepacia, Nocardia).
  • Microbes survive inside phagocytes → persistent antigen → granulomas and inflammatory complications. Pus and abscesses do form (cells arrive; they just can't kill).
    • Clinical picture: recurrent pyoderma, pneumonia, deep skin / organ (liver) abscesses, osteomyelitis.

  • DHR test: stimulated neutrophils burst → ROS oxidize dihydrorhodamine 123 → fluorescent rhodamine 123 → flow cytometry shows a rightward shift. CGD → no or minimal shift. (Carrier mothers of X-linked CGD → two populations.)
  • NBT test (older): normal neutrophils reduce nitroblue tetrazolium → dark blue/black; CGD cells stay unstained (NBT-negative).

CheckA 4-year-old girl has had skin abscesses, pneumonia and osteomyelitis since infancy; B cells, T cells and Ig are normal. What will the phagocyte function test show?
Inability to generate an oxidative burst (CGD; likely p47phox since she's a girl). Not migration failure (LAD — no pus), not fusion failure (CHS — albinism), not MPO deficiency (mostly silent).

  • Three outcomes when M. tuberculosis meets a macrophage:
    • A — macrophage activated (IL-12 → Th1 → IFN-γ) → TB killed
    • B — macrophage killed by TB → lysis → spread
    • C — both survive → latent TB infection (LTBI)

CheckWhy would giving a CGD patient IFN-γ help, while giving it to a patient lacking the IFN-γ receptor would not?
CGD cells have intact IFN-γ receptors → IFN-γ boosts residual killing. Without the receptor, the signal cannot be received — the defect is downstream of the drug.
Step 1 HY
  • LAD I = AR defect in CD18 (β2 subunit of LFA-1 integrin) → impaired migration/chemotaxis; cord separation > 30 days, marked neutrophilia, absent pus.
  • LAD II: same fucosylation defect blocks H antigen → Bombay (hh) phenotype + developmental delay.
  • Chédiak-Higashi = AR LYST mutation → microtubule dysfunction in phagosome–lysosome fusion; giant granules, pancytopenia.
  • CGD → abnormal DHR (↓ green fluorescence); NBT fails to turn blue. MPO deficiency = usually asymptomatic (↑ Candida), DHR normal.
  • IL-12 receptor deficiency (AR) → ↓ Th1, ↓ IFN-γ → disseminated mycobacterial/fungal infection, often after BCG.
  • Severe neutropenia (ANC < 500/µL) → invasive Candida/Aspergillus. Asplenia → Howell-Jolly bodies, encapsulated sepsis; vaccinate before splenectomy.
Step 1 trap
  • No pus = trafficking problem (LAD). Abscesses + granulomas = killing problem (CGD). Both are "phagocyte" but opposite tissue findings.
  • Albinism + infections + bleeding = Chédiak-Higashi, not simple oculocutaneous albinism.
  • CGD vs MPO deficiency: both affect bleach production, but MPO deficiency keeps the burst (DHR normal) and is usually silent.
  • "Inability to produce superoxide" and "no oxidative burst" both describe CGD — pick the one the stem's test measures.
Memory tool
  • CGD catalase ⊕ organisms: "Some Bugs Need Sturdy Armor" → S. aureus, Burkholderia, Nocardia, Serratia, Aspergillus (catalase = their armor against your H₂O₂). Lock in S. aureus + Aspergillus first.
  • Chédiak-Higashi "PLAIN": Progressive neurodegeneration, Lymphohistiocytosis, Albinism (partial), recurrent pyogenic Infections, peripheral Neuropathy.
  • LAD: "Roll II, Stick I" — LAD II breaks rolling (selectin ligand), LAD I breaks sticking (integrin).
Buzzwords
"delayed umbilical cord separation" → LAD I "ulcers without pus" + neutrophilia → LAD I "ash-gray / silvery hair", giant granules → Chédiak-Higashi "catalase-positive", liver abscess, Aspergillus → CGD "DHR: no shift", "NBT-negative" → CGD "BCGosis", disseminated mycobacteria → IL-12/IFN-γ defect Howell-Jolly bodies → asplenia

Step 1 HY
  • Early (C1–C4) deficiency → ↑ SLE (poor immune-complex clearance).
  • Terminal (C5–C9) deficiency → recurrent meningococcal bacteremia → give meningococcal vaccine; eculizumab (anti-C5) creates the same risk.

Memory tool

"SLEarly" → early classical defects give SLE. "C5–9 → Neisseria fine": the MAC is what Neisseria fears.

Step 1 trap

Recurrent Neisseria with normal Ig and lymphocytes → terminal complement, not an antibody defect. And don't pick C3 for Neisseria — C3 loss gives a broad pyogenic picture.

B cellsAntibody (B-cell) deficiencies

Q9. What causes X-linked (Bruton) agammaglobulinemia, and why do these boys stay well for the first ~6 months?
  • Cause: mutation in BTK (Bruton tyrosine kinase) → pre-B-cell receptor signals fail → arrest at the pre-B stage → no mature B cells → deficient antibody of all classes. X-linked recessive → boys.
  • Infections: recurrent high-grade (pyogenic) bacterial, especially respiratory: pneumococcal otitis media, sinusitis, pneumonia.
    • Classic case: a 10-month-old with recurrent otitis media and diaper-area cellulitis growing Pseudomonas aeruginosa and S. aureus.
  • Timing: maternal IgG crosses the placenta → protects for 0 → 6 months → as it decays, infections appear after ~6 months.
  • Findings: near-absent CD19⁺ B cells, ↓ IgG, IgA, IgM, absent/scanty tonsils and small lymph nodes (no follicles / germinal centers).
  • History: the first PID described (1952 — a boy with no immunoglobulins and recurrent pneumonia/sinopulmonary infections) and the first given specific therapy (gamma-globulin injections). He improved but died of chronic lung disease in his 4th decade → chronic lung damage (bronchiectasis) remains the long-term risk.
    • Its gene (BTK) was the first molecular basis found for a PID (now 400+) → better diagnosis and treatment of humoral defects.

Hyper-IgM: B cells are there, but they can't switch

  • CD40L deficiency (on TFH cells; most common form is X-linked) → T cells can't signal B cells via CD40 → no class switching, no germinal centers → normal (or ↑) IgM, ↓↓ IgG, IgA, IgE.
    • CD40L also activates macrophages/DCs → opportunistic infections too.
  • AID deficiency → B-cell enzyme for class-switch recombination (and somatic hypermutation) missing → B cells make only IgM; T-cell help normal → no opportunistic infections; lymphoid hyperplasia.

CheckA 10-month-old boy, well until 4–5 months, has recurrent otitis and pneumonia, absent tonsils, all Ig classes very low and nearly no CD19⁺ cells. Which process is defective?
B-cell maturation via BTK (XLA). Not plasma-cell differentiation (CVID — B cells present), not thymic T development, not oxidative burst, not IgA switching.
Step 1 HY
  • XLA → live vaccines contraindicated (oral polio → vaccine-associated paralysis); ↑ enterovirus (echovirus meningoencephalitis) and Giardia.
  • CVID: B cells present but ↓ plasma cells / Ig; usually diagnosed after puberty; ↑ autoimmunity, bronchiectasis, lymphoma.
  • X-linked hyper-IgM: severe pyogenic infections early + Pneumocystis, Cryptosporidium, CMV.
Step 1 trap
  • XLA vs CVID: XLA = boy, no B cells, onset ~6 months. CVID = either sex, B cells present, later onset.
  • Hyper-IgM is not "too many plasma cells making IgM" — IgM is normal or high because switching fails.

T cellsCell-mediated (T-cell) deficiency

Q10. How does a 22q11.2 deletion produce DiGeorge syndrome, and how does it present?
  • Cause: microdeletion of chromosome 22q11.2 (22q11.2 deletion syndrome) — a chromosomal deletion, usually de novo (autosomal dominant when inherited). It is not X-linked.
  • Embryology: deletion disrupts the 3rd and 4th pharyngeal pouches →
    • 3rd pouch → thymus + inferior parathyroids; 4th pouch → superior parathyroids
    • → hypoplastic / absent thymus (too few mature, functional T cells) + parathyroids (hypocalcemia)
    • Associated 3rd/4th arch (neural crest) defects → aortic arch and conotruncal heart anomalies; 1st/2nd arch → facial and palate anomalies.

Heart defects

Congenital, conotruncal (tetralogy of Fallot, truncus arteriosus, interrupted aortic arch).

Facial features

Distinct facies: wide-set eyes, short philtrum, small mouth, low-set notched ears; cleft palate.

Immune problems

Thymic hypoplasia/aplasia → infections; also ↑ autoimmune disease (impaired thymic tolerance).

Hypocalcemia

Parathyroid defect → ↓ PTH → ↓ Ca²⁺ → muscle spasms / tetany, seizures. Often the first clue.

Delays

Speech, language, learning and developmental delays.

  • Infection timing and type: unlike antibody defects, infections appear early in infancy (< 6 months) → mainly viral and fungal (candidiasis, thrush).
  • Newborn clue: seizure on day 3 from hypocalcemia + murmur + absent thymic shadow on CXR.
CheckA newborn with a conotruncal defect has ionized Ca²⁺ 0.8 mmol/L and absolute lymphocytes 900/µL. One diagnosis for both labs?
DiGeorge: hypocalcemia (parathyroid hypoplasia) + lymphopenia (thymic hypoplasia). Lymphopenia alone could be SCID; the calcium and heart pin it to 22q11.2.
Memory tool

CATCH-22: Cardiac (conotruncal), Abnormal facies, Thymic hypoplasia, Cleft palate, Hypocalcemia, chromosome 22. Pouches 3 + 4 → "Three = Thymus".

Step 1 HY
  • Labs: ↓ T cells, ↓ PTH, ↓ Ca²⁺; CXR: absent thymic shadow. Confirm with FISH / chromosomal microarray.
  • Velocardiofacial syndrome = same deletion, palate/facial emphasis. Complete DiGeorge may need thymus transplant.
Step 1 trap

Embryology distractors: 1st/2nd arches (face, ear), ultimobranchial body (thyroid C cells, calcitonin), neural crest/notochord. The answer for thymus + parathyroid = 3rd and 4th pharyngeal pouches. And DiGeorge is not X-linked.

CombinedCombined B- and T-cell deficiencies (SCID)

Q11. What is SCID, how does it present, and how do the T/B/NK patterns point to the gene?
  • SCID is not one disease but a group of rare genetic disorders with both cellular (T) and humoral (B/antibody) deficiency — T cells fail to develop; in some forms B and/or NK cells are absent too.
    • Even when B cells are present, they can't make useful antibody without T-cell help → functionally combined.
    • Susceptible to viral, fungal and severe bacterial infections → "human agar plates".
  • Key clinical features:
    • Severe, recurrent infections that respond poorly to treatment; repeated pneumonia and ear infections
    • Failure to thrive (poor growth from infection burden)
    • Oral thrush and chronic diarrhea
    • Chronic skin rashes and skin infections — e.g., GVHD from engrafted maternal T cells (or unirradiated transfused blood)
    • Unusual opportunistic infections: Pneumocystis jirovecii (formerly P. carinii), a fungus
    • Family history of SCID / PID → consider it in the newborn

The common γ chain (γc) — why X-linked SCID is so severe

γc (IL-2Rγ) is a shared chain of cytokine receptors (not of the T-cell receptor). One mutation knocks out six cytokine signals. All signal through JAK3 (bound to γc) + JAK1 → STAT.

The logic behind the pattern

NK cells need…

IL-15 (γc/JAK3) and survival from ADA toxins — but no V(D)J and no IL-7. → NK spared in RAG/Artemis and IL-7Rα.

B cells need…

V(D)J (RAG/Artemis) — but human B cells develop without IL-7. → B present (non-functional) in γc/JAK3/IL-7Rα.

ADA = poison

dATP builds up → inhibits ribonucleotide reductase → no dNTPs → all dividing lymphocytes die → T⁻B⁻NK⁻.

The "bubble boy" — what one patient taught

  • David (born 1971, older brother died of SCID) was delivered germ-free and lived 12 years in plastic isolators waiting for a matched donor.
  • Received marrow from a histoincompatible sibling, T-cell depleted to prevent GVHD → 80 days later fever, thrombocytopenia, GI pain and bleeding → died on day 124.
  • Autopsy: EBV-driven B-cell proliferation (polyclonal → oligoclonal → monoclonal B-cell lymphoma) — the donor marrow carried latent EBV and he had no T cells to control it.
  • 1993: his cells revealed common γ chain mutations → showed how one shared receptor chain links IL-2, -4, -7, -9, -15, -21 → new therapies.
CheckA 3-month-old girl of consanguineous parents has thrush, chronic diarrhea and Pneumocystis pneumonia; flow shows T⁻ B⁻ NK⁺. Candidate genes?
RAG1/RAG2 or Artemis (AR, V(D)J defects). ADA would also remove NK cells; γc/JAK3 would leave B cells.
Step 1 HY
  • ADA-SCID: first disease treated with gene therapy; also enzyme replacement (PEG-ADA).
  • Omenn syndrome = hypomorphic (leaky) RAG mutation → erythroderma, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia, ↑ IgE, oligoclonal T cells.
  • Artemis-SCID patients are radiosensitive (general NHEJ defect).
  • SCID: absent thymic shadow, no germinal centers; no live vaccines, irradiated blood only. JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib) exploit this same pathway.
Step 1 trap
  • Girl with T⁻B⁺NK⁻ → JAK3 (AR), not X-linked γc.
  • T⁻ B⁺ with low Ig is still SCID — B cells are present but useless without T help.
  • ADA is a purine salvage enzyme (not de novo synthesis).
  • Only the γc form is X-linked; all other SCID forms listed are AR.
Memory tool

Read SCID patterns as "what does each cell need?"

  • Lose a signal (γc/JAK3) → T and NK go (IL-7, IL-15).
  • Lose recombination (RAG/Artemis) → the two rearranging cells go (T, B).
  • Lose IL-7Rα → only T goes.
  • Lose ADA → everything goes (poison).

LO 2A systematic diagnostic work-upFrom "how many infections are too many?" to the gene

By the end: you can list the warning signs, rule out non-immune causes, order the right first-line labs, choose targeted tests by presentation, use newborn clues and TRECs, and know which test confirms each disorder.
Q1. How many infections are too many — when should you start an immunodeficiency work-up?

Warning signs

  • ≥ 8 new ear infections in 1 year
  • ≥ 2 serious sinus infections in 1 year
  • ≥ 2 months on antibiotics with little effect
  • ≥ 2 pneumonias in 1 year
  • ≥ 2 deep-seated infections
  • Recurrent, deep skin or organ abscesses
  • Persistent thrush (mouth or skin) after age 1
  • Need for IV antibiotics to clear infections
  • Infant failure to gain weight / grow
  • Family history of primary immunodeficiency

Pattern matters as much as number

  • More than ~2 serious infections not easily cleared by ordinary antibiotics (needing IV or stronger drugs) → think immune defect.
  • Severe, unusual (opportunistic) or hard-to-clear infections count more than frequent mild colds.
  • Remember: "8 ears, 2 of everything else."

Q2. How do you match the therapy to the defect?

XLA / antibody defects

Ig replacement (IVIG/SCIG) + prompt antibiotics. Killed vaccines safe but add little; no live vaccines.

SCID

HSCT (or gene therapy) = cure. Bridge: isolation, TMP-SMX, IVIG, antifungals, irradiated CMV-negative blood.

CGD

Prophylactic TMP-SMX + itraconazole + IFN-γ; consider HSCT.

DiGeorge

Calcium (+ vitamin D) for hypocalcemia, cardiac repair; partial forms often improve; complete forms → thymus transplant.

Complement

Vaccinate (meningococcal, pneumococcal, Hib); early antibiotics; ± prophylaxis.

LAD / CHS

Antibiotics; HSCT is curative for severe LAD I and Chédiak-Higashi.

→ Rule of thumb: Ig replacement fixes only antibody deficits; defects of T cells or phagocytes need cell replacement (HSCT / gene therapy).

Q3. Which safety rules protect patients with immune deficiencies?
  • No live attenuated vaccines in significant T-cell, combined or antibody defects (BCG, oral polio, rotavirus, MMR, varicella) → BCGosis, vaccine-associated polio, disseminated vaccine virus.
  • Irradiated (and CMV-negative) blood products for SCID / T-cell–deficient infants → irradiation inactivates donor T cells that could engraft and cause fatal transfusion-associated GVHD.
    • Same principle as the GVHD-like rash from engrafted maternal T cells.
  • Transplant lessons (the "bubble boy"): T-cell–depleted, mismatched marrow carrying latent EBV → uncontrolled EBV-driven B-cell lymphoma. Modern HSCT screens donors and monitors for viral reactivation.
CheckA boy with XLA on monthly IVIG: which vaccine type is contraindicated, and why do killed vaccines add little?
Live vaccines (e.g., oral polio) are contraindicated. Killed vaccines are safe but he cannot make antibody — protection comes from the IVIG.

Step 1 HY
  • IVIG is the treatment of choice for XLA and CVID; not for selective IgA deficiency (risk of anaphylaxis; IVIG contains trace IgA and doesn't replace mucosal IgA).

  • TMP-SMX prophylaxis is aimed at Pneumocystis in T-cell defects (and at staph in CGD).

1A 10-month-old boy has had three episodes of otitis media and one pneumonia since age 6 months. Examination shows very small tonsils and barely palpable lymph nodes. 2A newborn boy has a seizure on day 3 of life. He has a harsh murmur, low-set notched ears and a short philtrum. Serum calcium is low, and a chest x-ray shows no thymic shadow. Abnormal development of which structures best explains the seizure and the radiograph?

C — 3rd and 4th pharyngeal pouches (22q11.2 deletion, DiGeorge) → thymus and parathyroids. 1st/2nd arches → face/ear; 2nd pouch → palatine tonsil; ultimobranchial body → C cells (calcitonin), not hypocalcemia; neural tube is unrelated.

3A 2-month-old boy has failure to thrive, persistent oral thrush, chronic diarrhea and an eczematous rash. No lymph nodes are palpable and the thymic shadow is absent. Flow cytometry: CD3⁺ T cells absent, CD19⁺ B cells normal in number, NK cells absent; immunoglobulins are low. Which is the most likely defect?

4A 5-year-old girl has had recurrent skin abscesses, a liver abscess growing Staphylococcus aureus, and Aspergillus pneumonia. Lymphocyte subsets and immunoglobulins are normal. Which test result is most expected?

D — CGD. Catalase ⊕ organisms + abscesses → no NADPH oxidase burst → DHR not oxidized. A girl suggests AR p47phox. CD18 loss = LAD (no pus, not abscesses); CH50 = complement; giant granules = Chédiak-Higashi; absent TRECs = SCID.

5A 19-year-old college student is hospitalized for Neisseria meningitidis bacteremia; he had meningococcal meningitis at 14. CBC, immunoglobulins and lymphocyte subsets are normal. Which is the most likely underlying deficiency?

E — terminal complement (C5–C9). Without the MAC, Neisseria can't be lysed. C1-INH → hereditary angioedema; C2 (early) → pyogenic infections + SLE; MPO deficiency → usually silent (Candida); IgA deficiency → sinopulmonary/GI infections.

6A 4-week-old boy has omphalitis; the umbilical cord is still attached. WBC is 48,000/µL with 85% neutrophils. A skin lesion on his thigh is a deep ulcer with a thick scab and no pus. Which process is defective?

A — LAD I (CD18 β2 integrin). Neutrophils are made (neutrophilia) but can't stick and exit → delayed cord separation, no pus. B = Chédiak-Higashi; C = CGD (pus/abscesses form); D = mycobacterial susceptibility; E = complement (normal cord).

7A 2-year-old with light silvery hair and pale skin has recurrent pyogenic skin infections, frequent nosebleeds and easy bruising. Nystagmus is noted. Which mechanism underlies his disorder?

C — Chédiak-Higashi (LYST). Trafficking defect → giant granules in neutrophils, abnormal melanosomes (albinism), defective platelet dense granules (bleeding), neuro findings. Isolated tyrosinase defect (oculocutaneous albinism) does not cause infections or bleeding. A = LAD II; B = CGD; D = MPO deficiency.

8A 7-month-old boy develops a draining nodule at his BCG vaccination site, followed by fever, hepatosplenomegaly and positive cultures for Mycobacterium bovis. Lymphocyte counts and immunoglobulins are normal; DHR is normal. A defect in which molecule is most likely?

B — IL-12/IFN-γ axis → macrophages never activated to kill intracellular mycobacteria → disseminated BCG (BCGosis). Normal DHR excludes CGD; normal lymphocytes/Ig exclude SCID and XLA; CD18 and LYST give pyogenic, not mycobacterial, disease; C5 → Neisseria.

9A healthy-appearing 10-day-old girl's newborn screen reports undetectable T-cell receptor excision circles on the dried blood spot. Which is the most appropriate next step?

D. Low TRECs = few new T cells → confirm lymphopenia and define the T/B/NK pattern, then genetic testing. Waiting loses the infection-free window for HSCT; rotavirus is a live vaccine (contraindicated); DHR and CH50 test phagocytes and complement.

10A 3-month-old girl whose parents are first cousins has Pneumocystis pneumonia, thrush and chronic diarrhea. Flow cytometry shows absent T and B cells with normal NK cells. Her T cells failed to develop because of a defect in which process?

E — V(D)J recombination (RAG1/2 or Artemis), T⁻B⁻NK⁺. NK cells don't rearrange receptor genes, so they're spared. Purine salvage (ADA) also kills NK cells; γc signaling spares B cells; pre-BCR (BTK) spares T cells; DiGeorge spares B cells.

11An 8-year-old boy with X-linked chronic granulomatous disease continues to have serious infections despite TMP-SMX and itraconazole prophylaxis. A subcutaneous cytokine is added. What is its main benefit?

A — IFN-γ. Upregulates microbicidal mechanisms, ↓ granuloma inflammation and ↓ severe infections. It does not fix the gene (only HSCT/gene therapy does); G-CSF raises counts; IVIG replaces antibody.

12A 4-month-old with adenosine deaminase–deficient SCID is awaiting stem cell transplant. Accumulation of which metabolite explains why all his lymphocyte lineages are missing?

C. Without ADA (purine salvage), deoxyadenosine → dATP accumulates → inhibits ribonucleotide reductase → no dNTPs for DNA synthesis → rapidly dividing T, B and NK precursors die (T⁻B⁻NK⁻). The others are unrelated metabolic disorders.

13A 14-month-old boy has recurrent sinopulmonary infections and Pneumocystis pneumonia. Serum IgM is elevated; IgG, IgA and IgE are very low. B-cell numbers are normal. Which molecule is most likely defective?

B — CD40L (X-linked hyper-IgM). No T-cell help → no class switching (IgM high/normal, others low), and impaired macrophage activation → opportunistic infections. BTK = no B cells; CD18 = LAD; ADA = SCID; MHC I deficiency → low CD8.

14A 2-year-old has had two pneumonias this year, chronic sinusitis requiring two months of antibiotics with little effect, and one infection that needed IV antibiotics. Growth is normal and CBC is normal. Which is the best next test?

D. Recurrent sinopulmonary bacterial infections → antibody (most common category) → quantitative Ig + vaccine responses (function). NBT/DHR comes later if humoral tests are normal; targeted genetics follows a pattern; TRECs are a newborn T-cell screen.
