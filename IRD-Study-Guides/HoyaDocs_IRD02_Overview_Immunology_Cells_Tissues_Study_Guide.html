LO 1What the immune system is forDefend against threats from outside and inside by telling dangerous non-self from self

By the end: you can state the immune system's job in one sentence, give examples of non-self, name the four ways the system fails, and define antigen, antibody, cytokine, leukocyte, complement and humoral vs cellular immunity.
Q1. What is the immune system actually trying to do?
  • Think of it as an oath of office: support and defend the body against all enemies, foreign and domestic.
    • Foreign threats → pathogens: bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites.
    • Domestic threats → our own cells gone wrong: pre-cancerous / transformed cells, self-reactive lymphocytes.
  • Everything depends on one ability: distinguishing self from dangerous non-self.
  • Two jobs, every time:
    • Sensors → detect threatening non-self (attack a virus, not your lunch).
    • Effectors → actually destroy or remove it.
  • "Non-self" is broad: a pathogen, a cell expressing tumor antigens, a transplanted organ, or something benign like pollen or shellfish.
  • The immune system touches every organ system, and immune-driven inflammation is part of many disease processes you will meet later.
  • Why it feels confusing: many players, a complex network of interactions, and lots of redundancy. You do not need everything before you can learn anything: build the skeleton first (this guide), then add detail.
CheckA kidney transplant and a patch of pollen are both "non-self". Why is one supposed to be attacked and the other ignored?
Neither is dangerous in itself. The system should ignore harmless non-self (pollen); attacking it = allergy. A transplant is attacked because it is non-self (that is why recipients need immunosuppression), even though it is not harmful.
Q2. What happens when the system gets it wrong?

Disease occurs when harmful non-self is ignored, or when self or harmless non-self is attacked.

ConditionWhat went wrongType of error
Infectious diseaseFails to clear a pathogenIgnores / under-responds to harmful non-self
CancerFails to eliminate altered (malignant) selfIgnores a domestic threat
AutoimmunityAttacks normal selfAttacks self
Allergy / hypersensitivityAttacks harmless non-self (pollen, shellfish)Over-responds to harmless non-self
CheckWhich two conditions are failures to respond, and which two are responses aimed at the wrong target?
Failure to respond: infection and cancer. Wrong target: autoimmunity (self) and allergy (harmless non-self).

How selectively an antibody binds one antigen over others ("which target?").

How strongly it binds that antigen ("how tight?"). Rises during a response by affinity maturation (LO 3).

CheckIs complement humoral or cellular? Is it innate or adaptive?
Humoral (a soluble protein cascade) and innate. "Humoral" describes where it works (in fluid), not which arm it belongs to: antibodies are humoral + adaptive.
Step 1 trap
  • Humoral ≠ adaptive. Humoral = soluble (antibody and complement). Complement is humoral but innate.
  • On boards, "cell-mediated immunity" usually means T-cell immunity (CD4/CD8), contrasted with antibody (B-cell) immunity.
Memory tool

"Humor" = body fluid (old medicine's four humors) → humoral factors float in plasma, lymph and secretions.

Specificity = which lock; affinity = how tight the key grips.

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A man develops hives and wheezing every spring when trees pollinate. Which kind of immune error is this?
Attacking harmless non-self (allergy / hypersensitivity). Pollen is non-self but not dangerous; the response itself causes disease.
A2A cell-free sample of serum binds and inactivates a bacterial toxin in a test tube. Is the activity humoral or cellular?
Humoral. No cells present → a soluble factor (antibody neutralizing the toxin).
A3Ten days after receiving horse-derived antivenom, a woman has fever, an itchy rash and painful joints. Explain.
Serum sickness. Her immune system treats horse proteins as antigen → makes antibody → immune complexes deposit in vessels/joints/kidney (type III hypersensitivity).
A4Two antibodies both recognize only the same toxin. Antibody X releases it easily; antibody Y holds on tightly. How do they differ?
Same specificity, different affinity: Y has higher affinity.

Q1. What are the core differences between innate and adaptive immunity?
FeatureInnateAdaptive

CheckA population of cells responds within hours to a bacterium and responds identically when the same bacterium returns months later. Innate or adaptive?
Innate: fast, pre-formed, no memory. An adaptive response would be slow the first time and faster/stronger the second.
CheckWhat is clonality, and which cells have it?
Each B or T cell carries a receptor for only one antigen; when it divides, its whole clone shares that specificity → adaptive cells are clonal. Innate cells lack clonality: one macrophage carries many different pattern receptors.
Memory tool

Innate = a fire alarm: built in, rings the same for every fire, instant. Adaptive = a detective: slow to build the case the first time, but never forgets a face.

Step 1 trap

"Fast" is not the same as "innate" on a second exposure: a memory response is fast too, but it is adaptive (antigen-specific, bigger than last time). Ask: is it specific and does it improve?

Q3. What physical and chemical barriers guard each body site?

Physical (mechanical) barriers

  • Skin (keratinized epithelium)
  • Cilia (mucociliary escalator)
  • Mucosa and mucus
  • Flushing: tears/blinking, urine, coughing/sneezing, peristalsis

Chemical barriers

  • HCl in the stomach
  • Lactic acid in the vagina (and on skin)
  • Lysozyme in sweat and tears → breaks down peptidoglycan in bacterial walls
  • Sebum, propionic acid, antimicrobial compounds; normal flora compete
SiteBarriers
EyeBlinking, tears, lysozyme
SkinStructural barrier, sweat + sebum, lactic and propionic acid, lysozyme, normal flora
Respiratory tractCoughing, sneezing, mucus, ciliary action, phagocytes, lysozyme

Urogenital tractFlushing (lavaging) by urine, acidic urine, lysozyme, vaginal lactic acid (normal flora)
  • Pathogens most often enter across mucosal surfaces (eyes, nose, mouth, airway, gut, urogenital tract) = the path of least resistance. Intact skin is formidable unless broken (wound, scratch, insect bite).
    • That is why most immune cells sit just beneath skin and mucosae.
CheckWhy is lysozyme more effective against gram-positive bacteria?
Lysozyme cleaves peptidoglycan; gram-positives have a thick, exposed peptidoglycan wall, while gram-negatives hide a thin layer under an outer membrane.
Step 1 HY
  • At mucosal surfaces, secretory IgA performs immune exclusion by blocking microbial attachment.
  • Barrier failure patterns: burn injury → Pseudomonas/Staph; cystic fibrosis / ciliary dyskinesia → recurrent sinopulmonary infection; PPI use (less gastric acid) → ↑ C. difficile and enteric infection; broad-spectrum antibiotics kill normal flora → C. difficile, Candida.
Step 1 trap

Flushing (urine, tears, peristalsis) is a mechanical barrier, not chemical. Urinary stasis (BPH, stones, reflux) removes flushing → recurrent UTI.

Q4. How do innate cells recognize "danger" without antigen-specific receptors?

MAMPs (= PAMPs)

Microbe- (pathogen-) associated molecular patterns: found on pathogens. Shared by whole classes of microbes (e.g., LPS, flagellin, viral nucleic acids).

DAMPs

Damage-associated molecular patterns: released by damaged host cells / tissue (e.g., mitochondrial DNA, histones, heat-shock proteins). Explains sterile inflammation.

  • Both are detected by pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) that are common to many innate leukocytes (e.g., Toll-like receptors).
  • One macrophage expresses many PRRs at once: TLR-2, TLR-4, the LPS receptor CD14, mannose, glucan and scavenger receptors → innate cells lack clonality.
  • Sentinel cells that detect invaders first: epithelial cells, dendritic cells, macrophages. PRR engagement → release of cytokines + chemokines → effector cells recruited.
CheckA crush injury with no infection still produces redness and swelling. What triggered the innate response?
DAMPs from damaged cells, detected by the same PRR families that detect MAMPs.
Step 1 HY
  • CD14 + TLR4 recognize LPS (gram-negative endotoxin) → septic shock cytokines.
  • TLR2 → lipoteichoic acid / lipoproteins (gram-positive). TLR5 → flagellin.
  • PRR → NF-κB → IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α (pro-inflammatory cytokines).
Memory tool

MAMPs mark Microbes; DAMPs mark Damage. Same receptor families read both.

Q6. How does the innate system hand off to adaptive immunity, and how does adaptive immunity return the favor?

The link between innate and adaptive immunity = dendritic cells activating CD4⁺ T cells. Activated T cells then activate CD8⁺ T cells and convert B cells into antibody-making plasma cells.

Uptakeconventional DC (cDC) takes up microbes by phagocytosis, receptor-mediated endocytosis or (macro)pinocytosis
→
LicensingPRR signal → ↑ MHC I/II, B7 (CD80/86), adhesion molecules, CCR7
→
Migrationvia lymphatics to the draining lymph node (T-cell zone, paracortex)
→
Presentationpeptides on MHC I and II → naive T cells activated
  • cDCs are innate cells, but they are the main cells able to activate naive T cells → the bridge.
  • Adaptive → innate feedback: antigen recognition makes innate tools targeted:
    • Antibodies direct complement, phagocytes and NK cells to the pathogen.
    • T cells (especially TH1) activate macrophages.
CheckMice lacking CCR7 pick up a skin microbe normally, yet no T-cell response develops. Why?
Without CCR7, antigen-loaded DCs cannot migrate to the lymph node, so they never meet naive T cells, which wait in secondary lymphoid organs.
Step 1 HY
  • DCs are the most potent activators of naive T cells. They need signal 1 (MHC–peptide → TCR) + signal 2 (B7 → CD28).
  • DC subsets: cDC1 → activate CD8⁺ T (cross-presentation on MHC I); cDC2 → activate CD4⁺ T; plasmacytoid DC → huge amounts of type I interferon in viral infection; follicular DC → holds intact antigen for B cells in follicles.
  • NK cells kill antibody-coated cells by ADCC via CD16 (FcγRIII).
Step 1 trap

Follicular DCs are not true DCs: not from bone marrow, no MHC presentation; they just display intact antigen on their surface for B cells. Don't pick them as the cell that primes naive T cells.

Memory tool

DC = "Delivery Courier": picks up the package in the tissue, drives the lymphatic highway (CCR7 = the GPS), delivers to the T cells in the node.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A man with an enlarged prostate retains urine and has recurrent UTIs. Which innate barrier failed?
Flushing (lavaging) action of urine. Stasis lets bacteria adhere and multiply.
A2Cell-free plasma from a healthy donor lyses a suspension of gram-negative bacteria. What is responsible, and which arm/category?
Complement: humoral, innate (can also be triggered by natural antibody). No cells are present, so it must be soluble.
A3Predict: a drug blocks the maturation of dendritic cells (no ↑ MHC, B7 or CCR7). What happens to the first response to a new virus?
Innate responses (macrophages, complement, NK cells) proceed, but naive T cells are not primed → no CD8 killing, no T help for B cells → weak or absent adaptive response.
A4A patient's serum CRP rises sharply on day 2 of pneumonia. Which organ made it, in response to what?
The liver, in response to cytokines (mainly IL-6) from activated macrophages: an acute phase protein of the induced innate response.

By the end: you can draw the lineage tree (and name the NK exception), give the function of every myeloid cell, sort any component into innate or adaptive, compare BCR vs TCR and MHC I vs II, name the T-cell subsets and their targets, and list the antibody effector functions and the soluble factors.
Q1. Where do all immune cells come from?
  • All leukocytes arise from the hematopoietic stem cell in bone marrow → two branches.
  • Common myeloid progenitor → essentially all innate leukocytes.
  • Common lymphoid progenitor → B and T cells (adaptive) plus NK cells and innate lymphoid cells (innate!).
CheckA large granular cell from the common lymphoid progenitor kills virus-infected cells within hours, without prior exposure. Which cell, which arm?
NK cell → innate, despite lymphoid origin: no clonal receptor, no priming needed.
Step 1 trap
  • Lymphoid ≠ adaptive. NK cells and ILCs are lymphoid but innate.
  • Macrophages are tissue-resident; the circulating form is the monocyte. Mast cells live in tissue; basophils circulate.
Step 1 HY
  • NK cells: CD16, CD56; kill cells that have lost MHC I (viruses/tumors downregulate it; "missing self") with perforin + granzymes; activated by IL-2, IL-12, IFN-α/β. NK receptors read MHC I only, not MHC II.
  • ILCs make effector cytokines in response to cytokines from macrophages and DCs (innate mirror of TH subsets).
Q2. What does each myeloid (innate) cell do?

Macrophage tissue

Phagocytosis + bactericidal killing; antigen presentation (MHC II); sensor that releases cytokines/chemokines. Comes from blood monocytes.

Dendritic cell tissue → node

Antigen uptake in peripheral sites + antigen presentation to naive T cells. Long cytoplasmic processes.

Neutrophil blood

Phagocytosis + bactericidal killing; main effector vs extracellular bacteria; first recruited cell. Multilobed nucleus.

Eosinophil blood

Kills antibody-coated parasites (helminths) by granule release. Bilobed nucleus, bright pink granules.

Basophil blood

Promotes allergic responses and augments anti-parasitic immunity. Dense dark-purple granules.

Mast cell tissue

Releases granules of histamine + other active agents → inflammation, allergy; also anti-parasitic.

Functional groupCells
PhagocytesMacrophages, dendritic cells, neutrophils (eosinophils weakly)
Professional APCs (MHC II)Macrophages, dendritic cells (+ B cells, which are lymphoid)
Mediator-releasing (granule) cellsEosinophils, basophils, mast cells

Match the effector to the size of the threat

Small pathogens

Bacteria → macrophages + neutrophils (engulf them)

Huge pathogens

Helminths → eosinophils + basophils (and mast cells): too big to eat → granule release

CheckA chemotherapy patient has an absolute neutrophil count near zero. What infections is she most at risk for?
Bacterial (and fungal) infections: neutrophils are the main phagocytes for small extracellular pathogens.
Step 1 HY
  • Neutrophil chemotaxis: IL-8, C5a, LTB₄. Febrile neutropenia → empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics.
  • Eosinophil granules: major basic protein. Eosinophilia: parasites, asthma/allergy, adrenal insufficiency, neoplasia.
  • Basophil granules: histamine + heparin; basophilia → think CML.
  • Mast cells bind IgE via FcεRI; cross-linking → degranulation (type I). Cromolyn stabilizes mast cells.
  • Macrophages activated by IFN-γ (TH1) → granulomas.
Memory tool

Small bugs get eaten, big worms get bombed. Phagocytes (macrophage, neutrophil) eat; granulocytes (eos, baso, mast) throw granule "bombs" at things too big to swallow.

Q4. How do lymphocytes get receptors for tens of millions of antigens?
  • B cells develop in bone marrow ("B for bone marrow"); T cells develop in the thymus ("T for thymus").
  • After development they circulate among secondary lymphoid tissues waiting for their antigen; if none comes within a few weeks, they die and are replaced.
  • Each lymphocyte has one receptor specificity (clonality). Humans can make tens of millions of unique antibodies and TCRs by:
    1. V(D)J recombination at receptor loci: (a) mixing gene segments, (b) imprecise joining of segments
    2. Random pairing of heavy and light chains (BCR) or α and β chains (TCR)
    3. Somatic hypermutation of antibody variable regions (B cells only, after activation)

CheckWhy can a B cell recognize a bacterial toxin floating in plasma but a T cell cannot?
The BCR binds native antigen directly. A TCR only sees peptide fragments presented on MHC by another cell.
Step 1 HY
  • V(D)J recombination needs RAG1/RAG2 (then NHEJ repair). RAG deficiency → SCID (no B or T cells; NK cells preserved).
  • Somatic hypermutation + class switching need AID (activation-induced cytidine deaminase).
Q5. How do T cells see antigen: MHC class I vs class II?

T cells cannot recognize free-floating antigen. An antigen-presenting cell digests proteins into short peptides and holds them up on MHC.

MHC class IMHC class II
Expressed onAll nucleated cellsProfessional APCs: dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells
Peptides presentedSynthesized inside the cell (viral, tumor)Endocytosed (exogenous) peptides

CheckA virus infects respiratory epithelium. Which T cells can recognize the infected epithelial cells directly?
CD8⁺ T cells, via viral peptide on MHC I. Epithelial cells lack MHC II, so CD4 cells are primed by DCs in the lymph node instead.
Memory tool

Rule of 8: CD8 × MHC I = 8; CD4 × MHC II = 8.

Step 1 HY
  • MHC I = HLA-A, -B, -C + β₂-microglobulin; loaded in the ER via TAP.
  • MHC II = HLA-DP, -DQ, -DR; invariant chain/CLIP blocks the groove until the endosome.
Q6. What kinds of T cells are there, and which pathogens does each handle?

  • CD8⁺ killer T cells recognize viral (or bacterial) peptides on MHC I and kill infected cells; they can also kill tumor cells presenting tumor antigen on MHC I.

No B or T cells (no V(D)J recombination → SCID). NK cells remain: their receptors are germline-encoded.
A5A tumor stops expressing MHC I to hide from CD8 T cells. Which innate cell should now kill it?
NK cell: loss of MHC I removes its inhibitory signal ("missing self").

Q2. Can I list every step in order, from entry to clearance?
  1. Barrier breach: microbe gets past skin/mucosa (wound, mucosal entry).
  2. Immediate innate: antimicrobial proteins and complement may destroy it at once.
  3. Sensing: tissue macrophages engulf it (PRRs see MAMPs) → chemokines recruit effectors, cytokines start inflammation.
  4. Induced innate: neutrophils and monocytes (→ macrophages) arrive and phagocytose; NK cells attack infected cells; local (swelling, redness) and systemic (fever) effects; liver makes acute phase proteins.
  5. Antigen capture: cDCs take up the microbe and travel to the local lymph node.
  6. Presentation: cDC digests proteins → short peptides on MHC II (to CD4⁺) and MHC I (to CD8⁺).
  7. T-cell activation: CD4⁺ TH cells help CD8⁺ cells, B cells and innate cells that recognize the same antigen; CD8⁺ cells travel to the site and kill infected cells.
  8. B-cell response: some activated B cells quickly become plasma cells secreting low–moderate affinity antibody; others undergo affinity maturation + class switch → high-affinity plasma cells or memory B cells.
  9. Clearance: antibody neutralizes the virus and marks infected cells for innate effector cells; infection resolves.
  10. Memory: memory B and T cells respond faster next time → re-infection mild or asymptomatic.
CheckOrder these: (a) plasma cells secrete high-affinity antibody, (b) DCs migrate to the lymph node, (c) macrophages release cytokines at the site, (d) CD4⁺ T cells are activated.
c → b → d → a. Innate sensing first; DCs carry antigen to the node; naive T cells are primed; T help then drives B cells to high-affinity plasma cells.
Step 1 trap

The adaptive response is organized in the lymph node, not at the infection site. Naive T cells never go to the tissue to find antigen; the DC brings it to them. Only effector T cells return to the tissue.

Memory tool

"Eat → Alarm → Courier → Commander → Factory → Archive": macrophage eats, cytokines alarm, DC couriers, CD4 commands, plasma cells manufacture, memory cells archive.

Q3. Why is the second encounter with the same microbe usually mild?

Primary response

  • Slow lag (days–weeks)
  • First plasma cells → low–moderate affinity, mostly IgM
  • Ends by leaving memory B + T cells

Secondary response

  • Faster, bigger response from memory cells
  • Already class-switched, high-affinity (IgG, IgA)
  • Microbe cleared before much disease → mild/asymptomatic

This is the principle behind vaccination: create memory without the disease.

Step 1 HY
  • Serology: IgM = acute/primary infection; IgG = later, past infection or immunity (e.g., HBV anti-HBc IgM = acute).
  • Infants <2 yr respond poorly to polysaccharide antigens (T-independent) → conjugate vaccines (polysaccharide + protein) recruit T help and memory.
Buzzwords
"Faster, stronger on re-exposure" → adaptive memory"Low-affinity IgM first" → primary response"Fever + CRP" → cytokines, induced innate

Day 1: neutrophils (and macrophages): induced innate. Day 10: antigen-specific effector T cells and antibody (adaptive) have arrived.
A2A child's tender, enlarged neck lymph node appears a few days after a throat infection. What is happening inside the node?
DCs have carried antigen in; naive T and B cells are proliferating (T-cell activation, germinal centers) → node enlarges (reactive lymphadenopathy).
A3A vaccinated adult is exposed to measles and never gets sick. Which cells explain this?
Memory B and T cells from vaccination respond rapidly with high-affinity antibody and effector T cells before the virus spreads.

By the end: you can sort organs into primary vs secondary, give the job of each, locate B and T zones in a lymph node and spleen, and predict what goes wrong when an organ is missing.
Q1. What is the difference between primary and secondary lymphoid organs?
Primary (central)Secondary (peripheral)

RoleWhere immune cells develop (B in marrow; T mature in thymus)Where mature lymphocytes reside, meet antigen, and the adaptive response is organized
Memory tool

Primary = the school (marrow, thymus: cells are trained). Secondary = the job site (nodes, spleen, MALT: cells go to work when antigen shows up).

Q2. What do the bone marrow and thymus do?

Bone marrow

  • Site of hematopoiesis → all blood cell types
  • Primary site of B-cell development
  • Home of long-lived plasma cells

Thymus

  • Immature precursors migrate from marrow → mature as thymocytes into T cells → enter blood
  • Lobules: dark, densely cellular cortex + paler medulla
  • Hassall (thymic) corpuscles in medulla = diagnostic
  • No germinal centers, ever; involutes with age (fat)
Step 1 HY
  • Thymus cortex = positive selection (MHC restriction); medulla = negative selection (AIRE, removes self-reactive T cells).
  • Thymic epithelium from the 3rd pharyngeal pouch. DiGeorge (22q11.2): absent thymus + parathyroids, conotruncal heart defects, hypocalcemia → absent thymic shadow, T-cell deficiency.
  • Thymoma → myasthenia gravis.
Step 1 trap

A lymphoid organ with germinal centers is not the thymus. The thymus is a primary organ where T cells develop; it does not mount antigen responses.

Q3. How is a lymph node built, and where do B and T cells sit?
  • Lymph nodes filter lymph; they are encapsulated (connective-tissue capsule), larger than nodules and contain lymphoid nodules.
  • Lymph arrives by multiple afferent lymphatics through the capsule → leaves by the efferent lymphatic at the hilum.
  • Zones: outer cortex follicles = B cells (germinal centers when activated) · deep cortex (paracortex) = T cells + DCs · medullary cords = B cells and plasma cells.
  • Lymphocytes enter a node two ways: through afferent lymphatics or across high endothelial venules (HEVs) (cuboidal endothelium in the paracortex) from blood.
  • Germinal center (pale center of a secondary follicle) = antigen-activated B cells proliferating + affinity maturation/class switching, with follicular DCs, macrophages and lymphoblasts.
CheckWhere in the node do licensed DCs meet naive T cells?
The paracortex (deep cortex, T-cell zone).
Step 1 HY
  • Paracortex: underdeveloped in DiGeorge; enlarges in viral infection (EBV) → paracortical hyperplasia.
  • Follicles: poorly formed in X-linked agammaglobulinemia (no mature B cells).
  • Naive lymphocytes home to HEVs via L-selectin (CD62L); CD44 is an adhesion molecule (binds endothelium/matrix) that supports lymphocyte trafficking.
Memory tool

B on the outside (Border follicles), T in the middle (paraCorTex), plasma cells by the exit (medulla).

Q4. What does the spleen do that lymph nodes cannot?
  • The spleen filters blood (lymph nodes filter lymph) → traps blood-borne pathogens and removes old/damaged blood cells. Encapsulated.

White pulp

  • Dense lymphoid tissue → immune responses to blood-borne antigen
  • B cells: follicles / germinal centers
  • T cells: periarteriolar lymphoid sheath (PALS) around the central artery
  • Marginal zone (between white and red): main site of blood-borne antigen capture

Red pulp

  • Lighter, blood-filled cords and sinusoids
  • Macrophages remove old/damaged RBCs
CheckAfter splenectomy, why can't lymph nodes take over?
Lymph nodes filter lymph, not blood; blood-borne bacteria bypass them.
Step 1 HY
  • Asplenia (surgery, sickle cell autosplenectomy) → sepsis with encapsulated organisms: S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae b, N. meningitidis, Salmonella → vaccinate.
  • Smear after splenectomy: Howell-Jolly bodies, target cells.
  • Marginal zone macrophages + B cells handle polysaccharide antigens.
Q5. What is MALT and why is it the largest lymphoid tissue?
  • MALT = mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue: unencapsulated lymphoid tissue in the mucosa of the GI, respiratory, urinary and reproductive tracts.
  • The largest collection of lymphoid tissue in the body, because mucosal surface area is enormous and mucosae are the main portal of entry.
  • Forms: single cells in the lamina propria, diffuse tissue, or nodules (temporary aggregates during infection, or permanent: tonsils, Peyer patches, appendix).

Step 1 HY
  • Lymphocytes first primed in gut lymphoid tissue (Peyer patch) acquire gut-homing receptors → oral vaccines (e.g., rotavirus) give gut immunity; injected ones generally don't.
  • Gut tissue is tolerance-biased: lamina propria macrophages/DCs make IL-10; secretory IgA does not activate complement.
  • Peyer patch M cells sample antigen; Salmonella/Shigella/Yersinia invade through them.

1A 9-year-old boy gets a wooden splinter in his palm. Eighteen hours later the area is red, warm, swollen and tender, and a small amount of pus is expressed. Which cell type predominates in the pus?

C. Neutrophil. Within hours, macrophage chemokines recruit neutrophils (induced innate), the main phagocytes for small extracellular bacteria; pus is mostly neutrophils. Eosinophils/basophils target large parasites; plasma cells and CD8 T cells are adaptive and take days.

2A 2-week-old infant has tetany from hypocalcemia, a conotruncal heart defect and no thymic shadow on chest x-ray. A lymph node biopsy would show the greatest depletion in which region?

A. Paracortex. DiGeorge syndrome → thymic aplasia → no T-cell maturation; the paracortex (deep cortex) is the T-cell zone. Follicles (B cells from marrow) and medullary cords (B/plasma cells) are relatively preserved; sinuses and hilum are not lymphocyte zones.

3A 25-year-old man had his spleen removed after a motorcycle crash. Two years later he develops high fever and hypotension within hours. Which organism is most likely?

E. S. pneumoniae. The spleen filters blood and its marginal zone responds to polysaccharide capsules; asplenia → overwhelming sepsis by encapsulated bacteria. Candida (TH17/neutrophils), CMV (T cells), Giardia (IgA) and TB (TH1/macrophages) are not specifically linked to asplenia.

4A 4-month-old girl with chronic diarrhea, thrush and failure to thrive has a mutation in RAG1. Which cell type is most likely present in normal numbers?

B. NK cells. RAG1/2 are needed for V(D)J recombination of BCR and TCR genes → no B or T cells (SCID). NK cells come from the common lymphoid progenitor but use germline-encoded receptors, so they are spared. All other options need a rearranged antigen receptor.

5A hepatitis virus replicates in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes, which do not express MHC class II. Which cell can directly recognize and kill an infected hepatocyte by identifying a viral peptide?

D. CD8⁺ T cell. Proteins made inside the cell are shown on MHC I (all nucleated cells) to CD8⁺ T cells. CD4 subsets (A, C, E) need MHC II, which hepatocytes lack; B cells recognize native antigen and do not kill infected cells. NK cells kill cells that lose MHC I, not by recognizing a specific peptide.

6Cell-free plasma from a healthy volunteer who was never exposed to a laboratory strain of E. coli is mixed with the bacteria, and many bacteria lyse within an hour. Heating the plasma to 56 °C abolishes this effect. Which component is responsible?

A. Complement. A heat-labile, soluble (humoral), innate protein cascade that lyses bacteria without prior exposure. Lysozyme mainly hits gram-positive peptidoglycan; NK cells are cells (absent here); no memory exists without exposure; CRP is an opsonin that activates complement but does not itself lyse.

7A 30-year-old returns from a year in rural Southeast Asia with abdominal pain. Stool shows hookworm eggs; blood shows marked eosinophilia and serum IgE is high. Which CD4⁺ T-cell subset drove this response?

C. TH2. Helminths → TH2 (IL-4 → IgE class switch; IL-5 → eosinophils). TH1 targets intracellular vesicle microbes; TH17 recruits neutrophils vs Candida/extracellular bacteria; Tregs suppress; CD8 is not CD4 and kills infected cells.

8A patient with diphtheria receives equine antitoxin and improves. Nine days later she develops fever, an urticarial rash, joint pain and proteinuria, with low serum C3. What is the mechanism?

E. Immune complexes. Serum sickness (type III): she makes antibody against foreign horse serum proteins → complexes deposit in vessels, joints, glomeruli → complement consumed (low C3). Type I (A) is immediate; type II (B) targets cell surfaces; type IV (C) is T-cell mediated; the antitoxin neutralized the toxin.

9An enzyme in tears and sweat cleaves the β-1,4 bond in the bacterial cell wall polymer. Bacteria of which organism are most susceptible?

B. S. aureus. Lysozyme (a chemical barrier) breaks down peptidoglycan; gram-positives have a thick, exposed layer. Gram-negatives (A, C, D) protect their thin peptidoglycan with an outer membrane; Mycoplasma has no cell wall at all.

10A nurse receives a second dose of hepatitis B vaccine. Compared with the first dose, her antibody rises sooner, reaches higher titers and binds antigen more tightly. Which explains this best?

D. Memory B cells. Memory is adaptive: after affinity maturation and class switching in germinal centers, memory B cells respond faster with high-affinity IgG. Innate cells (A, C) and complement (B) have no antigen-specific memory; maternal IgG disappears within months.

11A biopsy of an enlarged cervical lymph node shows cortical nodules with pale centers containing large proliferating lymphocytes, follicular dendritic cells and tingible-body macrophages. What process is occurring in these pale centers?

B. Affinity maturation. These are germinal centers (secondary follicles): activated B cells mutate their variable regions and the highest-affinity clones are selected. Positive selection is in the thymic cortex; HEV entry and DC priming of T cells occur in the paracortex; RBC removal is in splenic red pulp.

12A 3-year-old boy has recurrent sinopulmonary infections and Pneumocystis pneumonia. Serum IgM is elevated, while IgG, IgA and IgE are nearly absent. B-cell numbers are normal. Which molecule is most likely defective?

D. CD40L. TFH help to B cells (needed for class switching) requires CD40L–CD40; without it, B cells stay stuck making IgM (X-linked hyper-IgM). RAG defects remove B cells; MHC I loss affects CD8; CCR7 loss blocks DC migration; CD16 mediates ADCC.

13A construction worker's leg is crushed under a beam. There is no break in the skin, yet the leg becomes red, hot and swollen within hours and his serum CRP rises. What initiated this inflammation?

A. DAMPs. Damaged cells release DAMPs (e.g., mitochondrial DNA, heat-shock proteins), read by the same PRR families as MAMPs → cytokines → sterile inflammation and acute phase proteins. No microbes means no LPS (B); adaptive T/IgE responses (C, D, E) are not involved within hours of a first injury.
