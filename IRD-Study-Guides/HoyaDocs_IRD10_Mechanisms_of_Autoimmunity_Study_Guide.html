Mechanisms of Autoimmunity

Learning objectives

  1. LO 1Explain which hypersensitivity types (Gell & Coombs II, III and IV) actually do the damage in autoimmune disease, and walk through the stacked safety nets of self-tolerance that normally keep self-reactive lymphocytes quiet.
  2. LO 2Explain what it means for a tissue to be "immune privileged" (its antigens sequestered), which sites qualify, how they stay protected, and what happens when that protection is breached.
  3. LO 3List and explain the leading theories for how an autoimmune response gets started once tolerance slips.
  4. LO 4Explain how infections and innate pattern-recognition receptors (PAMPs, DAMPs, Toll-like receptors) can tip self-reactive lymphocytes into autoimmune disease, using strep, EBV, enterovirus and viral myocarditis as examples.
  5. LO 5Explain how autoantibodies arise and how to use them to diagnose SLE, Sjögren syndrome, autoimmune thyroid disease, celiac disease and juvenile arthritis, including what sensitivity vs specificity means for each test.
  6. LO 6Explain the inherited side of autoimmunity, especially which HLA alleles raise the risk of which diseases and how strongly.

LO 1Tissue injury and the layers of self-toleranceHow autoimmune damage happens, and the safety nets that usually stop it

By the end: you can say which Gell & Coombs types cause autoimmune injury (and why type I does not), fill in the six-layer tolerance table from memory, explain signal 1 vs signal 2, and name the single-gene defect that breaks each layer.
Q1. What is autoimmunity, and why does it happen at all?
  • Autoimmunity = the adaptive immune system attacks self because self-tolerance has failed → an "escape from self-tolerance."
  • Two groups of factors converge:
    • Genetic factors → HLA alleles (decide which self-peptides get shown to T cells) and the TCR repertoire (a skewed or narrowed set of T-cell receptors makes self-attack likelier).
    • Infection and environmental exposure → supply danger signals and release self-antigen.
  • Genes + environment → disordered immune regulation → autoimmunity.
  • Autoimmune disease disproportionately affects females (biological factors including estrogen).
CheckIdentical twins share all their HLA genes, yet one twin can develop SLE years before (or without) the other. What does that tell you?
Genes set the risk, but an environmental trigger (infection, UV, drugs, hormones) is also needed. Concordance in identical twins is well under 100%.
Q2. Which Gell & Coombs types do the damage in autoimmune disease?
  • Autoimmune tissue injury is mediated by types II, III and IV. Type I (IgE) is allergy to harmless environmental antigen, not an autoimmune mechanism.
  • Most autoimmune diseases mix several types (SLE uses all three: II, III and IV).

Sensitized T lymphocytes and macrophages → type IV (delayed-type) contact hypersensitivity, e.g., nickel. The 24–72 h delay = T-cell recruitment; antibody types act in minutes to hours.
Step 1 trap

"Which hypersensitivity type causes autoimmune disease?" is never type I. And don't assume one disease = one type: SLE is II (anti-platelet) + III (immune complexes, predominant) + IV.

Memory tool

ACID: Anaphylactic (I) · Cytotoxic / antibody (II) · Immune complex (III) · Delayed T cell (IV). Autoimmunity lives in C-I-D.

Q3. What are the six layers of self-tolerance?

Think of them as successive safety nets: a self-reactive clone has to slip through every one before disease appears.

LayerMechanismSite of action

Peripheral anergyCellular inactivation by weak signaling without co-stimulusSecondary lymphoid tissue

CheckWhich two layers act in the primary lymphoid organs, and which act in secondary lymphoid tissue or inflamed sites?
Only central tolerance acts in primary organs (thymus, marrow). Anergy, Tregs, cytokine deviation and clonal exhaustion act in secondary lymphoid tissue (the last three also at sites of inflammation). Segregation works at barriers (BBB, peripheral organs).
Q4. How does central tolerance work, and what does AIRE add?
  • Central tolerance = negative selection: developing lymphocytes that bind self strongly are deleted (apoptosis) before they leave.
    • T cells → thymus. B cells → bone marrow; B cells can also rescue themselves by receptor editing (new light-chain rearrangement).
  • AIRE (autoimmune regulator) lets thymic medullary epithelial cells express tissue-restricted self-antigens (insulin, thyroid, adrenal, parathyroid proteins…).
    • So thymocytes can "see" organ-specific proteins → self-reactive T cells are deleted (or become natural Tregs).
    • AIRE mutation → organ-specific antigens never shown → self-reactive T cells mature and escape to the periphery.
  • AIRE works on T cells in the thymus only, not on B cells in the marrow.
CheckA thymocyte specific for an insulin peptide meets insulin on a medullary epithelial cell. What happens, and what if AIRE were absent?
It binds strongly → deleted (or diverted to a Treg). Without AIRE, insulin is never expressed in the thymus → the clone survives and can attack β cells.
Step 1 HY — AIRE

AIRE deficiency = autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 1 (APS-1, autosomal recessive). "Without AIRE your body will CHAR": chronic mucocutaneous Candidiasis, Hypoparathyroidism, Adrenal insufficiency (Addison); the R repeats the hallmark, Recurrent Candida.

Step 1 trap

Self-reactive B cells are not removed "by AIRE." B-cell central tolerance (deletion, receptor editing) happens in the marrow without AIRE. Low-affinity self-reactive B and T cells escape central deletion simply because their binding is too weak to trigger it.

Q5. What is peripheral anergy? (signal 1 vs signal 2)
  • A naive T cell needs signal 1 (TCR binds peptide–MHC) plus signal 2 (B7 = CD80/86 on the APC binds CD28 on the T cell).
  • Peripheral anergy = signal 1 without co-stimulus → the lymphocyte is functionally inactivated, in secondary lymphoid tissue.
  • Resting APCs presenting self-peptide express little B7 → self-reactive T cells become unresponsive.
  • Danger: if infection up-regulates B7 on that same APC, the same self-reactive T cell can be activated instead.
CheckA naive CD4⁺ T cell binds self-peptide–MHC II on a resting dendritic cell that has almost no B7. Outcome?
Anergy: signal 1 without B7–CD28 inactivates the cell. If an infection had up-regulated B7 on that DC, the same cell could be activated → autoimmunity.
Q6. How do regulatory T cells keep self-reactive cells in check?
  • Tregs (CD4⁺ CD25⁺ FOXP3⁺) suppress by:
    • Inhibitory cytokines → IL-10 and TGF-β.
    • Contact signals → CTLA-4 on the Treg binds B7 on the APC (higher affinity than CD28) → B7 is not available for CD28 → responder T cells are not co-stimulated.
  • Where: secondary lymphoid tissue and sites of inflammation.
  • Two origins:
    • Natural (thymic) Treg → a T cell that recognizes self-antigen in the thymus differentiates into a Treg.
    • Induced (peripheral) Treg → a T cell that recognizes self or commensal microbiota antigen in the periphery in the presence of TGF-β (e.g., from an unlicensed DC = not activated by infection).
  • Regulatory ("infectious") tolerance: a Treg can suppress self-reactive lymphocytes that recognize different peptides from its own, provided the antigens come from the same tissue or are presented by the same APC.
    • → one Treg specificity can protect a whole organ from many autoreactive clones; suppression "spreads" locally.
CheckA Treg specific for a thyroglobulin peptide sits on the same dendritic cell as an effector T cell specific for a thyroid peroxidase peptide. Will the Treg suppress it?
Yes → regulatory (infectious) tolerance. Suppression works across different peptides on the same APC / same tissue (IL-10, TGF-β, CTLA-4).
Step 1 HY — FOXP3 and CTLA-4
  • FOXP3 = master transcription factor of Tregs. Loss → IPEX: Immune dysregulation, Polyendocrinopathy (neonatal type 1 DM, thyroiditis), Enteropathy (watery diarrhea), X-linked → male infants, often with eczematous dermatitis.
  • CTLA-4 haploinsufficiency → autoimmune cytopenias, enteropathy, lymphoproliferation. CTLA-4 polymorphisms add risk for Graves and type 1 DM.
  • Drugs: abatacept (CTLA-4–Ig) blocks B7 → treats RA. Ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4) releases the brake → immune-related adverse events (colitis, hypophysitis, thyroiditis).
Memory tool

CTLA-4 "steals" B7; CD28 "starts" the T cell. Treg = "25 FOXy regulators" → CD25⁺ FOXP3⁺.

Q7. What are cytokine deviation and clonal exhaustion?

Cytokine deviation

  • APCs vary the cytokines they secrete → self-reactive CD4⁺ T cells are pushed to TH2 instead of TH1.
  • TH1 (IFN-γ) drives macrophage-mediated tissue damage; TH2 is comparatively less inflammatory for most autoimmune lesions.
  • → less inflammatory cytokine, more cytokines that dampen inflammation.

Clonal exhaustion

  • Apoptosis after activation → activated clones are deleted even if antigen persists.
  • Mainly at sites of inflammation, also in secondary lymphoid organs.
  • Only memory cells remain. One route: Fas–FasL activation-induced cell death.
CheckWhy would a defect in Fas cause both lymphadenopathy and autoimmune cytopenias?
Activated lymphocytes cannot be removed (failed clonal exhaustion) → they accumulate (lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly) and self-reactive clones persist → autoantibodies against blood cells.
Step 1 HY

Autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome (ALPS) = Fas (or FasL) defect → failed activation-induced apoptosis → lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, autoimmune cytopenias, ↑ CD4⁻CD8⁻ (double-negative) T cells.

Step 1 trap

Cytokine deviation moves cells toward TH2, which is protective for most organ-specific autoimmunity, but TH2 is not "harmless" overall: it drives allergy (type I) and chronic asthma (type IV-TH2).

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1An 8-year-old has recurrent oral thrush since infancy, tetany from low calcium, and now hyperpigmentation with hypotension. Which tolerance layer failed?
Central tolerance (AIRE mutation → APS-1). Tissue-restricted antigens were never shown in the thymus, so parathyroid- and adrenal-reactive T cells escaped.
A2A 4-month-old boy has intractable watery diarrhea, eczematous rash and insulin-requiring diabetes. Which cell population is missing its master regulator?
Regulatory T cells (FOXP3 → IPEX). X-linked, so boys; enteropathy + endocrinopathy + dermatitis.
A3Predict: a drug that blocks CTLA-4 is given to a cancer patient. What happens to B7 availability, and what side effects appear?
More B7 is free to bind CD28 → stronger co-stimulation of T cells (including self-reactive ones) → immune-related colitis, hepatitis, hypophysitis, thyroiditis.
A4Autoimmune thyroiditis shows a lymphocytic infiltrate destroying follicles plus anti-thyroglobulin antibodies. Which Gell & Coombs types are involved, and which type is never part of it?
Types II and IV (antibody + T-cell destruction). Type I (IgE) is not an autoimmune injury mechanism.

LO 2Immune-privileged (sequestered) sitesWhere the immune system is kept out, and why breaching it is dangerous

By the end: you can name the four privileged sites, list the four mechanisms that protect them, explain why privilege is not absolute, and recognize sympathetic ophthalmia.
Q1. Which sites are immunologically privileged, and why have privilege at all?
  • Four classic sites: brain, eye, testis, uterus (fetus).
  • Purpose: protect tissues whose function would be wrecked by inflammation → vision, non-dividing neurons, germ cells, the semi-foreign fetus.
  • This is the "antigen segregation" layer of tolerance in action; it also applies partly to peripheral organs such as thyroid and pancreas.
CheckWhy does the fetus need privilege even though it is "self" to the mother?
It expresses paternal HLA antigens that are foreign to the mother; without privilege it would be rejected like a graft.
Memory tool

"BEaT the Uterus" → Brain, Eye, Testis, Uterus (fetus).

Q2. How do privileged sites keep lymphocytes away?
  • No lymph drainage → antigen rarely reaches the draining node → naive cells are never primed.
  • Barriers (blood–brain, blood–retina, blood–testis) → exclude naive lymphocytes.
  • Fas ligand (FasL, CD95L) on resident cells → binds Fas (CD95) on entering lymphocytes → caspase-mediated apoptosis.
  • TGF-β (anti-inflammatory) accompanies any antigen that leaves.
  • Mechanisms 3 + 4 are active suppression, not just hiding.
CheckNaive lymphocytes entering the anterior chamber of the eye quickly die. Which molecule on ocular cells is responsible?
Fas ligand → binds Fas on the lymphocyte → apoptosis (the same death-receptor pathway CTLs use).
Q3. Is privilege absolute? What happens when it breaks?
  • No. Two consequences:
    • Privilege can be broken (trauma, inflammation) → sequestered self-antigen reaches lymph nodes → autoimmunity.
    • Local suppression can impair defense against infection inside the site.
  • Release of sequestered antigen (a mechanism of autoimmune induction, LO 3): hidden antigen enters circulation/lymph after trauma, massive cell death or inflammation → lymphocytes that were never tolerized (antigen never reached the thymus or periphery) respond.

Step 1 HY
  • Sympathetic ophthalmia: weeks after trauma to one eye → granulomatous uveitis in both eyes. Treat with corticosteroids/immunosuppression; early removal of a blind injured eye prevents it.
  • Other "hidden antigen" examples: anti-sperm antibodies after vasectomy or testicular trauma.
Step 1 trap

Sympathetic ophthalmia is not molecular mimicry (no microbe) and not infection spreading to the other eye; it is release of sequestered self-antigen with T-cell (type IV) injury.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1Six weeks after a penetrating injury to his right eye, a man develops pain and blurred vision in his uninjured left eye. Mechanism?
Release of sequestered antigen. Trauma breaks privilege → intraocular proteins prime T cells → they attack both eyes (sympathetic ophthalmia).
A2Predict: a mouse is engineered so its corneal cells lack Fas ligand. What happens to corneal grafts placed in it?
Higher rejection. Entering Fas⁺ lymphocytes are no longer killed, so active suppression is lost.
A3A viral infection inside the brain is cleared much more slowly than the same virus in skin. Which feature of privilege explains this?
Local immune suppression (TGF-β, FasL, barriers) also blunts protective responses; privilege is a trade-off.

Q2. What is molecular mimicry?
  • Antigenic cross-reactivity: a microbial antigen is structurally similar to a self-antigen, so antibodies or T cells raised against the microbe also attack host tissue.
  • The response is appropriate for the microbe; the self-damage is collateral.
  • Classic: post-group A strep → rheumatic fever (cardiac myosin) and Sydenham chorea (neuronal dopamine receptors). Details + diagram in LO 4.
  • Celiac disease is proposed as possible mimicry: anti-gluten responses spread to self tissue transglutaminase (LO 5).
CheckIn mimicry, is the immune response "wrong"?
No. It is a correct anti-microbe response; the self-attack happens because a host protein looks alike.
Step 1 HY

Guillain-Barré syndrome after Campylobacter jejuni: lipo-oligosaccharide mimics GM1 ganglioside on peripheral nerves → ascending demyelinating paralysis. A boards favorite for "molecular mimicry."

Step 1 trap

Post-strep glomerulonephritis is an immune-complex (type III) disease, not a mimicry example. Mimicry after strep = rheumatic fever and Sydenham chorea.

Q3. What is epitope spreading, and how does one T cell help many B cells?
  • Epitope spreading: an autoimmune response that starts against one epitope broadens over time to other epitopes on the same or physically linked self-antigens.
  • Mechanism = linked recognition ("B cells pitch in"):
    • A B cell binds part of a particle (e.g., DNA in a nucleosome) with its BCR → internalizes the whole particle → presents peptides from all its proteins (e.g., histone H1) on MHC II.
    • → one H1-specific helper T cell can help B cells of many specificities.
CheckA patient with SLE first has only anti-histone H1; a year later she also has anti-dsDNA. How?
Epitope spreading: the H1-specific helper T cell helps DNA-specific B cells that took up nucleosomes and present H1 peptide. Only one T-cell specificity is needed.
Q4. How do low-affinity self-reactive lymphocytes get switched on? (chronic infection / inflammation)
  • Lymphocytes that bind self with low affinity escape central deletion and usually ignore self. They become activated when:
    1. The self-peptide is presented by an APC with high co-stimulatory molecules because of concomitant infection / pro-inflammatory cytokines.
    2. Massive cell death or inflammation releases sequestered self-antigen in large amounts.

Q5. Receptor antibodies, aberrant class II MHC, and polyclonal activators: how does each work?

Type II vs receptor

  • IgG (or IgM) against a receptor without destroying the cell.
  • Stimulate → overproduction: Graves (anti-TSH receptor).
  • Block / remove → loss of function: myasthenia gravis (anti-ACh receptor → internalization, degradation → weakness).
  • Drug as target: drug binds a cell (hapten) → antibody → cell destroyed (e.g., penicillin hemolysis).

Inappropriate class II MHC

  • Normally class II–negative tissue cells express MHC II → present self-peptide to CD4⁺ T cells.
  • Seen on pancreatic β cells (type 1 DM) and thyroid epithelium (Graves).
  • Induced by IFN-γ and mitogens → the tissue cell becomes its own APC.

Polyclonal activation

  • Mitogens activate many B/T clones regardless of specificity → autoreactive (mostly IgM) antibody.
  • B-cell mitogens: EBV, CMV, LPS (gram-negative).
  • Superantigens cross-link MHC II with the TCR Vβ outside the peptide groove → huge fraction of CD4⁺ T cells fire.
CheckGraves and myasthenia gravis are both type II. Which one kills the target cell?
Neither destroys cells as its main effect: both alter receptor signaling. Graves stimulates; MG blocks and causes receptor internalization (and some complement damage at the end plate).
Step 1 HY — superantigens

S. aureus TSST-1 (toxic shock syndrome) and S. pyogenes erythrogenic exotoxin A → bind MHC II + TCR Vβ outside the groove → polyclonal T-cell activation → IL-1, IL-2, IFN-γ, TNF storm → fever, rash, shock.

Step 1 trap

Superantigens bind outside the peptide groove and are not processed. A question saying "processed and presented in the groove" describes a conventional antigen, not a superantigen.

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1Two weeks after Campylobacter diarrhea, a man has ascending leg weakness and areflexia. Mechanism?
Molecular mimicry: bacterial lipo-oligosaccharide resembles GM1 ganglioside → antibodies attack peripheral nerve (Guillain-Barré).
A2A thyroid biopsy shows follicular cells strongly expressing HLA-DR in a patient with Graves disease. What induces this, and why does it matter?
IFN-γ (and mitogens) induce inappropriate class II MHC → thyrocytes present self-peptide directly to CD4⁺ T cells, sustaining the response.
A3A patient with toxic shock has a huge rise in IL-2 and IFN-γ within hours. Why are so many T cells activated at once?
Superantigen (TSST-1) links MHC II to TCR Vβ regardless of peptide → polyclonal activation of up to ~20% of T cells.
A4A woman with myasthenia gravis has reduced end-plate ACh receptors. Gell & Coombs type and effect on the receptor?
Type II, receptor-blocking: antibody blocks AChR and causes its internalization/degradation → fatigable weakness.

LO 4Infection and pattern-recognition receptorsHow microbes and danger signals tip the balance

Q1. What do pattern-recognition receptors detect?
  • PRRs (e.g., Toll-like receptors) = innate, "primitive" receptors that act before adaptive immunity.
  • PAMPs = pathogen-associated molecular patterns → on microbes (LPS, flagellin, viral RNA, CpG DNA).
  • DAMPs = damage-associated molecular patterns → released by cell damage or death (self DNA/RNA, HMGB1, ATP, uric acid).
  • DAMPs explain how sterile tissue injury supplies danger signals that activate self-reactive cells.

Q3. How does group A strep cause rheumatic fever and Sydenham chorea?
  • Anti-strep antibody (e.g., against M protein) binds look-alike host proteins:
    • Cardiac myosin → rheumatic fever (carditis, mitral regurgitation, heart block).
    • Neuronal dopamine receptors (basal ganglia) → Sydenham chorea (involuntary, jerky movements).
  • ASO (anti-streptolysin O) and anti-DNase B document the preceding strep infection.
CheckA 6-year-old had a sore throat weeks ago; now a ring-shaped trunk rash, migrating joint pain, a new mitral murmur and first-degree heart block. Diagnosis, mechanism, confirmatory titers?
Acute rheumatic fever → molecular mimicry (strep antigens vs cardiac myosin). Confirm the strep infection with ASO and anti-DNase B. Mitral valve is most often involved; PR prolongation reflects conduction involvement.
Step 1 HY — rheumatic fever
  • Classified as type II hypersensitivity. Follows pharyngitis only (not skin infection).
  • Major criteria J♥NES: Joints (migratory polyarthritis), ♥ carditis, Nodules (subcutaneous), Erythema marginatum, Sydenham chorea.
  • Histology: Aschoff bodies, Anitschkow (caterpillar) cells. Treat/prevent with penicillin.
Step 1 trap

Strep skin infection (impetigo) can cause post-strep glomerulonephritis (type III) but not rheumatic fever. Rheumatic fever = pharyngitis → mimicry.

"ring-shaped, non-itchy trunk rash" → erythema marginatum"ASO, anti-DNase B" → recent group A strep"jerky involuntary movements after sore throat" → Sydenham chorea
Q4. Which viruses are linked to autoimmunity: EBV, enterovirus, and COVID-19?

EBV B-cell latency

EBV persists latently in B lymphocytes for life. (Other herpesviruses hide elsewhere: HSV-1 in sensory ganglia, HHV-6 in T cells/monocytes.)

Linked to multiple sclerosis, RA, Sjögren, thyroiditis, autoimmune hepatitis.

Latent virus drives cross-reactive antibody (e.g., against myelin in MS) + polyclonal B-cell activation. MS = CNS demyelination → numbness, weakness, visual problems, poor coordination, fatigue.

Enterovirus → type 1 DM type IV

Enteroviral infection is a proposed trigger (neighbor children with febrile GI illness → T1DM within a year).

Anti-β-cell (islet) antibodies appear months to years before (at least 4–6 months) insulin secretion fails: GAD65, IA-2, insulin.

β-cell killing itself = CD8⁺ T cells (type IV); antibodies are markers.

Virus / vaccine → myocarditis

Antibody made against portions of a virus can cross-react with myocardial tissue (mimicry).

~1/100 COVID infections may cause severe myocarditis vs ~1/10,000 mild myocarditis with vaccination.

→ the infection carries far more myocarditis risk than the vaccine.

By the end: you can use SnOut/SpIn to pick screening vs confirmatory tests, give the frequency of ANA, anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm in SLE, explain neonatal lupus and congenital heart block, contrast Graves vs Hashimoto, name the celiac antibodies and recognize the two JIA cases.
Q1. Sensitive vs specific tests: what do SnOut and SpIn mean?

Sensitive test → SnOut

  • Sensitivity = % of people with disease who test positive.
  • Highly sensitive test, when negative, rules disease out.
  • Best for screening. Example: ANA (95–98% of SLE).

Specific test → SpIn

  • Specificity = % of people without disease who test negative.
  • Highly specific test, when positive, rules disease in.
  • Best to confirm a positive screen. Often insensitive → a negative does not exclude disease (anti-dsDNA only ~60%).
  • May relate to risk and prognosis (anti-dsDNA → renal, CNS).
CheckANA negative in a woman with joint pain and fatigue. How confident are you that she does not have SLE?
Very confident → ANA is ~95–98% sensitive, so a negative result essentially rules SLE out (SnOut).
Step 1 trap

Sensitivity is not positive predictive value. Sensitivity/specificity are properties of the test; PPV/NPV also depend on prevalence. ANA has a low PPV in the general population because 5–15% of healthy people are ANA⁺.

Memory tool

SnOut: Sensitive, Negative, rules Out. SpIn: Specific, Positive, rules In. ANA = the net (catches everyone); dsDNA/Sm = the fingerprint.

Q4. Sjögren syndrome, the placenta, and neonatal lupus
  • Sjögren syndrome → autoimmune attack on lacrimal and salivary glands → dry eyes (keratoconjunctivitis sicca), dry mouth (xerostomia) → trouble eating dry food, dental caries.
    • Autoantibodies: anti-SSA/Ro and anti-SSB/La (ribonucleoproteins).
  • Pregnancy is an immune "intersection":
    • IgG crosses readily during the last 3 months; the baby keeps maternal IgG for ~6 weeks to 3 months.
    • Good: maternal immunization (tetanus, hepatitis B) protects the newborn.
    • IgM does not cross; antibody the baby makes itself is IgM.
    • Bad: maternal IgG autoantibodies also cross.
  • Neonatal lupus (maternal anti-Ro ± anti-La IgG):
    • Fetal cardiac (conduction) tissue expresses the Ro antigen; the adult heart does not express this epitope → congenital heart block in the fetus but not the mother.
    • Inflammation + fibrosis of conducting tissue → heart block (may need a pacemaker); fetal heart failure possible. All at-risk pregnancies are now screened for anti-Ro.
    • Annular, photosensitive facial/periorbital rash → resolves as maternal IgG clears; heart block is permanent.

Q7. Juvenile idiopathic arthritis: two cases to recognize

Oligoarticular JIA, ANA⁺ eye risk

5-year-old · stiff, swollen left knee · Lyme negative · ANA 1:1280 speckled · iridocyclitis · joint fluid ~2,000 cells/µL, mostly lymphocytes, culture negative.

Up to 30% develop uveitis without symptoms → routine slit-lamp ophthalmology screening prevents vision loss (keratopathy, synechiae).

Treat: NSAID (naproxen with food) → add weekly oral methotrexate.

RF⁺ polyarticular JIA adult RA twin

10-year-old girl · 3 h morning stiffness · symmetric swelling of wrists, MCPs, PIPs · ANA negative · ESR 40 · RF⁺, anti-CCP⁺.

= childhood counterpart of adult rheumatoid arthritis; anti-CCP is the more specific marker. Untreated → joint destruction and hand deformity.

CheckWhy must a well-appearing child with ANA⁺ oligoarticular JIA see an ophthalmologist even without eye complaints?
Chronic anterior uveitis is often asymptomatic (up to 30%) → silent damage; regular slit-lamp exams catch it.
Step 1 HY

RF = IgM against the Fc of IgG; anti-CCP is more specific for RA. RA: HLA-DR4; MCP/PIP involvement with DIP sparing; boutonnière and swan-neck deformities.

Step 1 trap

In children with monoarthritis of the knee, exclude Lyme and septic arthritis first (culture-negative, ~2,000 cells/µL here is inflammatory, not septic, which is usually >50,000).

Q8. Eczema: a barrier disease that looks like autoimmunity
  • Atopic dermatitis: genetically permeable skin → cannot retain moisture or exclude irritants, allergens, microbes → cytokines and immune cells penetrate, especially when scratched.
  • "The itch that rashes": itch → scratch → barrier damage → inflammation → more itch.
  • Chronic itchy rash in flexural areas in children.
CheckWhy does scratching make eczema worse?
It breaks an already leaky barrier → more allergen and microbial entry → more cytokine-driven inflammation → more itch.
Step 1 HY

Barrier gene: filaggrin loss-of-function. Part of the atopic triad with asthma and allergic rhinitis (type I / TH2 biology).

Q3. What else does genetics tell us? (mice, twins, polygenic traits)
  • NZB × NZW F1 mice spontaneously develop lupus-like disease (DNA autoantibodies, immune-complex glomerulonephritis, hemolytic anemia) → genetic basis; cyclophosphamide suppresses proteinuria and DNA autoantibodies. Combining two strains' genes produces disease neither parent has in full → polygenic.
  • Identical twins → shared genes ↑ risk, but disease timing differs → environment matters.
  • Polygenic traits in the autoimmune phenotype → four categories:
    1. Immune response genes → HLA class II (HLA-DQ, DR).
    2. Production of antibodies to self (celiac, thyroid, pancreatic islet cells).
    3. Inflammatory response to synovium, eye, skin.
    4. Binding of IgG and immune complexes, attracting TH lymphocytes and driving cytokines (IL-1, TNF, IL-6).
  • Non-HLA single genes break specific tolerance layers (LO 1): AIRE, FOXP3, CTLA-4, Fas.
CheckWhich therapeutic targets come straight out of polygenic category 4?
The cytokines TNF, IL-6, IL-1 → anti-TNF (infliximab, adalimumab, etanercept), anti-IL-6R (tocilizumab), IL-1 blockade (anakinra).
