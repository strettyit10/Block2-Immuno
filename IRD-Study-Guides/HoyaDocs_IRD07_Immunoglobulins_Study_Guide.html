MAPBig picture

  • Antibody = glycoprotein made by plasma cells → main weapon of humoral (B cell) immunity
    • membrane-bound = BCR · secreted = soluble antibody · same specificity
  • Get it two ways → active (make it: slow, lasting, memory) · passive (borrow it: instant, weeks, no memory)
  • Y shape → 2 heavy + 2 light chains · S–S bonds
    • Fab tips (CDRs) → bind antigen · Fc stem → C1q + Fc receptors
    • Foreign Ig can itself be an antigen → anti-iso / allo / idiotypic responses
  • Variation → isotype (class, Fc) · allotype (person) · idiotype (clone, V region)
  • Binding → affinity (one site) · valency (number of sites) · avidity (all sites together)
  • Five classes → serum G > A > M > D > E
    • M first + complement · G memory + placenta · A mucosa · E allergy + worms · D naïve B cell

LO 1Active vs passive immunityMake your own antibody, or borrow someone else's

By the end: you can classify any exposure, vaccine or antibody product as natural/artificial and active/passive, predict how fast it protects and how long, and explain why we often give both together.
Q1. First: what is an antibody, and who makes it?
  • Antibodies = immunoglobulins (Ig): glycoproteins that specifically recognize and bind foreign substances (antigens).
  • Made by plasma cells = fully differentiated, antibody-secreting B cells.
  • Exist in two forms with the same specificity:
    • Membrane-bound on the B cell surface = the B cell receptor (BCR)
    • Secreted, soluble antibody in blood and secretions
  • Job: neutralize and eliminate foreign antigen → antibodies are the main mediators of humoral immunity (the B cell + antibody arm of adaptive immunity).
CheckA B cell's surface receptor and the antibody its plasma-cell descendants secrete: same or different antigen specificity?
Same. The secreted antibody is the soluble version of that clone's BCR.
Q2. What separates active from passive immunity?

The one question to ask: whose B cells made the antibody?

FeatureActive immunityPassive immunity
Source of antibodyYou make your own after exposure to antigenPreformed antibody from another person or animal
OnsetSlow (several weeks)Immediate
DurationLong-lasting (sometimes lifelong)Weeks to months only
Memory?Yes (memory B and T cells)No: your own B cells were never activated
Natural exampleInfection with the real organismMaternal IgG across the placenta; IgA in breast milk
Artificial exampleVaccination (killed, weakened/attenuated or component organism)IVIG, antisera / antitoxin / antivenom, therapeutic monoclonal antibody
  • Two forms of active immunity: natural (infection) and vaccine-induced. Both need the host's own B (and T) cells → slow but durable.
  • Transferred IgG has a half-life of ~3 weeks → passive protection fades over weeks to a few months.
CheckClassify: (a) measles infection, (b) MMR vaccine, (c) colostrum, (d) antivenom.
(a) natural active, (b) artificial active, (c) natural passive (secretory IgA), (d) artificial passive.
Memory tool

Active = you went to the gym: slow to build, but the muscle (memory) stays. Passive = you borrowed a friend's muscles: instant strength, gone when they leave.

Step 1 trap

"Natural" ≠ "active." Maternal antibody (placental IgG, breast-milk IgA) is natural passive. And a vaccine is artificial active, not passive, even though a clinician gave it.

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A man bitten by a rabid bat gets rabies immune globulin into the wound plus a rabies vaccine series. Which part protects him today and which protects him next year?
Immune globulin = passive, immediate, short-lived; vaccine = active, slower (weeks), with memory. The borrowed antibody covers the lag before his own response arrives.
A2A child with antibody deficiency receives monthly IVIG. Why monthly, and will she develop memory to the pathogens covered?
Monthly because IgG has a half-life of ~3 weeks; no memory, because her own B cells were never activated. Passive protection lasts only as long as the donor antibody.
A3Predict: a vaccine is given at the same injection site as a large dose of immune globulin against the same pathogen. What might go wrong?
The injected antibody can bind and neutralize the vaccine antigen locally → weaker active response. That is why the two are given at different sites.

Q2. What does the basic antibody (IgG) look like?
  • 4 chains: 2 identical heavy (H) + 2 identical light (L) → Y shape.
  • Disulfide bonds link H–H (at the hinge) and each H–L pair.
  • Each chain = an N-terminal variable (V) domain + one or more constant (C) domains.
    • Light chain: VL + CL (light chains come as κ or λ).
    • Heavy chain (IgG): VH + CH1 → hinge → CH2 → CH3.
    • V domains = highly variable → antigen-binding sites. C domains = conserved.
  • Two identical antigen-binding sites → bivalent.
CheckWhich chain contributes to both the Fab and the Fc, and which contributes only to the Fab?
Heavy chain → both (VH+CH1 in Fab; CH2+CH3 in Fc). Light chain → Fab only.
Q3. Fab, hinge, Fc: which part talks to whom?
RegionMade ofInteracts withDetermines
Fab ("arms")VH + CH1 + whole light chain (VL, CL)Antigen (epitope) via the paratopeSpecificity → idiotype
HingeHeavy chain between CH1 and CH2; proline-rich; holds H–H disulfides(none directly)Flexibility → both arms can grab epitopes at different spacings
Fc ("stem")Heavy-chain constant domains only (CH2, CH3); carries oligosaccharide side chainsFc receptors (macrophages, neutrophils, NK cells, mast cells…) and complement C1q (esp. IgG, IgM)Isotype → effector function (opsonization, complement, cell lysis)
  • The paratope = hypervariable loops = complementarity-determining regions (CDRs): 3 per chain → 6 per binding site, at the tip of each Fab.
    • Framework regions of the V domain = the scaffold holding the CDRs.
  • C1q binds CH2 near the hinge; Fc receptors bind the C-terminal Fc (CH2–CH3).
  • The heavy-chain constant region determines class → class switching swaps it → new effector function, same antigen binding.
  • IgM and IgE have no hinge: an extra domain (CH4) instead. IgG, IgA and IgD have a hinge.
  • "Trifunctional" antibody: ① antigen binding (Fab tips) ② Fc-receptor binding ③ complement (C1q) activation.
CheckA mutation in CH2 of a patient's IgG abolishes C1q binding. What is lost, what is kept?
Lost: classical complement activation by that IgG. Kept: antigen binding and neutralization (Fab untouched). Fc-receptor binding may or may not suffer depending on the exact site.
Memory tool

Fab = "fragment, antigen binding" → it fabricates the fit.
Fc = the 5 C's: Constant, Carboxy-terminal, Complement-binding, Carbohydrate side chains, Confers isotype.

Step 1 trap

Changing class (IgM → IgG) changes the Fc, not the antigen specificity. If a stem asks what a switched antibody keeps, the answer is the V region / idiotype. Also: antibodies do not bind pathogen lectins; their Fc functions are complement and Fc receptors.

  • Therapeutic Fab fragments: digoxin immune Fab (digoxin toxicity) and crotalid Fab antivenom (rattlesnake bite) → small, renally cleared, less immunogenic.

Q5. How did urinary light chains reveal where antibody variability lives?
  • Plasma cells make light chains in excess of heavy chains → free light chains spill into urine.
  • In multiple myeloma, one malignant plasma-cell clone pours out a single monoclonal κ or λ chain (the Bence Jones protein).
  • Each patient's protein is a pure sample of one antibody → comparing sequences showed variability is concentrated in 3 hypervariable regions (CDRs) separated by framework regions.
Q6. Monoclonal vs polyclonal: what is the difference?

Monoclonal

  • From a single plasma-cell clone
  • One specificity → recognizes one epitope
  • Most therapeutic antibodies (-mab drugs); myeloma M protein

Polyclonal

  • From many different B cell clones
  • Recognize multiple epitopes on the antigen
  • Normal product of infection / vaccination; immune sera (e.g., rabies immune globulin), IVIG, convalescent plasma
CheckOne B cell clone class-switches and now makes IgM, IgG and IgA, all with the same V region. Monoclonal or polyclonal?
Still monoclonal (one clone, one specificity). Polyclonal means many clones, not many isotypes.
Step 1 trap

"One B cell making several isotypes = polyclonal" is wrong. Polyclonal = antibodies from many different clones. A clone that class-switches keeps one idiotype → monoclonal in specificity.

Q7. When can an antibody itself act as an antigen?

When the recipient's immune system sees part of a transferred antibody as foreign. Which part depends on how "foreign" the antibody is:

ResponseTarget on the antibodyTypical trigger
Anti-isotypicConstant region of another species (class-specific)Horse antivenom / antitoxin, mouse monoclonal; or an isotype the host lacks (anti-IgA in IgA deficiency)
Anti-allotypicAllelic constant-region variants of another personHuman antibody from another individual: pooled plasma, IVIG, convalescent serum (polyclonal)
Anti-idiotypicThe unique variable region / paratopeA monoclonal drug: one idiotype at high concentration → anti-drug antibodies (ADA)
  • Isotypes are shared by all healthy members of a species but not between species → even humans react to mouse IgG.
  • All humans share isotypes but differ in allotypes → another person's antibody can be anti-allotypic.
  • Polyclonal preps rarely provoke anti-idiotypic responses: each idiotype is too dilute. A monoclonal presents one idiotype in abundance, and engineered paratopes may not occur naturally → can break tolerance even with a fully human constant region.
  • ADAs can neutralize the drug or speed its clearance → loss of response.
CheckA snakebite victim gets horse antivenom. Ten days later: fever, rash, arthralgia. Which part of the antivenom did he respond to?
The horse constant regions → anti-isotypic response. The resulting immune complexes cause serum sickness.
Step 1 HY
  • Serum sickness = type III hypersensitivity (immune complexes) ~1–2 weeks after foreign protein (antivenom, chimeric mAbs): fever, urticaria, arthralgia, proteinuria, ↓ C3.
  • Immunogenicity of mAbs: murine (-omab) > chimeric (-ximab) > humanized (-zumab) > human (-umab).
  • Selective IgA deficiency: anaphylaxis to IgA-containing blood products (anti-IgA antibodies) → give IgA-depleted / washed products or IgA-deficient donors.
Step 1 trap

A patient missing a class (IgA deficiency) who reacts to donor IgA is making an anti-isotypic response (to a whole class they never learned to tolerate), not anti-idiotypic. Anti-idiotypic = against the V region, classically from a high-dose monoclonal.

Memory tool

How foreign is the antibody?
Other species → ISO (whole class looks alien)
Other person → ALLO (allele differences)
One clone, high dose → IDIO (its individual tip)

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1Convalescent (polyclonal) plasma is given to a patient with active infection. If she reacts to the transferred antibodies, which response is most likely, and why not anti-idiotypic?
Anti-allotypic. People differ by allelic constant-region variants; each idiotype in a polyclonal pool is at low concentration, so an anti-idiotypic response is unlikely. (Transfer of antibody = passive immunity.)
A2A patient on a fully human therapeutic monoclonal antibody loses response; his serum has antibodies that block the drug's antigen-binding site. What are they?
Anti-idiotypic antibodies (ADA) against the drug's unique paratope; a human Fc does not prevent this.
A3A man with undetectable IgA has anaphylaxis minutes into a blood transfusion. Explain.
He never made IgA, so he is not tolerant to it → he formed anti-IgA (anti-isotypic) antibodies; donor IgA triggered anaphylaxis. Use IgA-depleted/washed products next time.
A4You digest IgG with papain and test each fragment on a bacterial suspension. Which fragment binds the bacteria but cannot clump them, and which activates complement?
Fab binds but cannot agglutinate (monovalent); Fc carries the C1q site but no longer binds antigen, so on its own it cannot trigger complement on the bacterium.

LO 3Key vocabularyIsotype, allotype, idiotype · epitope, paratope · valency, affinity, avidity

By the end: you can say where on the antibody each type of variation lives and between whom it varies, list the 9 human isotypes, and explain why low-affinity IgM still binds bacteria so well.
Q1. Isotype, allotype, idiotype: where is the difference, and between whom?
TermLocation of variationVaries betweenControls
IsotypeHeavy-chain constant region (also κ vs λ light chains)Classes / subclasses; the same set in every healthy member of a speciesEffector function
AllotypeAllelic point variants in constant regions (mainly IgG, IgA, κ)Individuals of the same speciesNothing functional; explains anti-allotypic responses
IdiotypeVariable region (paratope / CDRs)Clones: highest variability (millions)Antigen specificity

Isotype

  • 9 human isotypes: IgM, IgD, IgE, IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4, IgA1, IgA2.
    • Set by the heavy chain: μ, δ, ε, γ1–4, α1–2.
    • Light chains have their own 2 isotypes: κ and λ (an antibody uses one or the other).
    • Classes differ mainly in their heavy-chain constant domains (CH2/CH3 = Fc; IgM and IgE add CH4); smaller differences split IgG into 4 and IgA into 2 subclasses.
  • Not conserved across species → anti-isotypic response to animal antibody.

Allotype

  • Allo = allelic: inherited (often single amino-acid) differences in Ig constant regions between people.
  • Not a separate class, just person-to-person variation inside an isotype. Example: IgG "Gm" allotypes (~25 in humans); IgA2 has two allotypes.

Idiotype

  • The unique antigenic features of the V region = what makes this antibody bind this antigen.
  • Generated by V(D)J recombination (+ junctional diversity + H/L chain pairing) and refined by somatic hypermutation.
CheckOne person's IgM and IgG1 carry identical V regions. Which of isotype, allotype, idiotype do they share?
Same idiotype and same allotypes (same person); different isotypes (μ vs γ1). This is exactly what class switching produces.
Memory tool

ISO = the same in all of us (Greek isos, equal). ALLO = alleles differ between people. IDIO = individual clone (like an idiosyncrasy).
Variability: idio >> allo > iso.

Step 1 HY

Fab determines idiotype; Fc determines isotype. Class switching (driven by TFH cytokines + CD40L) changes the isotype only; somatic hypermutation tunes the idiotype → affinity maturation.

Q2. Epitope vs paratope?

Epitope (on the antigen)

  • = antigenic determinant: the part of the antigen an antibody binds
  • One antigen / pathogen has many epitopes → bound by many different antibodies
  • Antibodies bind linear or conformational epitopes; T cells see only linear peptides on MHC

Paratope (on the antibody)

  • The part of the antibody that binds the epitope
  • Formed by the CDRs of VH + VL at the Fab tip
  • CDR differences between clones = idiotypes
  • Hapten = small chemical that binds preformed antibody but cannot induce a response alone (needs a carrier protein).
CheckWhy does a whole bacterium trigger a polyclonal response?
It carries many different epitopes; each is recognized by a different B cell clone.
Memory tool

EPItope sits on the antigen's skin (epi = upon). PARAtope is the antibody's "parachute" landing pad that lands on it. Lock (epitope) and key (paratope).

Q3. Valency, affinity, avidity: how strongly does an antibody hold on?
  • Valency = number of antigen-binding sites.
    • Monomers = 2 (IgG, IgD, IgE, serum IgA, membrane IgM) → prototype is bivalent
    • Secretory IgA dimer = 4 · secreted IgM pentamer = 10 (multimers joined by the J chain)
  • Affinity = strength of a single paratope–epitope interaction.
  • Avidity = overall binding strength across all paratope–epitope interactions of a multivalent antibody.
    • Depends on affinity and valency: more sites engaged → stronger avidity.
    • More than additive: if one site lets go, the others hold the antibody in place until it rebinds.
    • Hinge flexibility helps several sites engage at once.
  • IgM: low affinity, high avidity (10 binding sites), especially on repetitive surface epitopes (bacterial capsules).
  • Later IgG gains high affinity via somatic hypermutation but has only 2 sites.
  • Older terms: intrinsic affinity = one paratope–epitope pair; functional affinity = a bi-/polyvalent antibody on repeated epitopes (a bivalent antibody can bind ~104× more strongly than its monovalent Fab). Some texts use "avidity" for the average binding strength of a whole antibody population.
  • For a neutralizing antibody (blocking a toxin or virus), the key property is high affinity; IgG is the main neutralizer.
CheckIntact IgG and its Fab have identical binding sites. Which holds a bacterium with repeated epitopes more strongly?
Intact IgG: same affinity per site, but 2 sites bind at once → greater avidity.
Memory tool

Avidity ≈ affinity × valency (a handy simplification, not a literal product).
Affinity = one hand's grip; avidity = how hard it is to pull away someone holding on with all ten fingers.

Step 1 trap

Do not call IgM "high affinity." Early IgM is low affinity; it wins on avidity. Affinity maturation makes later IgG the high-affinity antibody.

Buzzwords
"single binding site strength" → affinity"total / functional binding strength" → avidity"number of antigen-binding sites" → valency"antigenic determinant" → epitope"allo = allelic" → allotype

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1IgM made early in a primary response has low affinity, yet it binds bacterial capsules very effectively. Explain.
Pentameric IgM has 10 binding sites → high avidity despite low affinity per site, especially on repetitive surface epitopes.
A2Two healthy siblings both make IgG1 against the same tetanus epitope. Which of isotype, allotype, idiotype could differ between their antibodies?
Allotype (different alleles) and idiotype (different clones/CDRs) can differ; isotype (IgG1) is the same.
A3A lab compares a monovalent Fab with the parent IgG on a virus coated with repeated spike proteins. Predict their affinity and avidity.
Same affinity (identical paratope); IgG has far greater avidity because both arms engage at once.

Q3. IgM and IgD: the naïve B cell's antibodies

IgM

  • Monomer as BCR; secreted as a pentamer with J chain (J chain links cysteine residues) → valency 10
  • First antibody of the primary response; can be made T-independently
  • Most effective activator of the classical pathway: one bound pentamer can engage C1q → key against bacteria
  • Opsonin; forms immune complexes
  • Low affinity, high avidity
  • "Natural" IgM exists before exposure → rapid C1q binding to microbes
  • Can be transported into secretions → partial mucosal back-up when IgA is missing

IgD

  • Monomer (valency 2)
  • Co-expressed with IgM on mature, naïve B cells ready to leave the bone marrow (B cells show IgM first, then IgM + IgD)
  • Very low in serum (≤1%)
  • IgM and IgD on the same cell share the same V region: made by alternative splicing of one heavy-chain RNA, not class switching
Step 1 HY
  • IgM in fetal / cord blood against a pathogen → congenital infection (IgM does not cross the placenta, so the fetus made it).
  • IgM → cold autoimmune hemolytic anemia; IgG → warm AIHA.
  • Acute infection serology: IgM = recent, IgG = past or later phase.
Step 1 trap

IgD appearing next to IgM is not class switching (which needs AID and deletes DNA). It is alternative RNA splicing; both share one V region.

Q4. IgG: the workhorse of the secondary response
  • Primary response: first exposure, naïve B cells → slow; IgM first, then switched antibody.
  • Secondary response: re-exposure, memory cells → faster, larger, higher-affinity, mainly IgG. Each re-exposure can raise affinity further (somatic hypermutation).
  • IgG = most abundant serum Ig (~70–75%); monomer, 4 subclasses (slightly different structure and function; e.g., IgG2 against T-independent polysaccharide antigens).
  • Functions: classical complement, opsonization, ADCC (NK, CD16), neutralization (diffuses well through tissue, high affinity), only Ig across the placenta → passive immunity to the fetus.
  • Placental transfer and long half-life (~3 weeks) depend on FcRn.
  • Which class a B cell switches to depends on the TFH cytokines it receives (e.g., IL-4 → IgE and IgG1).
CheckA newborn's serum contains IgG against rubella but no IgM against it. Interpretation?
Maternal IgG transferred across the placenta (passive), not fetal infection. Fetal IgM would signal congenital infection.
Buzzwords
"secondary / anamnestic response" → IgG"FcRn" → placental transfer, long IgG half-life"CD16 on NK cells" → ADCC by IgG"first antibody made" → IgM

  • Protease-resistant
  • Does NOT activate complement (or opsonize at the mucosa) → no inflammation at mucosal surfaces

  • IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation = type I hypersensitivity (anaphylaxis, allergic asthma, urticaria).
  • Helminth infection → TH2 (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) → ↑ IgE + eosinophilia.
Q7. Two Ig-pattern diseases: selective IgA deficiency and Wiskott-Aldrich

Selective IgA deficiency

Ig pattern ↓↓ IgA (serum + mucosal), normal IgG and IgM

Clinical Most common primary immunodeficiency; often asymptomatic; recurrent sinopulmonary / GI infections in childhood

Twist IgM partly compensates at mucosa; anaphylaxis to IgA in blood products

Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome (WAS)

Genetics X-linked recessive, WAS gene → WASP protein

Mechanism WASP works with Arp2/3 to branch actin → needed for cell migration + TCR signaling → defective T cell activation, ↓ IL-2 → ↓ naïve T proliferation and ↓ Tregs → TH2 skew → ↑ IgE

Triad Thrombocytopenia (small platelets) · eczema · recurrent (pyogenic) infections; ↑ risk of autoimmunity + malignancy

Ig pattern ↑ IgE, ↑ IgA; ↓ (to normal) IgM and IgG

Dx / Tx Smear: small platelets; WAS gene sequencing (definitive). Hematopoietic stem cell transplant; IVIG for infections

CheckWhy does IVIG help a WAS patient, and what type of immunity is it?
It supplies preformed IgG against many pathogens = artificial passive immunity, covering the weak antibody response (especially to encapsulated bacteria).
Memory tool

WATER: Wiskott-Aldrich, Thrombocytopenia, Eczema, Recurrent (pyogenic) infections.
WAS Ig pattern: the two "allergy/mucosa" letters go up (E, A); the "first/memory" letters go down (M, G).

Step 1 HY

Boards phrase WAS as "↑ IgE and IgA, ↓ to normal IgM and IgG." Small platelets distinguish WAS from ITP (large platelets). Infections are often with encapsulated organisms.

Step 1 trap

Eczema + recurrent infections + high IgE also fits hyper-IgE (Job) syndrome, but that has normal platelets, cold staph abscesses and retained primary teeth. Thrombocytopenia with small platelets in a boy → WAS.

ApplyLO 4 application questions

A1A breastfed infant is protected from enteric pathogens in the gut lumen. Which antibody, what type of immunity, and why no gut inflammation?
Secretory IgA from milk = natural passive immunity. It neutralizes and blocks attachment but does not activate complement; secretory component makes it protease-resistant.
A2A child with a heavy intestinal helminth burden: which Ig class is most elevated, and which cell does it recruit?
IgE → eosinophils (plus mast cells). TH2 cytokines drive both ↑ IgE and eosinophilia.
A3A 2-year-old boy has eczema, petechiae with small platelets on smear, and recurrent pyogenic infections. Predict his immunoglobulins.
Wiskott-Aldrich: ↑ IgE and IgA, ↓ (to normal) IgM and IgG. X-linked WAS mutation; cure = stem cell transplant.
A4A patient with selective IgA deficiency has only mild sinus infections. Which antibody partly covers his mucosa?
IgM: pentameric IgM with J chain can also be transported into secretions.
A5Cord blood shows IgM against Toxoplasma. What does it mean?
Congenital infection: IgM cannot cross the placenta, so the fetus made it itself.
