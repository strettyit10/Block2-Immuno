NSAIDs I: Mechanisms, Uses and Toxicity

Learning objectives

  1. LO 1Explain what all NSAIDs share: what they are approved to treat, which chemical families they fall into, and why being weak acids that ride on albumin matters.
  2. LO 2Explain how blocking cyclooxygenase lets NSAIDs calm inflammation, relieve pain (and why that relief has a ceiling), and bring a fever down.
  3. LO 3Explain when and why NSAIDs are chosen for menstrual cramps, for fever (including the child with a viral illness), and for closing a patent ductus in a newborn.
  4. LO 4Explain why aspirin, and only at a low dose, protects patients with unstable angina or a previous heart attack.
  5. LO 5Recognize the cardiovascular and stomach side effects NSAIDs cause at ordinary doses, and why the COX-2 selective drugs swapped stomach safety for heart risk.
  6. LO 6Know when NSAIDs must be stopped or modified, especially around surgery and in patients at risk of GI bleeding, and how to protect the stomach.

Q1. What are NSAIDs, and what are they approved to treat?
  • NSAID = nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug: anti-inflammatory without being a corticosteroid.
  • Key class facts:
    • Multiple therapeutic indications
    • Most widely used class of drugs (revenues >$10 billion/year)
    • Many are over-the-counter → patients take them without telling you; ask every patient.
  • Six approved therapeutic uses:
    1. Inflammation
    2. Acute and chronic pain
    3. Fever
    4. Primary dysmenorrhea (menstrual cramps)
    5. Unstable angina / post-MI → aspirin only
    6. Patent ductus arteriosus in neonates → indomethacin only
CheckWhich two NSAID uses belong to a single drug each?
Unstable angina / post-MI → aspirin and neonatal PDA closure → indomethacin.

Q4. Which two shared properties come from being carboxylic acids, and why do they matter?
  • Weakly acidic
  • Highly bound to plasma albumin
  • Together → NSAIDs localize to inflamed sites (mechanism in LO 2 Q3), which is part of how they work.
  • Albumin binding also sets up displacement drug interactions (preview of NSAIDs II).

All NSAIDs inhibit COX-1 and COX-2 with varying potencies; some are more COX-2 selective. But the anti-inflammatory EFFICACY of every NSAID is equivalent to aspirin: none exceeds it (not indomethacin, not sulindac, not celecoxib).

Check"Celecoxib must beat ibuprofen for RA because it is more potent at COX-2." True?
No. Potency ≠ efficacy. All NSAIDs have the same maximal anti-inflammatory efficacy as aspirin; COX-2 inhibitors have not beaten other NSAIDs in chronic inflammatory disease.
Step 1 HY
  • Boards love "reversible vs irreversible": aspirin = irreversible (acetylates COX); every other NSAID = reversible.
  • Celecoxib contains a sulfonamide → avoid in sulfa allergy.
  • Ketorolac = the parenteral (IV/IM) NSAID for short-term moderate–severe pain.
Step 1 trap

Don't confuse potency (dose needed, COX affinity) with efficacy (maximal effect). A more potent or more selective NSAID is not more anti-inflammatory. By contrast, for glucocorticoids, receptor potency does track anti-inflammatory efficacy.

Memory tool
  • Analgesic-only = "A-K": Acetaminophen, Ketorolac.
  • Anti-inflammatory-only = "I-S-C": Indomethacin, Sulindac, Celecoxib.
  • "-profen" = propionic; "-ac" ending (diclofenac, ketorolac, sulindac, etodolac) = acetic.
Buzzwords
"black box warning" → celecoxib (CV + GI)"aspirin only" → unstable angina / post-MI"indomethacin only" → neonatal PDA"weak acid, albumin-bound" → NSAID class

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A pharmacy student says naproxen is "stronger" than aspirin for rheumatoid arthritis inflammation because it has a longer half-life. Correct?
No. Half-life changes dosing frequency (naproxen ~12 h → twice daily), not maximal anti-inflammatory efficacy, which is equal to aspirin for all NSAIDs.
A2You need a non-opioid analgesic that is not approved for inflammation and can be given parenterally after an outpatient procedure. Which drug-to-know fits?
Ketorolac: analgesic-only indication, injectable, short-term use.
A3Which drug-to-know is NOT a carboxylic acid NSAID, and why does that predict poor anti-inflammatory activity?
Acetaminophen. It is essentially neutral (very weak phenolic acid) and <20% protein-bound, so it is not carried to and trapped in inflamed tissue (see LO 2).

Step 1 trap

Glucocorticoids block both branches (upstream of AA and COX-2 induction); NSAIDs block only COX. "Drug that reduces both prostaglandins and leukotrienes" = a steroid, not an NSAID.

Q5. What is the analgesic ceiling, and how do you get past it?

Analgesic ceiling = the dose beyond which giving more drug gives no more pain relief.

Four rules of the NSAID analgesic ceiling

  1. All NSAIDs have a ceiling.
  2. Not all NSAIDs have the same ceiling.
  3. COX potency does NOT predict the ceiling (so analgesia involves more than COX block).
  4. NSAID + opioid is additive/synergistic and "raises" the ceiling.
Ceiling vs aspirinDrugs
Higher than aspirinIbuprofen, naproxen, ketoprofen, diflunisal, ketorolac
Equal to aspirinAcetaminophen, indomethacin, sulindac, celecoxib
  • Dose-sparing effect: combining lets you use lower doses of both → fewer opioid and NSAID side effects.
  • Fixed combinations: codeine, hydrocodone, oxycodone with aspirin (e.g., Percodan) or acetaminophen (Vicodin, Percocet).
  • An FDA advisory panel recommended stopping acetaminophen/opioid combinations (acetaminophen hepatotoxicity as doses escalate).
CheckIndomethacin is one of the most potent COX inhibitors. Will it give better post-op analgesia than aspirin?
No. Its ceiling equals aspirin's; COX potency doesn't predict the ceiling.
Step 1 trap

When pain persists at the max NSAID dose, the answer is not "double the NSAID" (ceiling, more toxicity) but add an opioid (or step up the ladder).

Memory tool

Picture a room with a ceiling: piling on more NSAID just bumps your head. An opioid is a ladder through the roof. "Propionic acids out-punch aspirin for pain" (ibuprofen, naproxen, ketoprofen all have higher ceilings).

Buzzwords
"plateau of relief with ↑ dose" → analgesic ceiling"lower doses of both" → dose-sparing NSAID + opioid"sensitize nociceptors" → PG role in pain
Q6. How do NSAIDs bring a fever down?
PyrogenInfection → cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF)
→
Hypothalamus↑ PGs (PGE2) → set point raised
→
"Chills"Vasoconstriction, piloerection, epinephrine, shivering → temp climbs
→
NSAID↓ hypothalamic PGs → set point reset → vasodilation + sweating → temp falls
  • NSAIDs decrease pyrogen-induced PG elevation in the hypothalamus → "reset" the set point → heat loss + ↓ core temperature.
  • No effect on normal body temperature (no pyrogen-driven PGs to block). Contrast hyperthermia (heat stroke): set point is normal, so NSAIDs don't help.
CheckWill ibuprofen lower the temperature of a healthy afebrile runner?
No. NSAIDs only lower a set point that has been raised by pyrogen-induced PGs.
Step 1 HY

Fever pathway: IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α → ↑ COX-2 in hypothalamus (OVLT/preoptic area) → PGE2 → ↑ set point. Antipyretics act on PGE2 synthesis; heat stroke (normal set point) needs cooling, not antipyretics.

Buzzwords
"resets the hypothalamic thermostat" → antipyretic action"chills, rigors" → set point just raised"sweating, flushing" → set point just lowered

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A patient with knee OA takes acetaminophen; pain improves but the warm, swollen effusion does not. Explain.
Acetaminophen is analgesic, not anti-inflammatory: weak peripheral COX block and no accumulation in the acidic, inflamed joint.
A2Predict: a new NSAID is 10× more potent at COX than ibuprofen. Will its analgesic ceiling be 10× higher?
No. COX potency does not predict the ceiling; potency only changes the dose needed.
A3After dental extraction, a patient at maximum ibuprofen dose still has moderate pain. Best adjustment?
Add a weak–moderate opioid (e.g., hydrocodone): additive/synergistic, raises the ceiling and is dose-sparing.
A4A patient on high-dose prednisone has fewer leukotrienes AND prostaglandins. A patient on naproxen has fewer prostaglandins only. Why?
Steroids act upstream (↓ PLA2-driven arachidonic acid release, ↓ COX-2 induction); NSAIDs block COX only, leaving LOX running.

Q3. Which antipyretics are used, and what is Reye syndrome?
  • Most commonly used antipyretics: aspirin, acetaminophen, ibuprofen.
  • Preferred antipyretic traits: effective, quick onset, short half-life, minimal side effects.
  • Acetaminophen is an effective antipyretic even though it is a weak COX inhibitor peripherally.

Reye syndrome

Rare, life-threatening: vomiting, lethargy, ↑ liver enzymes → coma.

Who: children recovering from viral infections (influenza, chickenpox).

Risk: higher with aspirin than with acetaminophen or ibuprofen → aspirin not given to children for fever.

Not an overdose: occurs at normal doses (aspirin + virus interaction).

CheckIs Reye syndrome a dose-dependent salicylate toxicity?
No. It is an interaction between aspirin and a viral illness in a child, seen at ordinary doses.
Step 1 HY
  • Reye = fulminant hepatic failure + encephalopathy: microvesicular fatty liver, hypoglycemia, ↑ ammonia, cerebral edema.
  • Mechanism: aspirin metabolites ↓ mitochondrial β-oxidation.
  • Exception: aspirin is still given to children for Kawasaki disease.
Step 1 trap

Reye (child, post-viral, vomiting, lethargy, ↑ AST/ALT) vs salicylate overdose (tinnitus, hyperventilation → respiratory alkalosis then anion-gap metabolic acidosis, fever). Same drug, different picture (overdose in NSAIDs II).

Buzzwords
"child + flu/varicella + aspirin" → Reye syndrome"microvesicular steatosis + encephalopathy" → Reye"effective, quick onset, short t½" → ideal antipyretic

Q1. Which prostanoids control platelets and vessel tone, and where do they come from?

CheckWhich enzyme makes platelet TxA2, and which make endothelial PGI2?
TxA2: COX-1 (platelets). PGI2: COX-1 and COX-2 (endothelium).
Q2. How does low-dose aspirin produce a net antithrombotic, vasodilatory effect?
  • Aspirin is an irreversible inhibitor of COX-1 and COX-2: it acetylates a serine in the active site. Recovery needs new enzyme.
  • Inhibition is permanent in platelets (lifespan 8–10 days) but transient in endothelial cells.
  • Overall predominant effect: inhibition of platelet aggregation + vasodilation (↓ TxA2 + PGI2 sparing).
  • This protective effect is not seen with other NSAIDs (they are reversible, so platelets recover as the drug washes out).
CheckWhy does one low dose of aspirin affect platelets for a week but endothelium for only hours?
Platelets are anucleate and cannot make new COX; endothelial cells resynthesize COX.
Step 1 HY
  • Aspirin → ↑ bleeding time, no change in PT or PTT.
  • Boards dose bands: <300 mg/day antiplatelet; 300–2400 mg/day analgesic/antipyretic; 2400–4000 mg/day anti-inflammatory.
  • Ibuprofen taken before aspirin occupies platelet COX-1 and blocks aspirin's acetylation → lost cardioprotection (give aspirin first).
Memory tool

PGI2 = "Platelet-Gathering Inhibitor". TxA2 = "Thrombus eXcitor". Aspirin "permanently fires the platelet's factory worker (COX), and the platelet can't hire a new one: no nucleus, no HR."

Buzzwords
"irreversible acetylation of COX" → aspirin"PGI2 sparing" → low-dose aspirin"isolated ↑ bleeding time" → aspirin/NSAID platelet effect
Q3. Who should take it, at what dose, and what is the benefit?
  • Aspirin is the only NSAID approved for prophylaxis in:
    • Unstable angina
    • Post-myocardial infarction
  • Benefit: trials show a ~50% decrease in recurrent MI (both conditions improve with less platelet aggregation + better coronary flow).
  • Dose: 60–325 mg/day. Only the lower dose is protective; the protective effect is lost at higher doses (endothelial COX also suppressed → PGI2 no longer spared).
  • Without a history of MI (primary prevention)? Current recommendation (USPSTF, 2022) is against starting low-dose aspirin for primary prevention of CVD in adults ≥60 years: bleeding risk outweighs benefit.

Secondary prevention: YES

  • Prior MI, unstable angina
  • Low dose, daily
  • ~50% ↓ re-infarction

Primary prevention ≥60 y: don't start

  • No prior CVD event
  • Bleeding risk > benefit
CheckA patient takes 1,000 mg aspirin daily "for his heart." Problem?
Yes: only low dose (60–325 mg/day) is cardioprotective; higher doses lose the PGI2-sparing advantage (and add GI/bleeding risk).
Step 1 trap

"More aspirin = more protection" is wrong. Also, secondary prevention (post-MI) ≠ primary prevention (no event yet). A post-MI patient should take it; a healthy 70-year-old generally should not start.

Step 1 HY

Acute MI / ACS: chew aspirin immediately (mortality benefit). Other antiplatelets for comparison: clopidogrel (P2Y12/ADP), abciximab (GP IIb/IIIa).

ApplyLO 4 application questions

A1A 58-year-old 3 months post-MI and a healthy 72-year-old both ask about daily low-dose aspirin. Advice?
Post-MI: yes (secondary prevention, ~50% ↓ recurrent MI). Healthy 72-year-old: don't start for primary prevention (bleeding risk).
A2A man on low-dose aspirin after an MI starts ibuprofen 400 mg several times daily for knee pain. Why might cardioprotection fall, and what's safer?
Ibuprofen can occupy platelet COX-1 and block aspirin's irreversible acetylation; NSAIDs at analgesic doses also ↓ PGI2 (vasoconstriction). Acetaminophen is the safer analgesic.
A3Predict: if a drug blocked COX irreversibly but platelets had nuclei, would low-dose aspirin still be selectively antiplatelet?
No. Platelets would resynthesize COX like endothelium; the selectivity depends on the platelet's lack of a nucleus.

Side-effect families of NSAIDs: cardiovascular · GI · COX-2 inhibitors (here) and renal, hypersensitivity, pregnancy, drug interactions (NSAIDs II).

Q1. What are the cardiovascular effects of NSAIDs at analgesic doses?

Platelets (COX-1 → TxA2 ↓)

  • Inhibition of platelet aggregation
  • Longer bleeding time

Endothelium (COX-1/2 → PGI2 ↓)

  • Vasoconstriction → ↑ blood pressure
CheckWhy might a hypertensive patient's BP rise after starting daily naproxen?
↓ endothelial prostacyclin → vasoconstriction (plus renal Na⁺ retention, NSAIDs II).
Q2. How big is the NSAID GI problem, and how do NSAIDs cause ulcers?
  • ~30 million people use NSAIDs daily; ~13 million for chronic illness (RA, OA).
  • 10–20%: heartburn, nausea, indigestion.
  • 2–4%: massive bleed and/or perforated ulcer.
  • NSAID-related GI deaths have been estimated in the same range as deaths from HIV → GI toxicity is the major limitation of NSAIDs.

Three mechanisms of NSAID-induced ulcers

1 · Direct irritant

Acidic drug injures mucosa on contact.

2 · Concentrated in epithelium

Gastric epithelial cells concentrate NSAIDs → ↓ oxidative phosphorylation, ↓ mucosal cell proliferation.

3 · ↓ Prostaglandins

Loss of COX-1 "housekeeping" PGs → ↓ mucus, ↓ HCO₃⁻, ↓ blood flow, ↓ repair.

  • Ibuprofen lowest (~2); naproxen (~9) and indomethacin (~11) intermediate; ketoprofen highest of the drugs to know (~24). Acetaminophen ≈ 1.1 (no added risk).
CheckName the three mechanisms of NSAID ulcers.
Direct irritation · concentration in gastric epithelial cells (↓ ox-phos, ↓ proliferation) · ↓ prostaglandin synthesis.
Step 1 HY

Gastric PGE2/PGI2: ↓ acid secretion, ↑ mucus + HCO₃⁻, ↑ mucosal blood flow. NSAIDs are the #2 cause of peptic ulcer after H. pylori; classic: elderly RA/OA patient with melena or hematemesis.

Step 1 trap

Enteric coating doesn't make NSAIDs safe: it reduces the direct-contact injury only; the systemic PG loss still happens once the drug is absorbed.

  • Not superior in chronic inflammatory disorders.

LO 6Contraindications and precautions: surgery and GI protectionWhen to stop, what to substitute, how to shield the stomach

By the end: you can time NSAID discontinuation before surgery, choose a post-op analgesic when bleeding is a risk, and list every strategy to minimize NSAID GI injury.
Q1. How long before surgery should NSAIDs be stopped?
  • Aspirin is bad <7 days before surgery → stop ≥7 days ahead (irreversible; platelet lifespan 8–10 days).
  • Other NSAIDs are reversible → discontinue for 5 half-lives. Ibuprofen ≈ 1 day.
  • Routinely stopping all NSAIDs 5–10 days ahead is based on the misconception that all NSAIDs affect platelets like aspirin → leaves patients without adequate pain control.
CheckWhy is "5 half-lives" enough for ibuprofen but not for aspirin?
After 5 half-lives ~97% of a reversible drug is gone and platelet COX recovers. Aspirin's effect outlasts the drug: platelets stay inhibited until replaced (8–10 days).
Q2. What should be used for pain after surgery when bleeding is a risk?
  • Post-surgery with risk of bleeding → NSAIDs are contraindicated (antiplatelet effect, longer bleeding time).
  • Use acetaminophen (no meaningful antiplatelet action) ± opioids.
CheckAfter open-heart surgery with chest drains still bleeding, which non-opioid analgesic?
Acetaminophen. NSAIDs are contraindicated with ongoing bleeding risk.
Step 1 trap

Two common wrong answers: "stop ibuprofen 7–10 days before" (over-treating; it's reversible) and "stop aspirin 1–2 days before" (under-treating; irreversible). Remember which drug is irreversible.

Memory tool

"Aspirin = a week; everyone else = five half-lives." Aspirin's clock is the platelet's life, not the drug's.

A1Bob has surgery in 7 days and takes both aspirin and ibuprofen. What should he do?
Stop aspirin now (≥7 days); stop ibuprofen 1–2 days before (≈5 half-lives). Acetaminophen is fine to use.

A3A 26-year-old woman on chronic naproxen asks for misoprostol for stomach protection; she is trying to conceive. Concern?
Misoprostol is an abortifacient: avoid in pregnancy or when pregnancy is possible; use a PPI instead.

It assumes all NSAIDs act like aspirin. Reversible NSAIDs need only 5 half-lives; the long hold leaves patients without adequate pain control.
