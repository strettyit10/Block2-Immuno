Mucosal & Skin Immune System

Learning objectives

  1. LO 1Explain what sets mucosal immunity apart from systemic immunity: which surfaces count, how its immune cells are organized (Peyer's patches, MALT) or scattered, and why it must tolerate most of what it meets.
  2. LO 2Explain how the gut lining acts as both a wall and an alarm system: junctions, mucus, antimicrobial peptides, microbial sensors, alarmins, and the choice between a regulatory, type 17 or type 2 response.
  3. LO 3Describe the special tools that defend mucosal surfaces, especially intraepithelial lymphocytes, and how lymphocytes primed in the gut find their way back to mucosal tissue.
  4. LO 4Explain how secretory IgA is made, carried across the epithelium and used to protect surfaces without causing inflammation.
  5. LO 5Explain the routes by which antigen in the gut lumen reaches dendritic cells, and how those cells decide between tolerance and immunity.
  6. LO 6Explain why commensal bacteria keep mucosal tissue healthy, and how a broken barrier or a misdirected response to them leads to allergy, asthma, celiac disease and IBD.

LO 1What makes mucosal immunity differentSites, organization and the tolerate-vs-eliminate balance

By the end: you can list the mucosal tissues, name the two features that define mucosal sites, fill in the epithelium / lamina propria / Peyer's patch table, and explain how a Peyer's patch differs from a lymph node.
Q1. Which surfaces are "mucosal," and what problem do they have to solve?
  • Mucosal tissues = epithelial surfaces in contact with the outside world:
    • GI tract (the main focus here), respiratory tract, urogenital tract, mammary glands, plus conjunctiva and exocrine glands (lacrimal, salivary).
    • The skin is the other big barrier tissue with its own immune system (see the Step 1 box below).
  • These sites are not sterile: they meet food, inhaled particles and an enormous commensal microbiota, far more foreign antigen than any other tissue.
  • So the mucosal immune system must balance two opposite jobs:
    • Ignore / tolerate food antigens, harmless substances and commensal bacteria.
    • Eliminate pathogenic microbes, their products and toxins.
  • A systemic-style inflammatory response to every luminal antigen → chronic inflammation that would wreck the absorptive lining. So the gut's default is tolerance (Tregs, IL-10, TGF-β); inflammation is saved for microbes that breach the barrier.

CheckWhy would a "systemic-style" response to gut antigens be harmful?
The gut sees food and commensals constantly. Treating them as threats would cause constant inflammation that destroys the absorptive epithelium, so tolerance is the default.
Q2. What are the two defining features of a mucosal site, and where do the immune cells sit?
  • Two features distinguish mucosal sites:
    • The epithelial layer: a barrier and an active immune sensor.
    • The microbiota: commensal bacteria living on the surface.
  • Gut lining basics:
    • Enterocytes (most epithelial cells) absorb nutrients; their microvilli and the villus shape maximize surface area.
    • Crypts at the villus base hold stem cells that keep replacing the epithelium; Paneth cells sit at the crypt base.
    • Goblet cells are scattered in the epithelium and make mucus.
  • Immune cells are either organized (Peyer's patches, isolated lymphoid follicles) or dispersed (lamina propria, within the epithelium).

Q3. What is a Peyer's patch, and how does it differ from a lymph node?
  • Peyer's patches = organized mucosal secondary lymphoid tissue (part of GALT), concentrated in the ileum, sitting just under the epithelium (mucosa / submucosa).
    • On histology: large, dense, basophilic (purple) lymphoid nodules below the villi, no capsule, often with pale germinal centers (where B cells proliferate and switch to IgA).
  • Key difference from a lymph node: a Peyer's patch samples antigen directly from the gut lumen through the overlying M cells, not from afferent lymph.
    • It has efferent lymphatics draining to the mesenteric lymph nodes, where gut responses are also primed.
  • MALT (mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue) = all organized lymphoid tissue at mucosal surfaces (inductive sites):
    • GALT: Peyer's patches, appendix, isolated lymphoid follicles.
    • NALT: tonsils, adenoids. BALT: bronchus-associated.
    • Solitary lymphatic nodules (no capsule, with germinal centers) occur throughout the GI, respiratory and urinary tracts; clusters of them in the ileum = Peyer's patches.
  • The microbiota primes MALT development: germ-free animals have small, poorly developed Peyer's patches and little IgA.

Step 1 HY — skin immune system
  • Langerhans cells = epidermal DCs (Birbeck granules "tennis rackets", CD1a⁺, S-100⁺, langerin/CD207) → carry antigen to regional lymph nodes. Neoplasm = Langerhans cell histiocytosis.
  • Keratinocytes express TLRs and release alarmins (IL-1, IL-33, TSLP) and defensins; dermal DCs, mast cells and macrophages sit below.
  • Tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM): CD69⁺, often CD103⁺; stay in epidermis/dermis and respond fast on re-exposure (fixed drug eruption, recurrent psoriasis plaques).
  • Skin lacks M cells, organized lymphoid follicles and dedicated mesenteric-type nodes → skin antigen must be carried by DCs to regional nodes; skin-homing T cells use CLA + CCR4/CCR10.
Step 1 trap
  • Peyer's patches have no afferent lymphatics: antigen comes from the lumen (M cells). Only efferent lymph → mesenteric LN.
  • "Unencapsulated lymphoid nodule in the mucosa" = MALT, not a lymph node.
  • Peyer's patches are densest in the ileum (not duodenum or colon).
Step 1 HY — MALT lymphoma

MALT lymphoma (extranodal marginal-zone B-cell lymphoma) arises from chronic antigen stimulation: H. pylori gastritis (may regress with eradication), Sjögren (parotid), Hashimoto (thyroid).

Memory tool

"Patch = Pick-up, Propria = Perform." Peyer's patches are where antigen is picked up and naive cells are primed (inductive); the lamina propria is where effectors perform.

MALT family = G-N-B: Gut, Nose (tonsils/adenoids), Bronchi.

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1Mice are raised completely germ-free. What do you predict about their gut-associated lymphoid tissue and mucosal IgA?
Small, immature Peyer's patches and follicles with little IgA. The microbiota primes MALT development; without it the inductive sites barely form.
A2A biopsy of the distal ileum shows large purple lymphoid nodules beneath the villi with pale centers and no capsule. Name the structure and what happens in the pale centers.
Peyer's patch with germinal centers. The pale centers are where B cells proliferate and class switch (largely to IgA).
A3A drug blocks lymph flow from the gut. Which is still possible: antigen reaching a Peyer's patch, or primed cells reaching the mesenteric lymph node?
Antigen still reaches the Peyer's patch (it comes straight from the lumen through M cells). Transport to the mesenteric node needs efferent lymph and would be blocked.
A4A patient with chronic H. pylori gastritis develops a low-grade gastric B-cell lymphoma. What tissue did it arise from, and what may make it regress?
Gastric MALT (acquired from chronic antigen stimulation). H. pylori eradication can make early MALT lymphoma regress.

LO 2The epithelium: wall and alarmBarrier, constitutive defenses, sensors, alarmins and the response choice

By the end: you can name the junction proteins, sort mucosal defenses into constitutive vs inducible, explain why gut TLRs are basolateral, match each trigger to a Treg / type 17 / type 2 response, and compare intestinal macrophages with blood monocytes.
Q1. How does the epithelium form a selective barrier, and what happens when it fails?
  • The columnar epithelium absorbs nutrients but keeps microbes out. Three junctions seal the gaps between cells:
    • Tight junctions (claudins, occludin, ZO-1): the main seal blocking paracellular flux of microbes and antigens.
    • Adherens junctions (E-cadherin, α/β-catenins).
    • Desmosomes (desmoglein, desmocollin, desmoplakin; anchor keratin).
  • Barrier breakdown / damage is a common prerequisite of mucosal pathology.
    • Loss of tight junctions → "leaky gut" → more luminal antigen crosses → basolateral sensors fire → inflammatory cells recruited.
    • Food allergy, atopy, asthma, celiac disease and IBD all involve epithelial barrier breakdown.
CheckWhich junction is the main barrier against paracellular passage of bacteria, and which proteins make it?
Tight junction: claudins, occludin, ZO-1.

Q3. How does the epithelium sense microbes without reacting to the commensals sitting on it?
  • TLRs on enterocytes are on the basolateral surface and in endosomes, not on the apical (luminal) surface.
    • They only detect microbes that have crossed the barrier. Apical TLRs would be triggered constantly by the microbiota → chronic inflammation.
  • NOD1 / NOD2 = cytosolic sensors for bacterial products that reach the cytoplasm.
  • Inflammasomes = cytoplasmic multiprotein "nanomachines" that activate caspase-1.
  • Both pathways end in alarmins and inflammatory cytokines.

Constant LPS signaling from the commensal flora → NF-κB activation and chronic inflammation without any infection.
Step 1 HY

NOD2 (CARD15) loss-of-function = the classic genetic risk factor for Crohn disease; ATG16L1 (autophagy) is another. Both mean bacteria get in but are not cleared.

Step 1 trap

NOD-like receptors are cytosolic; TLRs are on membranes (surface or endosome). Don't place NOD2 "on the cell surface."

Q4. How do the epithelium and DCs decide between tolerance, a type 17 response and a type 2 response?

1 · Default: regulatory (Treg) response

  • The gut epithelium's default is a Treg response → tolerance of commensals and food.
  • Driven by TGF-β, present at high baseline levels (made by stromal cells and DCs).
    • TGF-β is secreted latent (bound to a latency peptide and to extracellular matrix); eosinophil MMPs release the active form.
    • Why lean toward Tregs? Enterocytes are especially vulnerable to Th1 cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF), so IL-10/TGF-β-producing Tregs that suppress Th1 activation are preferred.
  • Commensal Clostridium species ferment dietary fiber → butyrate → ↑ TGF-β → Tregs. Bacteroides fragilis also favors Tregs.

2 · Type 17: microbe crosses the barrier

  • Basolateral TLRs / NODs / inflammasome → IL-1 + IL-6 + TGF-β (with IL-23) → Th17 → clears extracellular bacteria and fungi.
  • Segmented filamentous bacteria (SFB) and lactobacilli are especially good Th17 inducers.
  • Same TGF-β, different partner: TGF-β alone → Treg; TGF-β + IL-6 → Th17. Th17 cells can later convert into Tregs to switch the response off.

3 · Type 2: worms and epithelial damage

  • Tuft cells sense succinate (a worm metabolic byproduct) → release the alarmin IL-25.
  • Damaged enterocytes (worms bite / embed to avoid being swept away) → IL-33 (IL-1 family) and TSLP.
    • In the airway, smoking and pollutants damage epithelium → IL-33 → type 2 responses.
  • IL-25 + IL-33 + TSLP → ILC2 activation → IL-13 (goblet mucus + smooth-muscle contraction), IL-5 (eosinophils), IL-4 (Th2, IgE switching) → Th2 responses with eosinophil and mast-cell activation.
CheckDo tuft cells directly recruit eosinophils and mast cells?
No, indirectly. Tuft cells release IL-25 → activates ILC2s → IL-5 / IL-4 / IL-13 → eosinophil and mast-cell activation, mucus and contraction.
Step 1 HY
  • Th17: induced by TGF-β + IL-6 (± IL-1, IL-23); makes IL-17, IL-22; recruits neutrophils. Defect (STAT3 / Job syndrome, IL-17 pathway) → Candida and S. aureus infections.
  • Treg: TGF-β + IL-2 → FoxP3; make IL-10, TGF-β. IPEX (FOXP3 mutation) → autoimmune enteropathy (diarrhea), type 1 diabetes, eczema.
Step 1 trap

TGF-β appears on both sides: alone = Treg, with IL-6 = Th17. A question that adds IL-6 to TGF-β is steering you to Th17, not tolerance.

Memory tool

Type 2 alarmins = "25-33-TSLP" → think "Tuft, Trauma, Thymic stromal". Worm expulsion = "weep and sweep" (IL-13 mucus + contraction).

Q5. What do the gut's sentinel cells and innate lymphocytes do differently?
  • Sentinel cells (macrophages, DCs, mast cells) express PRRs, respond to microbes and danger signals, and help move antigen to lymph nodes, but with limited inflammation.
  • Intestinal (resident) macrophages eliminate pathogens without creating inflammation:
    • Cannot initiate or maintain inflammation; do not secrete inflammatory cytokines; are not professional APCs.
    • Lack FcγR, FcαR, LPS receptors (TLR4, CD14), C3b receptors and IL-2 / IL-3 receptors.
    • PRR ligation fails to activate NF-κB.
    • They do secrete cytokine: large amounts of IL-10 (anti-inflammatory; supports Tregs and tissue repair).
  • Blood monocytes recruited by chemokines from activated epithelial cells are inflammatory → they account for the inflammation of invasive infection.

  • Innate lymphocytes are abundant in mucosa (within the epithelium or lamina propria): ILC2s, ILC3s, MAIT cells (mucosal-associated invariant T) and γδ T cells.
    • ILC3 → IL-22 → epithelial repair.
    • ILC2 → responds to epithelial damage (IL-25, IL-33, TSLP) → type 2; implicated in asthma and allergy.
CheckTrue or false: intestinal macrophages secrete no cytokines at all.
False. They make no inflammatory cytokines, but they secrete large amounts of IL-10.
Step 1 trap

If a vignette describes intense mucosal inflammation (e.g., Salmonella), the inflammatory macrophage-lineage cells are newly recruited blood monocytes, not resident intestinal macrophages.

Memory tool

Resident gut macrophage = "silent janitor": eats and kills debris and microbes quietly, hums IL-10, never sounds the alarm.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A child with a helminth infection has eosinophilia, ↑ IgE and ↑ intestinal mucus. Trace the pathway from the epithelium.
Tuft cells (succinate → IL-25) + damaged enterocytes (IL-33, TSLP) → ILC2 → IL-13 (mucus, contraction), IL-5 (eosinophils), IL-4 (Th2, IgE).
A2Salmonella breaches the ileal epithelium and causes intense inflammation. Which cells produce the inflammatory response, given resident macrophages are non-inflammatory?
Recruited blood monocytes (plus neutrophils), called in by chemokines from activated epithelial cells.
A3A smoker's damaged airway epithelium releases large amounts of IL-33. Which response is favored, and via which innate cell?
Type 2 (Th2) via ILC2s → IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 → eosinophils, IgE switching, mucus hypersecretion: a link between smoke injury and allergic airway disease.
A4A patient on prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotics develops overgrowth of an intestinal pathogen. Which constitutive defense was lost?
The commensal microbiome (colonization resistance: competition for nutrients/space and commensal antimicrobials).
A5A high-fiber diet raises colonic butyrate. What happens to the mucosal T-cell balance?
More Tregs. Clostridium species ferment fiber → butyrate → ↑ TGF-β → FoxP3⁺ Treg induction → homeostasis.

LO 3Mucosal effectors, IELs and homingThe special tools at the surface and how primed cells get back there

  • Smooth-muscle contractility: expels worms and mucus.
  • Defensins: antimicrobial peptides from epithelium.
  • Intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs): surveillance and destruction of distressed / damaged epithelial cells.

Step 1 HY
  • NKG2D = activating receptor on NK cells, CD8 T cells and γδ T cells; ligands MICA/MICB are induced by stress, infection and transformation.
  • IL-15 = key survival/activation cytokine for NK cells and CD8 memory / IELs (IL-2 family, shares IL-2Rβ and γc).

"TL" on IEL figures is the thymus leukemia antigen (non-classical MHC I), bound by CD8αα. And CD8αα (homodimer) = innate-like; CD8αβ (heterodimer) = classical, MHC-restricted.

Memory tool

"MIC check!" A stressed cell holds up its MIC (MIC-A/B); the IEL listens with NKG2D and kills. IL-15 turns up the volume.

Q3. How do lymphocytes primed in the gut get back to the gut (and other mucosae)?
  • Imprinting: gut DCs in Peyer's patches / mesenteric LN make retinoic acid (vitamin A metabolite) → induces gut-homing receptors α4β7 and CCR9 on the activated B and T cells.
    • Retinoic acid also favors Treg polarization and IgA class switching.
    • Under most normal circumstances, only lymphocytes that first meet antigen in gut-associated lymphoid tissue are induced to express gut-specific homing molecules.
  • Integrin attachment + chemokine signaling then localize them in the mucosa:

  • Common mucosal immune system: lymphocytes activated at one mucosal site home to other mucosal sites too.
    • Intranasal vaccine → protective IgA in the gut and genital tract; strategies in development include nasal sprays and "prime / pull" (prime systemically, then pull effectors to the mucosa).
    • Enteromammary circuit: gut-primed T cells and IgA plasma cells seed the mammary gland → breast-milk IgA against the mother's gut microbes → passive protection of the infant's gut.

CheckWhich integrin gets a T cell out of the blood into the lamina propria, and which one keeps it inside the epithelium?
α4β7 (binds MAdCAM-1 on gut endothelium) → lamina propria. αEβ7 / CD103 (binds E-cadherin) → stays in the epithelium as an IEL.
Step 1 HY — homing drugs
  • Vedolizumab = anti-α4β7 → gut-selective block of lymphocyte entry; used in Crohn disease and UC.
  • Natalizumab = anti-α4 (blocks α4β1–VCAM-1 and α4β7) → MS and Crohn; risk of PML (JC virus reactivation). Check JC antibody status.
Step 1 HY — vaccines + TRM
  • Oral live vaccines (Sabin polio, rotavirus, oral typhoid) → mucosal secretory IgA; injected Salk (IPV) → mainly serum IgG (blocks viremia, not gut carriage).
  • Tissue-resident memory T cells (CD69⁺, often CD103⁺) don't recirculate; persist in gut, lung and skin → rapid recall.
Step 1 trap

Don't swap the integrins: α4β7 → MAdCAM-1 (vessel), αEβ7 → E-cadherin (epithelium). And CCR9–CCL25 = small intestine, CCR10–CCL28 = large intestine / other mucosae.

Memory tool

"α4β7 = gut's door-key" (4 + 7 open the gut vessel). αE = E-cadherin = Embedded in Epithelium. Retinoic acid = "Return Address" stamped on gut-primed cells.

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1A vaccine is being designed against an intestinal pathogen. Oral or intramuscular, and why?
Oral. Only cells primed in GALT get retinoic-acid imprinting (α4β7, CCR9) and become gut-homing effectors / IgA plasma cells; an injected vaccine primes in peripheral nodes.
A2A monoclonal antibody blocks α4β7. What happens to effector trafficking, and why might this help IBD?
Effector T cells can't bind MAdCAM-1 → fewer enter the gut lamina propria → less mucosal inflammation, with systemic immunity largely preserved (vedolizumab).
A3A virus infects intestinal epithelial cells. Before virus-specific CD8 T cells have expanded, how are infected cells removed?
Innate-like CD8αα IELs and NK cells sense IL-15 and MIC-A/B via NKG2D and kill with perforin/granzyme, no specific antigen needed. Later, CD8αβ IELs recognize viral peptide on MHC I.
A4An intranasal vaccine generates protective IgA in the gut and genital tract. What explains this?
Shared mucosal homing (common mucosal immune system): cells primed at one mucosal inductive site seed many mucosal effector sites.
A5A breastfed infant is protected from the enteric bacteria colonizing the mother's gut. Name the circuit and the antibody.
Enteromammary circuit → secretory IgA in breast milk, made by plasma cells that were primed in the mother's Peyer's patches and homed to the mammary gland.

LO 4Secretory IgAHow it is made, moved across the epithelium and used

By the end: you can explain IgA class switching (TGF-β, eosinophils), compare serum vs secretory IgA, draw pIgR transcytosis step by step, explain why transport is one-way, and list the four ways SIgA protects without inflammation.
Q1. What makes a B cell switch to IgA, and why do eosinophils matter?
  • TGF-β induces B-cell class switching to IgA in germinal centers of gut lymphoid tissue (Peyer's patches, isolated follicles).
    • Helpers: Tfh cells (IL-21, IL-4), retinoic acid and APRIL also favor IgA.
    • IgA plasma cells then settle in the lamina propria.
  • TGF-β is stored latent (bound to a latency peptide / ECM). Eosinophils release matrix metalloproteases (MMPs) → active TGF-β → IgA class switching (and CD103⁺ FoxP3⁺ Treg induction). Eosinophils also secrete APRIL and IL-6.
  • Eosinophils are essential for IgA production and mucosal homeostasis: eosinophil-deficient mice have ↓ IgA, B cells switch to IgG1 instead, and ↓ Tregs.

Q2. How is secretory IgA built, and how does it differ from serum IgA?
FeatureSerum IgASecretory IgA (SIgA)
FormMonomerPolymer: mainly dimer + J chain + secretory component

  • IgA2 has a 13-amino-acid hinge deletion → shorter hinge → more resistant to bacterial proteases than IgA1 → that's why secretions are enriched in IgA2.
  • J chain joins the two monomers; it is what pIgR recognizes (with the Fc).
  • Secretory component (SC) = the cleaved extracellular piece of the polymeric Ig receptor, wrapped around the dimer. It:
    • protects IgA from proteolysis by luminal proteases, and
    • anchors SIgA to mucus.

CheckA bacterial protease cleaves the IgA hinge. Which subclass in secretions survives best?
IgA2: its shorter hinge (13-aa deletion) lacks the protease-sensitive site. Secretory component adds further protection.
Step 1 HY — IgA protease

IgA protease (cleaves IgA1) is a virulence factor of S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae type b and Neisseria (gonorrhoeae, meningitidis) → "SHiN"; these organisms colonize mucosa.

Step 1 trap
  • "IgA is a dimer" is true in secretions; serum IgA is mostly monomeric IgA1.
  • Class switching happens in B cells (germinal centers), not plasma cells. An IgA1-switched B cell can still switch to downstream genes (e.g., IgA2) because IgA2 is the most 3′ constant gene.
Q3. How does dimeric IgA get from a plasma cell into the gut lumen?
  • Plasma cells in the lamina propria secrete dimeric IgA + J chain.
  • Dimer binds the polymeric Ig receptor (pIgR) on the basolateral epithelial surface → endocytosis → transcytosis to the apical face.
  • At the apical surface pIgR is cleaved → IgA is released into the lumen still carrying the secretory component → binds the mucus layer.
  • Unidirectional: cleavage of SC means released IgA can't rebind apical pIgR.
    • Exception (uncommon): IgA is often glycosylated, so it can be taken back up via lectin receptors (e.g., Dectin-1 on M cells) → IgA–antigen complexes delivered to tolerogenic DCs.
  • pIgR also transports pentameric IgM (also J-chain-containing).
CheckA mouse lacks pIgR but has normal IgA plasma cells. Where does its IgA end up?
IgA is still made but can't be transcytosed → little SIgA in secretions; IgA accumulates in the lamina propria / serum, and luminal neutralization and toxin export are lost.

Step 1 HY — selective IgA deficiency
  • Most common primary immunodeficiency. Usually asymptomatic; may cause sinopulmonary and GI infections (Giardia), autoimmunity, atopy.
  • Anaphylaxis to IgA-containing blood products (anti-IgA antibodies) → use washed / IgA-deficient products.
  • False-negative IgA anti-tTG in celiac screening → always check total IgA; use IgG-based tests if low. Associated with celiac disease.
Step 1 trap

IgA does not fix complement and is not a good opsonin. If a stem asks which antibody "prevents attachment of bacteria and viruses to mucous membranes," it's IgA; "fixes complement" → IgM / IgG.

Memory tool

pIgR = "the Ferry." It picks IgA up at the back dock (basolateral), sails across, and is cut loose at the front dock; the passenger keeps the boarding pass (secretory component), and the ferry never sails back.

LO 5How mucosal antigen is recognizedM cells, the other sampling routes, and the DC's tolerance-or-immunity decision

By the end: you can describe the M cell and its pocket, list the six main routes (plus one rare route) by which luminal antigen reaches DCs, name the routes microbes exploit, and contrast quiescent vs licensed CD103⁺ DCs (the basis of oral tolerance).
Q1. What is an M cell, and how does it feed antigen to a Peyer's patch?
  • M (microfold) cells = specialized epithelial cells of the follicle-associated epithelium over Peyer's patches.
    • Lack microvilli and a thick mucus layer → microbes can reach them.
    • Non-specifically endocytose luminal molecules / microbes and release them into a pocket on their basolateral side, filled with DCs, macrophages and lymphocytes.
    • DCs load antigen on MHC II → prime T cells in the Peyer's patch and mesenteric LN → T cells help B cells make IgA.
  • Little inflammatory cytokine is made: the outcome is mostly a Treg response.
  • Pathogens exploit M cells to cross the epithelium: Salmonella (including S. typhi), Shigella, Yersinia; for Shigella, M-cell invasion is key to pathogenicity.
CheckWhy can microbes reach M cells more easily than neighboring enterocytes?
M cells lack microvilli and the thick mucus / glycocalyx that covers enterocytes, and they actively take up particles.
Step 1 HY
  • Salmonella typhi → invades M cells over Peyer's patches → ileal ulceration / perforation; rose spots, relative bradycardia.
  • Yersinia enterocolitica → M cells → mesenteric adenitis (pseudoappendicitis).
  • Shigella → M cells → basolateral invasion of colonocytes, cell-to-cell spread (actin rockets).

LO 6Commensals in health and diseaseWhat the microbiota does for us, and what happens when the barrier or the brakes fail

By the end: you can explain the protective roles of commensals, list their remote effects, match each inappropriate mucosal response to its mechanism, trace celiac disease from gliadin to villous blunting, and explain how NOD2 loss and Treg loss drive IBD (including the T-cell transfer colitis experiment).
Q1. Why are commensal bacteria good for us, and how far do their effects reach?
  • Local roles (recap from LO 2): colonization resistance (compete for nutrients and space), own antimicrobials, prime MALT, and shape T cells (SFB → Th17; Clostridium / B. fragilis butyrate → Tregs).
  • Mucosae host highly diverse microbiomes:
    • Colon = most diverse, dominated by Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes.
    • Skin = dominated by Actinobacteria (plus Proteobacteria, Firmicutes).
    • Vagina = low diversity (mainly Firmicutes / lactobacilli).
  • Remote (systemic) effects of the gut microbiota: ↓ IgE / Th2 responses, ↑ Tregs, bone remodeling, carbohydrate / lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, myelopoiesis, HPA axis.
  • Diseases it modulates: arthritis, EAE (MS model), IBD, atopy / asthma, metabolic and cardiovascular disease, type 1 diabetes (reduced by microbiota).
CheckWhich two phyla dominate the colon, and which dominates the skin?
Colon: Firmicutes + Bacteroidetes. Skin: Actinobacteria.
Q2. What are the common inappropriate mucosal responses, and what do they share?

Common thread: they all involve epithelial barrier breakdown.

Food allergy Type 2

Th2 → IgE against food antigens (e.g., peanut) → diarrhea or anaphylaxis.

Atopy / hay fever Type 2

Excess type 2 response to pollen, cat dander, dust mites: epithelium → ILC2 → IL-4 → Th2. Can lead to asthma.

Asthma Type 2

Chronic type 2 lung inflammation; IL-5 → eosinophils → fibrosis and loss of airway elasticity.

Celiac disease IEL killing

Small-intestinal inflammation triggered by gluten (gliadin); anti-tissue transglutaminase autoantibodies; IELs kill enterocytes → villous blunting.

IBD (Crohn, UC) vs microbiota

Inflammation of the colon (± ileum) from a dysregulated response to the microbiome.

CheckWhat single feature do food allergy, asthma, celiac disease and IBD share?
Epithelial barrier breakdown.
Step 1 trap

Wheat allergy ≠ celiac disease. Wheat allergy = IgE / mast-cell degranulation (type I). Celiac = T-cell/IEL-mediated (with anti-tTG antibodies); anti-gluten IgE is not a feature.

  • Anti-tTG autoantibodies are diagnostic, but not pathophysiologic.
  • Mast-cell degranulation from anti-gluten IgE is not typical of celiac disease.

Q4. What drives inflammatory bowel disease, and what does the transfer-colitis experiment prove?
  • IBD (Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis) = a dysregulated immune response against the gut microbiome, on a background of strong genetic susceptibility plus environment (diet, hygiene, antibiotics, NSAIDs, smoking, stress, enteropathogens).
    • Crohn-linked genes: NOD2, ATG16L1, XBP1 (bacterial sensing, autophagy, ER stress). UC-linked genes include IL-10; IL23R and others are shared.
  • Two routes to disease:
    • NOD2 loss of function → no autophagosomes in epithelial cells → bacteria keep getting carried across but not killed → chronic immune response and inflammation.
    • Loss of Tregs → nothing to tamp down responses to microbiome antigens → chronic inflammation.

1A 9-year-old boy has diarrhea and vomiting whenever he eats pizza. Serum IgA anti-tissue transglutaminase is positive, and duodenal biopsy shows villous blunting with increased intraepithelial lymphocytes. Which mechanism most directly causes the villous damage?

C. Gliadin → IL-15 → MIC-A on enterocytes → NKG2D⁺ IELs (CD8) and NK cells kill them → villous blunting. Anti-tTG (A) is diagnostic, not pathogenic. IgE/mast cells (B) = wheat allergy. Neutrophils (D) and eosinophils (E) are not the effector here.

2Mice lacking the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor are examined. Lamina propria plasma cells are abundant and produce dimeric IgA normally. Which finding is expected?

A. pIgR carries dimeric IgA across the epithelium and supplies secretory component; without it, IgA is made but stays on the tissue side. J chain (B) comes from the plasma cell. Switching (C) depends on TGF-β. SC (D) would be absent, not increased. Placental IgG transfer (E) uses FcRn.

3A 28-year-old woman with moderate ulcerative colitis starts a monoclonal antibody chosen because it selectively reduces lymphocyte entry into the gut without broadly suppressing CNS immune surveillance. Which interaction does it block?

E. Vedolizumab blocks α4β7–MAdCAM-1 on gut endothelium (gut-selective). α4β1–VCAM-1 (C) is also blocked by natalizumab (anti-α4), which carries PML risk. αEβ7 (A) retains cells in epithelium. CCR7 (B) and LFA-1 (D) are general homing/adhesion pathways.

4A public-health team compares an oral live attenuated vaccine with an injected inactivated vaccine against an enteric virus. The oral vaccine better prevents intestinal infection and shedding. Which mechanism best explains this?

B. Only cells first meeting antigen in GALT get gut-homing receptors (retinoic acid → α4β7, CCR9) and become secretory-IgA plasma cells (Sabin vs Salk). Injected vaccines do make memory (A). pIgR moves IgA/IgM, not IgG (D). Oral responses still need T help (E); C is false.

5A 24-year-old traveler develops sustained fever, abdominal pain and relative bradycardia. Blood cultures grow a gram-negative rod. The organism first crossed the intestinal epithelium through specialized cells overlying ileal lymphoid follicles. These cells are characterized by:

6Resident macrophages isolated from normal human jejunal lamina propria are stimulated with LPS. Compared with blood monocytes, which profile is expected?

C. Tuft cells sense succinate and release IL-25; with IL-33/TSLP from damaged enterocytes, ILC2s make IL-13 (mucus, contraction), IL-5 (eosinophils) and IL-4 (IgE). The other options pair cells with the wrong cytokines/responses (IL-6 + TGF-β gives Th17, not Treg).

8A bacterium colonizing the nasopharynx secretes a protease that cleaves the hinge region of IgA. Analysis of the patient's saliva shows that one IgA form remains largely intact. Which feature explains its resistance?

E. IgA2's shorter hinge lacks the IgA1-protease site (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, Neisseria). Secretory IgA is polymeric with J chain (A, B wrong); serum is ~95% IgA1 (C); IgA does not fix complement (D).

9Mice genetically lacking eosinophils are studied. Compared with wild-type mice, their Peyer's patches show fewer IgA⁺ B cells, more IgG1⁺ B cells and fewer CD103⁺ FoxP3⁺ Tregs. What eosinophil function best explains this?

B. Eosinophil MMPs (plus APRIL, IL-6) activate latent TGF-β, which drives IgA switching and Treg induction. The other options are not eosinophil roles in this setting.

10A 19-year-old with ileal Crohn disease carries a loss-of-function variant in a cytosolic bacterial sensor. Which mechanism best links this variant to his disease?
