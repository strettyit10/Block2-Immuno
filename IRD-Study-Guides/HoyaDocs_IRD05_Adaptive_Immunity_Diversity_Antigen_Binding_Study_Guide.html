Adaptive Immunity: Diversity & Antigen Binding

Learning objectives

  1. LO 1Walk through how B and T cells build millions of different antigen receptors from a small set of genes (segment shuffling, sloppy joints, random chain pairing and, in B cells only, hypermutation) and predict what happens when that machinery breaks.
  2. LO 2Explain how developing B cells (bone marrow) and T cells (thymus) are tested, and how cells whose receptors bind self too strongly are removed, edited or silenced.
  3. LO 3Lay out how MHC class I and class II are built, which cells carry them, where their peptides come from and how they get loaded, and why MHC is so diverse.
  4. LO 4Contrast the way antibodies and T-cell receptors "see" antigen: number of binding sites, type of epitope, need for MHC, chemistry of the target, and the TCR signaling partners.
  5. LO 5Describe how natural and engineered antigen receptors are turned into treatments: polyclonal vs monoclonal antibodies, therapeutic cancer and mRNA vaccines, TIL therapy and CAR-T cells.

LO 1Generating receptor diversityHow a few hundred gene segments become millions of receptors

By the end: you can list the four sources of diversity, draw V(D)J recombination step by step with every enzyme in the right order, state the 12/23 rule, explain CDRs, and read a SCID lymphocyte profile.
Q1. Why does adaptive immunity need so many different receptors, and what is the "diversity paradox"?
  • Timing: after a first viral infection, innate defenses hold the line for the first few days:
    • Days 1–3: macrophages release TNF-α, IL-12, IFN-α → NK cells kill infected cells; IFN-α puts neighboring cells on antiviral alert.
    • Days ~4–9+: T-cell killing and antibody rise and actually clear the virus. The first adaptive response takes days–weeks; a second exposure is much faster (memory).
  • Division of labor: conventional dendritic cells (APCs) present antigen to T cells → activated CD4⁺ T helper cells signal B cells to make antibody → activated CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells kill virally infected cells.
  • This only works if there is a huge pool of T and B cells, each recognizing a different antigen.
  • The paradox: the genome has only ~22,000 genes, yet we make ~25 million unique TCRs and even more unique antibodies.
    • Solution = combinatorial immunity: unique DNA recombination reactions in developing B and T cells assemble each receptor gene from interchangeable pieces.
    • Found only in jawed vertebrates, and only in lymphocytes.

Antibody (BCR)

  • Tetramer: 2 identical heavy + 2 identical light chains (κ or λ), held by disulfide bonds
  • Each chain: one variable (V) domain + constant (C) domain(s)
  • Binding site = VH + VL

T-cell receptor (TCR)

  • Dimer of two different chains: α + β (most common) or γ + δ
  • Each chain: one V + one C domain; always membrane-bound
  • Binding site = Vα + Vβ
CheckWhich part of an antibody or TCR is diverse, and which part is shared between clones?
The variable regions are diverse and bind antigen; the constant regions are shared and handle structure/effector or signaling functions.

CheckWhich of the four mechanisms needs antigen to happen first?
Only somatic hypermutation. It happens in activated B cells in germinal centers. The other three happen during development, before antigen is ever seen.
Q3. How does V(D)J recombination actually cut and paste DNA?
  • It is a DNA recombination reaction, not RNA splicing → the intervening DNA is cut out, so the change to that cell's genome is permanent and inherited by every daughter cell of the clone.
  • Recombination signal sequences (RSSs) flank every V, D and J segment and direct the recombinase:
    • Structure: heptamer (7 bp) – spacer – nonamer (9 bp); heptamer sits next to the coding segment.
    • Spacer = 12 bp or 23 bp (≈ one or two turns of the DNA helix).
    • 12/23 rule: recombination only joins a segment flanked by a 12-spacer RSS with one flanked by a 23-spacer RSS.
    • In the heavy chain, VH and JH both carry 23-RSSs and D carries 12-RSSs on both sides → V cannot join J directly; D must be included.
  • Two stages: cleavage (RAG1/2) then joining (NHEJ DNA-repair proteins).
StepEnzyme(s)Event

  • Why imprecise joining matters: the V–(D)–J junctions encode CDR3 → CDR3 is the most variable loop.
    • Cost: about two-thirds of rearrangements end up out of frame (nonproductive) → the cell must try again (other allele/locus) or die. Out-of-frame joins are wasted, not a source of diversity.
CheckA VH segment (23-RSS) and a JH segment (23-RSS) are brought together. Can they recombine directly?
No. The 12/23 rule needs one 12-spacer and one 23-spacer RSS. D (flanked by 12-RSSs) must join J first; then V joins DJ.
CheckP nucleotides vs N nucleotides: which enzyme makes each?
P (palindromic) = from Artemis opening the hairpin off-center. N (non-templated) = random bases added by TdT. Neither is encoded in the germline.
Step 1 HY
  • TdT is a nuclear marker of immature lymphoid cells → TdT⁺ = lymphoblasts (ALL / lymphoblastic lymphoma), not mature lymphoma.
  • Boards usually describe V(D)J simply as "RAG1/2-mediated recombination generating BCR/TCR diversity" and test the SCID consequence, not the enzyme order.
Step 1 trap

V(D)J recombination is DNA rearrangement (permanent). RNA splicing only attaches VDJ to the C region and lets mature B cells co-express IgM + IgD. Class switching is a different DNA event (AID-driven, after activation).

Memory tool

"RAG cuts, Ku catches, Artemis opens, TdT Tosses in bases, Ligase locks."
"Twelve pairs with twenty-three" (one-turn + two-turn) → D is the bridge in the heavy chain.
RSS = 7–(12/23)–9: "Heptamer hugs the gene."

Q4. How does the tip of a receptor actually grab antigen? (CDRs and the paratope)
  • Hypervariability is localized: sequence variation clusters in 3 hypervariable loops per V domain = the complementarity-determining regions (CDR1, CDR2, CDR3).
    • Each binding site has 6 CDR loops: 3 from heavy + 3 from light (or 3 α + 3 β).
    • The CDR loops are the turns at the ends of the β-strands of the Ig fold; the less variable framework regions form the β-sheet scaffold that holds them.
    • Together, the 6 CDRs at the tip form the paratope (the receptor's binding site). The bit of antigen it touches = the epitope.
  • How binding works: many weak, non-covalent interactions (H-bonds, electrostatic, van der Waals, hydrophobic) that add up to high affinity.
    • Non-covalent → binding is reversible and changes neither receptor nor antigen.
    • CDR shapes vary (pocket, groove, extended or flat surface) → many different antigens can be recognized.
CheckHow many CDR loops form one antigen-binding site, and which is the most variable?
Six (3 per chain). CDR3 is most variable because it spans the V–(D)–J junctions created by imprecise joining.

CheckWhy are NK cells spared in RAG deficiency but absent in X-linked SCID?
NK cells don't rearrange their receptors (RAG not needed), but they need IL-15 signaling through the common γ chain.
Step 1 HY
  • All SCID: ↓ TRECs, absent thymic shadow, thrush, chronic diarrhea, failure to thrive, Pneumocystis; avoid live vaccines; cure = HSC transplant.
  • Boards phrase X-linked SCID as "IL-2R γ chain" and RAG SCID as "defective V(D)J recombination."
  • JAK3 deficiency (signals downstream of γc) gives the same T⁻B⁺NK⁻ picture but is AR.
Step 1 trap

Radiosensitivity separates look-alikes: RAG and Artemis both give T⁻B⁻NK⁺ SCID, but only the NHEJ defect (Artemis) makes cells sensitive to ionizing radiation. And B⁺ in X-linked SCID does not mean normal antibodies (no T help).

Memory tool

"RAG spares the NK" (NK = No Knitting of DNA needed). "Gamma-chain takes the NK and the T", and it's the boy (X-linked).

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1Mice lacking TdT still make B and T cells. How are their receptors different?
Junctions lack N nucleotides → shorter, less diverse CDR3 → smaller repertoire. Segment choice, pairing and P nucleotides (from Artemis) still occur.
A2An infant with SCID has no T or B cells but normal NK cells; her fibroblasts are unusually sensitive to ionizing radiation. RAG1 or Artemis?
Artemis. NHEJ is needed both for coding-joint formation and for repairing radiation-induced DSBs. RAG deficiency gives the same profile without radiosensitivity.
A3An 18-year-old has lifelong low (not absent) T and B cells, normal NK cells and agammaglobulinemia, plus autoimmune hemolytic anemia and autoimmune pancreatitis. A hypomorphic RAG1 mutation is found. Why the autoimmunity?
A small, oligoclonal lymphocyte pool with poor central tolerance and too few Tregs → self-reactive cells escape control.
A4A newborn screen reports undetectable TRECs. What does this mean, and what is the next test?
Very little thymic T-cell output → suspect SCID. TRECs are excision circles from TCR V(D)J recombination. Next: flow cytometry for T, B and NK counts and naive (CD45RA⁺) T cells.

LO 2Selection and toleranceHow self-reactive receptors are weeded out

By the end: you can define positive vs negative selection, walk a B cell and a T cell through their stages (which chain rearranges when, where each selection happens), and explain what happens to a T cell that sees antigen without a second signal.
Q1. What do "positive" and "negative" selection mean?

Positive selection

The antigen receptor must signal for the cell to survive.

Proves the receptor is built correctly / useful.

Negative selection

Signaling through the receptor (strong self-binding) leads to death (or further rearrangement).

Removes dangerous, self-reactive cells → central tolerance.

  • Both B and T cells undergo both, in the primary lymphoid organs: bone marrow (B) and thymus (T).
CheckThe same event (receptor signaling) means "live" at one stage and "die" at another. How?
Early (pre-B, DP thymocyte), signaling = proof the receptor works → survive. Later (immature B), or when binding is too strong (DP thymocyte), signaling = reacts to self → die/edit.

Step 1 HY
  • AIRE in medullary thymic epithelium expresses tissue-restricted self-antigens for negative selection → AIRE deficiency = APS-1 (chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis, hypoparathyroidism, adrenal insufficiency).

Step 1 trap

Positive selection is about self-MHC recognition (restriction), not about foreign antigen. And CD4 lineage follows MHC II, CD8 follows MHC I. Don't flip them.

Memory tool

Goldilocks thymus: too cold (no binding) → neglect; just right → live; too hot → die. "MHC × CD = 8": MHC II × CD4 = 8, MHC I × CD8 = 8.

LO 3MHC class I and class IIStructure, distribution, peptide loading, diversity

By the end: you can fill in the full MHC I vs II comparison table, trace both loading pathways (TAP vs invariant chain/CLIP/HLA-DM), explain cross-presentation, and define polymorphism vs polygeny.
Q1. What is MHC actually for?
  • MHC (in humans, HLA) was discovered as the main barrier to organ transplantation: we react to non-self MHC much like non-self blood groups.
  • But rejecting grafts is not its job. Its physiological function: present peptides on the cell surface in a peptide-binding cleft so αβ T cells can see them.
    • αβ TCRs can bind their peptide only when it is presented by MHC.

Step 1 HY

  • RBCs lack MHC I → not killed by CD8⁺ T cells; a classic distractor.
Step 1 trap

Follicular dendritic cells are not APCs for T cells: they hold intact antigen (immune complexes) on their surface for B cells and do not present on MHC II. Conventional DCs are the T-cell primers.

Memory tool

"MHC I = Inside, all cells, I go to 8 (CD8)." "MHC II = outside, professionals Only, 2×2 = 4 (CD4)." Class I loci: ABC; class II: D-everything (DP, DQ, DR).

Q3. How are peptides loaded onto each class?
  • Both classes are made in the ER. Class I is loaded in the ER; class II leaves the ER with its groove blocked and is loaded in endosomes.

Class I loading molecules

  • Calnexin: holds the partly folded α chain until β2m binds
  • (Immuno)proteasome: degrades ubiquitinated cytosolic proteins and DRiPs (defective ribosomal products)
  • TAP: transports peptides cytosol → ER
  • Tapasin (+ calreticulin, ERp57): peptide-loading complex; tethers class I to TAP
  • ERAAP: trims peptides to fit
  • Golgi → surface

Class II loading molecules

  • Invariant chain (Ii): binds class II in the ER, blocks the groove (so it can't grab abundant ER self-peptides) and targets it to endosomes
  • Endosomal proteases (acidic): cleave Ii and digest endocytosed antigen
  • CLIP: short Ii fragment left sitting in the groove
  • HLA-DM: removes CLIP, lets antigenic peptide bind (peptide editing)
  • Plasma membrane: displays peptide–MHC II to CD4⁺ T cells
  • Cross-presentation: dendritic cells can present exogenous (endocytosed) proteins on MHC I → activate CD8⁺ T cells against viruses (and tumors) that do not infect DCs. (The reverse route also exists: autophagy delivers cytosolic proteins to endosomes for class II.)
  • Cytokines raise MHC: type I IFN (α/β) → ↑ MHC I on infected and neighboring cells; IFN-γ → ↑ MHC II on APCs (and ↑ class I).
CheckWhat is wrong with this statement: "Both MHC I and MHC II wait in vesicles bound to CLIP until peptides are loaded"?
Only class II carries the invariant chain/CLIP. Class I is held by calnexin, then the tapasin–TAP loading complex in the ER, and is loaded there with cytosolic peptides.
Step 1 HY
  • TAP deficiency = bare lymphocyte syndrome type I → ↓ MHC I → ↓ CD8⁺ T cells. Many viruses (HSV, CMV) block TAP or MHC I to dodge CD8⁺ cells (then NK cells step in).
  • MHC II deficiency (CIITA etc.) = bare lymphocyte syndrome type II → ↓ CD4⁺, ↓ Ig, SCID-like infections.
Step 1 trap

"Exogenous antigen on MHC I" is not a mistake if the cell is a dendritic cell: that's cross-presentation. Also, MHC I presents normal self peptides all the time; CD8⁺ cells usually ignore them (tolerance).

Buzzwords

Q4. Why is MHC so diverse, and what does that cost us?

Polymorphism

Many alleles of each MHC gene in the population. MHC genes are the most polymorphic genes known in humans.

Polygeny

Multiple MHC loci in each person's genome: HLA-A, -B, -C (class I); -DP, -DQ, -DR (class II).

  • Benefit: each person can bind and present a wide variety of peptides (their essential job). Alleles are codominantly expressed (maternal + paternal).
  • Cost: makes tissue/organ matching difficult.
  • Where's the variation? Polymorphic residues cluster in the peptide-binding groove → alleles differ in which peptides they bind.
    • Class I: HLA-B has the most alleles, then A, then C.
    • Class II: DRβ is the most polymorphic chain; DRα is essentially monomorphic.
  • Count it: a heterozygous person shows 6 different class I molecules per nucleated cell (A, B, C × 2 parents); usually 6–8 class II (extra DRβ genes and mixed α/β pairings).
  • TCR docking: α- and β-chain CDR1 and CDR2 contact the MHC α-helices; the hypervariable CDR3 loops sit over the peptide. Thymic positive selection checks that the recombined TCR can engage self-MHC this way. Each T cell carries ~30,000 identical TCRs.
CheckPolymorphism or polygeny: "each of us has HLA-A, -B and -C genes"?
Polygeny (several loci in one genome). "Hundreds of HLA-B alleles exist in humans" = polymorphism.
Step 1 HY

MHC genes sit on chromosome 6. Boards love HLA disease links: B27 (ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis), DR3/DR4 (type 1 DM), DR4 (RA), DQ2/DQ8 (celiac), B57:01 (abacavir hypersensitivity).

Memory tool

Poly-MORPH = many forms in the population. Poly-GENE = many genes in you.

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1A virus infects only hepatocytes, never DCs, yet strong virus-specific CD8⁺ T-cell responses arise in lymph nodes. Explain.
Cross-presentation: DCs take up viral antigen (e.g., dying infected cells) and present it on MHC I to naive CD8⁺ T cells.
A2An APC lacks functional HLA-DM. What sits in its surface class II grooves, and what happens to CD4⁺ responses?
Mostly CLIP → few antigenic peptides presented → weak CD4⁺ responses to exogenous antigen.
A3A tumor cell line has a TAP1 mutation. Which T cells fail to recognize its mutated cytosolic proteins, and which innate cell might still kill it?
CD8⁺ T cells fail (no peptides reach ER → little surface MHC I). NK cells can kill it (missing self).
A4Why does a B cell bother to put antigen-derived peptide on MHC II?
To get help: a CD4⁺ T cell recognizing that peptide–MHC II gives CD40L + cytokines → class switching and affinity maturation.

LO 4Antibody vs TCR recognitionSame fold, very different rules

By the end: you can list the four differences between antibody and αβ TCR binding, tell linear from conformational epitopes, define epitope/paratope/hapten, and name what the TCR uses to signal and to stabilize binding.
Q1. What are an epitope and a paratope?
  • Epitope = the part of the antigen that the receptor binds.
  • Paratope = the receptor's antigen-binding site, formed by the 6 CDR loops of the two variable domains; the conserved framework is the scaffold.
Step 1 HY

Hapten: small chemical that can bind preformed antibody but cannot trigger a response alone; immunogenic only when coupled to a carrier protein (supplies peptides for T-cell help). Example: penicillin on RBC proteins → drug-induced immune hemolytic anemia (type II hypersensitivity).

B cells have Broad taste: any shape, any molecule, Both hands (bivalent). T cells are Tidy eaters: only Trimmed peptide served on an MHC Tray.

Q3. Linear vs conformational epitopes: why can T cells only see linear ones?

Linear epitope

  • Contiguous amino-acid sequence
  • Survives denaturation (still bound on a Western blot)
  • Seen by antibodies and TCRs

Conformational epitope

  • Formed by 3-D folding (discontinuous residues)
  • Lost on denaturation
  • Seen by antibodies only
  • T cells: antigen must be processed (unfolded, cut into short peptides) to fit an MHC groove → folding-dependent and non-protein epitopes are lost. T-cell epitopes are often buried inside the native protein.
CheckA monoclonal antibody binds a viral protein in solution but not after the protein is boiled in SDS. Epitope type?
Conformational: destroyed by denaturation.
Q4. If α and β only recognize, what makes the TCR signal, and what holds it on?
  • The αβ chains have no inherent signaling capacity.
  • Signaling partners: CD3 complex (γ, δ, ε: εγ and εδ pairs) + the ζ (zeta) chain homodimer.
    • All chains other than α and β carry cytoplasmic ITAMs (immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs) that are phosphorylated on antigen binding and start signaling.
    • CD3 chains are transmembrane with extracellular Ig-like domains; ζ has a tiny extracellular part and a long tail with 3 ITAMs each. Charged transmembrane residues hold the complex together.
  • Co-receptors stabilize the TCR–peptide–MHC complex (required for stable interaction):
    • CD4 (helper T) → MHC class II (β2 domain)
    • CD8 (cytotoxic T; αβ dimer) → MHC class I (α3 domain)
CheckRecognition vs signaling: which TCR-complex chains do each job?
α + β = recognition; CD3 (γδε) + ζ = signaling via ITAMs; CD4/CD8 = stabilization.
Step 1 HY
  • CD3 is the pan-T-cell marker on flow cytometry. The B-cell equivalent signaling chains are Igα/Igβ (CD79a/b).
  • Superantigens (TSST-1, SEB) bypass the peptide groove: they cross-link the TCR Vβ region to MHC II outside the groove → massive polyclonal T activation → cytokine storm.

ApplyLO 4 application questions

A1Penicillin is too small to provoke antibodies alone, but a patient makes anti-penicillin antibodies after it binds his RBC proteins. What is penicillin acting as?
A hapten, immunogenic only when coupled to a carrier protein (the carrier supplies T-cell help peptides).
A2A T-cell clone recognizes a peptide from a viral protein. If you add the intact, folded viral protein to the T cells with no APCs, will they respond?
No. TCRs need the antigen processed into peptide and presented on MHC; they cannot bind free, folded protein.
A3A mutation removes the ITAM tyrosines from the ζ chain and CD3. The TCR still binds peptide–MHC. What happens?
Binding without signaling → no T-cell activation. α/β recognize but cannot signal on their own.

Q2. How do therapeutic cancer vaccines and mRNA vaccines work?

Cell-based

Whole tumor cells, or dendritic cells loaded with tumor antigens.

Viral-based

Engineered viral vectors deliver tumor antigens to the immune system.

Peptide-based

Tumor epitopes given as short or long peptides.

Nucleic acid-based

DNA or mRNA encoding tumor antigens, on various delivery platforms.

  • Goal: train the immune system to recognize tumor antigens and fight the tumor and its metastases, much like an anti-pathogen vaccine.
  • mRNA vaccines (best known from COVID-19): deliver mRNA that the recipient's own cells translate into the antigen.
    • No large-scale production/purification of proteins or inactivated pathogens → rapid custom vaccines.

CAR-T therapy

  • T cells collected from blood → a chimeric antigen receptor (think: engineered T-cell receptor) introduced in the lab → reinfused.
  • CAR = antibody-like binding domain + T-cell signaling domains → "like a BCR on a T cell": binds intact surface antigen directly, so no MHC needed.
  • Example: anti-CD19 CAR-T for B-cell malignancies (CD19 is on B cells at nearly all stages). CARs are being developed for many tumors, each against something specific to that tumor.

CheckWhich T-cell therapy does not require you to know the tumor antigen?
TIL therapy: the harvested T cells already target tumor antigens naturally (though many will be irrelevant).
Step 1 HY

Step 1 trap

A tumor that loses MHC I (e.g., β2-microglobulin loss) escapes CD8⁺ T cells, TIL and TCR-engineered cells, but not CAR-T (MHC-independent) and not NK cells.

Polyclonal antiserum → passive immunity (immediate, temporary, no memory); the vaccine supplies active immunity.

B. TdT adds N nucleotides at junctions → junctional (CDR3) diversity. A and E are combinatorial steps independent of TdT. C: SHM depends on AID. D: hairpins are made by RAG1/2 cleavage.

4Investigators engineer the immunoglobulin heavy-chain locus so that every D segment is flanked on both sides by RSSs with 23-bp spacers (V and J still carry 23-bp-spacer RSSs). Light-chain loci are normal. What is the most likely effect on B-cell development?

C. The 12/23 rule requires one 12-spacer and one 23-spacer RSS. With all 23s, D can't join J and V can't join J → no μ chain → no pre-BCR → no positive selection. B is the tempting wrong answer: V–J joining is also 23/23 and forbidden. A, D, E require a functional heavy chain.

5In the thymic cortex, a CD4⁺CD8⁺ thymocyte expressing a newly rearranged αβ TCR binds self-peptide presented on MHC class I with moderate affinity and shows no binding to MHC class II. What is the most likely fate of this cell?

E. Moderate binding = positive selection; MHC I engagement → CD8 lineage. A: neglect is for no binding. B: negative selection is for very strong binding. C: β rearranges earlier (double-negative). D: CD4 follows MHC II.

6A 2-year-old has recurrent sinopulmonary infections and chronic diarrhea. Labs: low IgG and IgA, CD8⁺ T cells normal, CD4⁺ T cells markedly reduced. Dendritic cells and B cells fail to express HLA-DR, -DQ and -DP. Which process best explains the low CD4⁺ count?

C. Cross-presentation: DCs route endocytosed (exogenous) antigen onto MHC I, priming CD8⁺ T cells against viruses that don't infect DCs. A: hepatocytes don't migrate. B: CD8 binds MHC I, not II. D: superantigens activate T cells nonspecifically, not virus-specific CD8⁺ responses. E: FDCs display intact antigen to B cells and don't present on MHC.

8A cell line of B cells carries a loss-of-function mutation in HLA-DM. When the cells are fed a foreign protein, CD4⁺ T cells specific for that protein respond poorly. Which molecule will most MHC class II molecules on these cells' surface contain in their groove?

B. HLA-DM removes CLIP (the Ii fragment left in the groove) so endosomal peptides can bind. Without it, class II reaches the surface still holding CLIP. A and E describe the class I pathway. C is the class I light chain. D: Ii is cleaved in endosomes even without DM; calnexin is a class I/ER chaperone.

9A monoclonal antibody raised against a viral envelope protein neutralizes the virus and stains infected cells strongly by immunofluorescence. On a Western blot of the same protein after boiling in SDS with reducing agent, the antibody gives no band. Which best describes its epitope?

D. Conformational epitopes are made by 3-D folding and vanish on denaturation; native protein is bound, denatured is not. A: linear epitopes survive denaturation (still bind on a Western). B: SDS doesn't strip glycans. C: antibodies don't need MHC. E: no hapten is involved.

10A 10-month-old receives a vaccine containing purified pneumococcal capsular polysaccharide and makes almost no protective antibody. E. αβ TCRs see processed peptide on MHC, so CD4⁺ help requires a protein carrier. The B cell binds the polysaccharide and presents carrier peptides on MHC II to get help → class switch, affinity maturation, memory. A, B, C and D are all antibody/BCR properties, not TCR properties.

11A patient with metastatic melanoma initially responds to tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy, but the tumor recurs. Sequencing shows biallelic loss of B2M (β2-microglobulin) in tumor cells, which still express a well-characterized surface glycoprotein. Which therapy is most likely to recognize the recurrent tumor?

C. Without β2m, the tumor displays no MHC I, so anything relying on CD8⁺ TCR recognition of peptide–MHC I (A, B, D) fails. A CAR binds intact surface antigen directly, like an antibody, with no MHC needed. E only supports existing T cells.

12A 32-year-old veterinarian is bitten by a raccoon later confirmed to be rabid. She has never been vaccinated. Along with wound care and the first vaccine dose, she receives human rabies immune globulin. Which best describes this immune globulin?
